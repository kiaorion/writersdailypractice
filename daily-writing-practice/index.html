Daily Writing Practice: how to write every day and keep it up.

How much to write, when to do it, how to fit it around a job, and what to do on the bad mornings. Built from the real routines of Grisham, Morrison, King, Hemingway and 90 other writers.

Start this week ↓

By Kia Orion, author of The Writer's Daily Practice

Quick answer

A daily writing practice is a small, fixed amount of writing you do at the same time every day, whether or not you feel like it. The working writers who kept one for decades almost all used a modest minimum (Grisham's was a single page) and a time of day nobody else could claim. Start smaller than feels respectable, protect the hour, and count the days you show up. The quality sorts itself out in revision.

Most advice about writing every day sounds like a pep talk. I wanted something more useful, so I went back through the routines on this site, more than 90 of them, and looked for what the long-haul writers actually did. The pattern is less heroic than you'd expect. Very few of them waited for inspiration. Almost all of them had a daily number and a fixed hour, and they kept both boring on purpose.

This guide is the practical version: how much to write, when to do it, how to fit it around a job, and what to do on the mornings it won't come.

How much should you write each day?

Less than you think. The daily amounts of famous writers vary by a factor of ten, and the low end produced some of the most commercially successful careers in publishing.

WriterDaily amountWhen
John GrishamOne page, about 200 wordsAt his desk by 5:30am, before a full day practising law
John SteinbeckRoughly a pageOpened every session with a warm-up letter to his editor
Ernest HemingwayAbout 500 wordsFrom first light to about midday, then stopped
Ray BradburyAt least 1,000 wordsNearly every day of his working life
Haruki MurakamiAbout 1,600 wordsTen manuscript pages, starting around 4am
Stephen King2,000 goal, 1,000 floorAt the desk by 8:30 or 9 every morning

Grisham is the one to study if you're starting out. He wrote A Time to Kill one page at a time while working as a lawyer and serving in the Mississippi legislature. A page is about 200 words. You can write 200 words on a bad day if you sit there long enough, which is the whole point of the rule. Once a book was rolling he'd often write far more, but the page was the promise.

Stephen King's version is useful for a different reason. He has a goal of 2,000 words and a floor of 1,000. The goal gives the day a shape. The floor stops a bad day from turning into a skipped one. If you set a single number, make it the floor.

Words, pages or minutes?

Pick whichever one you can't argue with. Word counts suit drafting. Minutes suit revision, research and the days when you're stuck, because thinking about a scene for twenty minutes still counts as practice even when the counter doesn't move. A lot of writers keep one of each: twenty minutes minimum, 300 words if the draft is moving.

When should you write?

At the time that is most reliably yours. The routines split three ways, and every group includes major writers.

Before dawn

Toni Morrison wrote before dawn because she was a senior editor at Random House raising two sons, and those were the only hours nobody else owned. Octavia Butler wrote at 2 or 3am for years before catching the bus to whatever day job was paying the rent. Sylvia Plath wrote at about four in the morning, before her children woke. These weren't morning people by temperament. They picked the hours the rest of life couldn't reach.

Mid-morning

Writers with more control over their days tended to work from breakfast to lunch. Virginia Woolf wrote from about ten until lunch. Flannery O'Connor wrote from about nine to noon, every morning. Garcia Marquez started after taking his kids to school. If your mornings are free, this is the most common pattern in the archive.

Late at night

Franz Kafka worked at an insurance office all day and wrote from about eleven at night until two or three. James Baldwin wrote at night too. It works, but it's the hardest version to sustain, because every bad day lands on top of your writing time. If you choose night, keep the session short and the minimum low.

Writing every day around a full-time job

This is the situation most people are actually in, and it's the one the archive is best at answering. Grisham, Morrison, Butler and Kafka all wrote their early books around jobs that had nothing to do with writing. What they shared was a fixed slot at an edge of the day, before work or after everyone else was asleep, plus a minimum small enough to hit when they were exhausted.

If you only have 30 minutes, that is enough for a page. Grisham's stolen hour before the office filled up produced a career. The mistake is waiting for a clear Saturday to do the real work, because Saturdays fill up too.

How to start a daily writing practice this week

1

Pick your minimum

One page, 200 words, or 15 minutes. Choose something you could do sick. You can always write more. You're only promising this much.

2

Pick your slot

Write it down as a time, not a part of the day. “6:15 to 6:45” survives a busy week. “Sometime in the morning” doesn't.

3

Set up the night before

Leave the document open, or the notebook on the table with a pen on it. The fewer decisions between waking and writing, the better. Steinbeck sharpened all his pencils before he began so he never had to stop.

4

Warm up for five minutes

Steinbeck opened every session with a short letter to his editor before touching the novel. A journal entry, a reflection or a single prompt does the same job. It gets your hand moving before the hard sentences.

5

Stop while it's still going

Hemingway quit each day when he knew what came next, so the next morning started with something to write. Leave yourself a half-finished sentence and tomorrow starts itself.

6

Track the days, not the words

A calendar with an X for each day you hit the minimum is enough. The chain matters more than any single session.

7

Plan for the missed day

You will miss one. Decide now that the day after a miss is non-negotiable. One gap is normal. Two in a row is how the habit quietly ends.

What to do on the mornings it won't come

Lower the bar instead of skipping. Write the minimum and stop. Write about why the scene isn't working. Copy out a paragraph you admire by hand and look at how it's built. All of those count. The practice is the appointment, and you can keep an appointment badly.

If you're stuck for days at a time, that's a different problem. The writer's block guide covers it, and free writing is the simplest way to get words moving without judging them. For structured drills, the writing exercises page has a set for each skill.

Does writing every day actually make you better?

It makes you faster and less precious about the work, and those two things are most of what separates people who finish books from people who don't. It doesn't guarantee good sentences. What it does is give you enough pages that some of them are good, and enough reps that you can tell which ones. William Stafford, who wrote every morning for decades, said the trick was to lower his standards until the writing came, then fix it later. You can read more about that in what happens when you write every day.

If you'd rather learn from someone who wrote around a job and still shipped, start with Grisham, Morrison and Butler. All three had less time than you probably do.

Frequently asked questions

A daily writing practice is the habit of showing up to write at the same time every day, regardless of inspiration or mood. It matters because consistency builds craft faster than sporadic bursts of motivation. Writers like William Stafford, Graham Greene, and Flannery O'Connor all credited their daily routines as the foundation of their work.

Fifteen to thirty minutes is enough to start. John Grisham wrote his first novel one page a day, about 200 words, before going to work as a lawyer. Stephen King aims for 2,000 words but treats 1,000 as his floor. Pick a minimum you can hit on your worst day and let good days run long.

Missing one day rarely matters. The day after is the one to protect. Decide in advance that you never miss twice in a row, and lower the minimum that morning if you need to. One gap is normal. A run of them is how a practice quietly ends.
