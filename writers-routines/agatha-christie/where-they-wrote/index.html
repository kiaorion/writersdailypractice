Where Did Agatha Christie Write?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

The world's best-selling novelist never had a study, a writing room, or a sacred corner. Interviewers turned up expecting a desk to photograph and found there wasn't one. She wrote wherever there was a flat surface and a machine.

The Direct Answer
Almost anywhere, with no dedicated room or fixed desk.
In her Autobiography she wrote that all she needed was a steady table and a typewriter, and that she had no special place to retire to.

No Study, No Sacred Desk

Most famous writers can point to a room. Christie couldn't. In her posthumously published Autobiography, she remembered the years after her first marriage and admitted, 'I never had a definite place which was my room or where I retired specially to write.' Writing, she said, was a task she did in spells and bursts while she got on with ordinary life. There was no morning walk to a designated study, no chair that was hers and hers alone, no shelf of talismans she had to arrange before the work could begin.

This drove interviewers a little mad. When a photographer arrived wanting a portrait of the author at work, the natural question was where she wrote her books. Her answer, by her own account, was a breezy 'Oh, anywhere.' A washstand, a dining table, a hotel room, the corner of a bedroom. It made no difference to her, and she clearly found the whole premise of the question slightly absurd, as though the magic lived in the furniture rather than in her own head.

All I needed was a steady table and a typewriter.

Agatha Christie, An Autobiography

Houses, Hotels, and Archaeological Digs

Over a long life she wrote in a lot of places. At Greenway, her beloved holiday home above the River Dart in Devon, and at Winterbrook House near Wallingford, and across the Middle East on digs with her second husband, the archaeologist Max Mallowan. On those expeditions she would write at whatever table the dig house offered, between sorting pottery and cataloguing finds. She didn't tie her output to a single desk because she didn't have to. The plotting happened in her head long before she sat down, so the physical spot mattered far less than it does for writers who compose at the page. The portability had a practical edge too. A novel that lives mostly in your head and your notebooks travels well. She could pick up a thread on a train, in a rented flat, or in a tent on a Syrian dig and lose nothing by it. Where a more precious writer would have stalled without the right conditions, Christie simply kept going. The work came with her instead of waiting at home, which is part of why she could keep up such a steady stream of books across decades of travel, two marriages, and a public life she mostly tried to avoid.

What You Can Steal

  • Drop the search for the perfect room. Christie proved you can write a hundred books without one. A steady table beats a dream office you never finish setting up.
  • Let the plotting be portable. If the thinking happens away from the desk, the desk stops being a bottleneck.
  • Lower the bar for where you start. Anywhere with a flat surface counts. The fewer conditions you attach, the more often you'll actually write.
  • Separate the place from the work. Treat writing as something you do, not somewhere you go.
