How Many Words a Day Did Anne Rice Write?

Kia Orion | August 2, 2026 | 5 min read

Search for Anne Rice's daily word count and one number comes back: 3,000. It sits on dozens of writing blogs, always without a source, and it matches nothing Rice said about her own method across four decades of interviews and reader questions on her own website.

The Direct Answer
She never named one. Her unit was the twelve-hour shift.
Rice described throwing herself at a novel in twelve-hour shifts day after day until it was finished. She drafted Interview with the Vampire in five weeks and Memnoch the Devil in about four. No daily word target appears anywhere in her own account of the work.

Where the 3,000 Figure Came From

The number traces to writing-advice listicles that stack famous authors into a table of daily word counts. Rice appears somewhere in the middle, credited with 3,000 a day, and the entry carries no citation. Every later version of the claim is copying the earlier one.

Rice ran a long-standing FAQ on her own website answering reader questions about craft, and she later posted at length on Facebook about how she worked. Neither contains a words-per-day figure. Her obituaries in the New York Times, The Guardian, and on the AP wire do not carry one either. The number stuck because a table needed a value in every row.

What She Actually Described

On her own site in 1999, answering a reader, Rice described her method as choosing a stretch of time with minimal distractions and then working in twelve-hour shifts day after day. She said she lost sleep and went slightly manic, and that she could not sleep for three days once a novel was done. She called that arrangement ideal.

The Wall Street Journal profiled her working method in November 2009 and reported a chapter a day as her target, with eight or nine hours at a stretch once she was inside a book. A chapter fits how she thought better than a word count would, because her structural unit was the episode. She worked in bursts of story rather than measured increments.

The speed records back that up. Interview with the Vampire was drafted in five weeks in the mid-1970s, and Rice added a detail almost nobody repeats: she spent another ten and a half weeks revising it the following year. Memnoch the Devil, the book she said nearly killed her, took about a month of writing after two years of thinking.

Interview with the Vampire was written in five weeks; this was written in about four weeks, and it was the same sort of experience: just surrendering to the process.

Anne Rice on Memnoch the Devil, interview with Rolling Stone, 1995

How Long Her Books Actually Run

Page counts are the only hard numbers available, since no publisher released word counts for her novels. From the Library of Congress records for the Knopf first editions: The Witching Hour (1990) at 965 pages, The Vampire Lestat (1985) at 481, The Queen of the Damned (1988) at 448, and Interview with the Vampire (1976) at 371.

The Witching Hour is her longest by a wide margin, roughly double the next book on that list. Paperback reissues push it past a thousand pages, which is why you will see 1,038 and 1,056 quoted for the same novel. Check which edition a page count belongs to before you repeat it.

At a typical 300 to 350 words per page for a hardcover of that era, The Witching Hour lands somewhere near 290,000 to 340,000 words. That is an estimate derived from the page count, not a figure Rice or Knopf ever published. Across 37 novels between 1976 and 2018 her output stands up next to any commercial novelist of her generation, and she got there without counting a single word.

What You Can Steal

  • Ignore any daily word count attributed to Anne Rice. She never gave one, and the 3,000 figure came out of a listicle that needed to fill a cell.
  • Try measuring in scenes instead of words. Rice aimed at a chapter a day, which keeps the unit of progress tied to the story rather than to a counter.
  • Clear a block of time before you start, then defend it. Her method depended on choosing a window with minimal distractions and giving the book everything inside it.
  • Budget for the second pass. Five weeks of drafting cost her ten and a half weeks of revision. The fast draft is only half the schedule.
