How Did Arthur Conan Doyle Write Sherlock Holmes?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Two things made Sherlock Holmes possible. A real doctor Doyle had watched work, and a run of empty afternoons in a medical practice that barely had patients.

The Direct Answer
He modeled Holmes on his old medical mentor and wrote the first novel in about three weeks.
The mentor was Dr Joseph Bell, per Doyle's own account in Memories and Adventures (1924).

A Detective Built From a Real Doctor

Doyle didn't dream up Holmes's powers from nothing. He lifted them from Dr Joseph Bell, the Edinburgh surgeon he had clerked for as a medical student in the late 1870s. Bell could glance at a stranger and name the man's trade, his recent travels, sometimes the illness he had walked in with, all from small physical signs most people never noticed. Doyle watched him read outpatients that way, again and again, and it lodged in his memory. It's said Bell once pegged a patient as a recently discharged Scottish soldier who had served in the Caribbean, reading it off the man's bearing and his health before a word was spoken.

It is no wonder that after the study of such a character I used and amplified his methods when in later life I tried to build up a scientific detective who solved cases on his own merits and not through the folly of the criminal.

Arthur Conan Doyle, Memories and Adventures (1924)

When he came to write his first detective story, Doyle handed that gift to a fictional character and dressed it up as a science. Holmes calls it deduction. Underneath, it is Bell's clinical eye pointed at crime instead of disease. The trick that made a great diagnostician became the trick that made a great detective.

Written in Three Weeks, Between Patients

Doyle wrote fast because he had the time and little else. He was twenty-seven, running a thin practice at 1 Bush Villas in Southsea, and the patients weren't exactly queuing at the door. He filled the quiet at his desk. A Study in Scarlet, the novel that introduced Holmes and Watson, took him roughly three weeks in 1886. Several publishers passed on it before Ward, Lock and Co. bought the thing outright for a flat £25, all rights, and printed it in the 1887 Beeton's Christmas Annual. He later regretted selling it so cheaply.

That speed is worth sitting with. The character who would define the rest of Doyle's life, and outlast him by generations, arrived in under a month of stolen hours. He built the machine before he understood how valuable it was. By 1893 he had grown so tired of Holmes that he pushed him off the Reichenbach Falls, only to be forced into a resurrection by readers who refused to let go.

The novels introduced Holmes, but the short stories are what turned him into a phenomenon. In 1891 The Strand Magazine began running self-contained Holmes cases, starting with A Scandal in Bohemia, each one a complete puzzle a reader could finish on a train. Doyle understood the appeal of a recurring hero in stand-alone episodes before serialized characters were common, and the format let him write to his strengths. Set a strange problem, let Watson narrate his own bafflement, then let Holmes unfold the chain of small observations that cracks it open.

Watson matters here more than he gets credit for. By telling the stories through a bright but ordinary companion, Doyle could hide the solution in plain sight and still surprise the reader at the reveal. It's a structural trick as much as a character one, and it made Holmes look brilliant on every single page.

What You Can Steal

  • Borrow a real person's skill. Holmes works because Bell was real first. Study someone who is genuinely brilliant at something, then hand that ability to a character.
  • Use the dead time. Doyle wrote in the gaps of a job that wasn't working. Your empty hours are a first draft waiting to happen.
  • Get it down fast, fix it later. Three weeks produced the whole story. Momentum on a rough draft beats a perfect sentence you never reach.
  • Give your character a method. Holmes has a repeatable way of seeing the world. A system readers can watch work is stickier than a clever one-off plot.
