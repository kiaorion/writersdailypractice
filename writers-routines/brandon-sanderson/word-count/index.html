How Long Are Brandon Sanderson's Books?

Kia Orion | August 2, 2026 | 5 min read

Sanderson's daily minimum gets quoted constantly. His book lengths get quoted far less, and they are the more startling number. A single Stormlight Archive novel now runs close to half a million words, which is roughly five average thrillers stacked end to end.

The Direct Answer
384,000 to 488,000 words per Stormlight Archive novel.
The Way of Kings about 384,000 and Wind and Truth about 488,000, with the series past 2.2 million words in total. His standalone novels are far shorter: Elantris around 201,000, Mistborn: The Final Empire around 210,000.

The Stormlight Archive, Book by Book

The counts here come from Coppermind, the fan wiki that tallies the published text chapter by chapter. The Way of Kings (2010) runs about 384,265 words and Words of Radiance (2014) about 398,238. Oathbringer (2017) jumps to 454,440, Rhythm of War (2020) to 455,891, and Wind and Truth (2024) to 487,746. Add the two novellas and the series stands at roughly 2.28 million words.

Wind and Truth carries three different official-looking numbers, which is a useful lesson in how slippery a word count can be. Peter Ahlstrom, Sanderson's editorial director, put it at 495,000 words in the State of the Sanderson post for 2024, counting the text printed on the illustrations. The manuscript delivered to Tor was reported at 491,000. Coppermind's count of the published prose is 487,746. All three are correct measurements of slightly different objects.

Sanderson's own round number for the first book is 400,000. In a 2019 progress update he wrote that book one was around that figure and the next two were longer, which lines up with the tallies.

The Rest of the Shelf

Outside Stormlight the books are ordinary sized. Elantris (2005) runs about 201,372 words. The original Mistborn trilogy goes 210,103, then 244,371, then 225,372. Warbreaker is around 196,014 and The Alloy of Law only 94,652.

The four secret projects he wrote during the pandemic and funded through a record-breaking Kickstarter in 2022 come to roughly 400,000 words combined. Sanderson made the comparison himself in that year's State of the Sanderson post, noting that all four books plus Defiant added together were not much longer than a single Stormlight novel.

What That Costs Him Per Year

About 300,000 words. He gives that figure on his own FAQ and used it again in the 2023 State of the Sanderson as a forward planning assumption, working out how many years three future books would take at that rate. The same post records that he started 2023 with about 100,000 words of Stormlight five written and finished the year at 450,000.

The daily arithmetic is messier than the headline suggests. He writes new fiction four days a week rather than seven, keeping one day for email, meetings, and grading, and he has said a typical week produces between 8,000 and 15,000 words. On Stormlight specifically he described averaging around 2,000 a day and called that slow for him, with 3,000 being a better rate.

While guidelines for wordcounts are helpful, I'm careful not to treat them like a factory quota, to be achieved regardless of quality.

Brandon Sanderson, Stormlight progress update, 2019

His record single day is worth knowing before you measure yourself against him. Writing the annotations for Mistborn: The Final Empire in 2007, he mentioned once doing 16,000 words in a day to finish a book, then beating it with about 23,000 on another. Both were end-of-book sprints, and he has said plainly that he writes faster at the end of a book than at the start.

For the full picture of how he works, including the two-block schedule and the hotel front desk where the habit started, see the complete Brandon Sanderson writing routine page.

What You Can Steal

  • Count the week, not the day. Sanderson's real rhythm is 8,000 to 15,000 words across four writing days. A weekly number survives a bad Tuesday.
  • Reserve one day a week for everything that is not writing. The admin day is the reason the other four stay clean.
  • Expect to be faster at the end. If your pace is slow at the twenty percent mark, that is often the book rather than you.
  • Treat the number as a gauge, not a quota. Sanderson uses word counts to judge progress and refuses to hit them at the cost of quality. That distinction is the whole difference between a system and a treadmill.
