What Is Brandon Sanderson's Writing Routine?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Sanderson's routine surprises people who picture novelists chasing the sunrise. He does his real drafting at night, splits the working day into two blocks, and protects a hard word-count floor. That structure is the whole reason he can write as much as he does.

The Direct Answer
Two writing blocks a day, roughly noon to 4 p.m. and 10 p.m. to 4 a.m., with a minimum of about 2,000 words.
He drafts at night, by his own account on brandonsanderson.com, because the house is quiet.

Two Shifts, Split by Family Time

Picture the opposite of the dawn-ritual writer. Sanderson wakes late, closer to noon than to sunrise, and the first hour or so of his day goes to email, business, and family logistics before any fiction gets written. He also runs Dragonsteel, the company that publishes much of his work, so that early stretch is real administration rather than a warm-up. Once it's cleared, the first writing session settles in during the early afternoon and runs a few hours.

Then he stops. Dinner and time with his family fill the evening, and he doesn't try to draft through it. The second block starts around ten at night, once the house has gone quiet, and carries into the small hours of the morning. Two sessions, a long break in the middle, and the heavier drafting happening while most people sleep.

Asked to describe a typical day, he laid the shape out plainly.

most days, usually, formally, I write from about noon until four, and then I'll hang out with my family and do other stuff until about ten, and I'll start writing from about ten until 4AM.

Brandon Sanderson, brandonsanderson.com FAQ

Why Night, and Why a Fixed Minimum

The late hours aren't a quirk for their own sake. Sanderson writes at night because there's less noise and nobody needs him, which lets him sink into a scene and stay there. The quiet is the tool. During daylight the interruptions pile up, so he moves the deep work to the hours that belong only to him.

The second pillar is a word count he won't skip. His daily minimum sits around 2,000 words. On a rough day he writes exactly that and closes the laptop with a clear conscience. On a strong day he clears four or five thousand. Spread across a year, the habit lands near 300,000 words, and that steady drip, rather than any single marathon, is the real engine behind his catalog. Stamina beats speed here. He shows up every day and holds the line.

He treats the schedule as a job with set hours, which takes the drama out of it. There's no waiting around for inspiration, and a slow afternoon doesn't trigger a guilt spiral, because the target is small enough to hit almost every day and the hours are already blocked.

The thinking doesn't stop when the writing does. Sanderson has said that even at the gym or while drifting off to sleep, part of his head is turning over the next scene or planning tomorrow's pages. By the time he sits down for a block, he usually already knows roughly what he's about to write, which is part of why two separate sessions stay productive instead of stalling on a blank screen.

That consistency is the part people underrate. He writes every day, in a steady rhythm he's held for years. A single 2,000-word day looks unremarkable. Two hundred of them in a row become a novel.

What You Can Steal

  • Write when the world leaves you alone. Sanderson chose night for the quiet. Find the hour nobody can interrupt and guard it.
  • Set a floor you can always clear. A 2,000-word minimum you can hit on a bad day beats a heroic goal you dread and dodge.
  • Split a long session in two. Two shorter blocks with a real break between them keep the tank from running dry by evening.
  • Give the first hour to admin. Clear email and logistics before you start so the writing block stays only for writing.
