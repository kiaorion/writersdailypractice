Celeste Ng's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 23, 2026 | 9 min read

Celeste Ng's debut novel took six years to write. Everything I Never Told You came out in 2014, opened with one of the strongest first lines in recent American fiction, and went on to become an Amazon Best Book of the Year. What most readers don't know is how much of that six years was revision. Ng writes slowly and deliberately, drafting in fragments, often working outside her home in cafes, and spending far more time reworking existing pages than generating new ones. She's said in interviews that she sometimes has to let a book find its own structure before she can impose one. That's a particular kind of patience, and it shows in the work.

Her books operate at a specific temperature. The tension in them isn't the loud kind. It builds from watching a family that looks ordinary from the outside begin to pull apart at its seams. Little Fires Everywhere maintains that suburban unease for nearly four hundred pages before the thing that was always going to happen finally does. That pacing isn't accidental. It comes from a routine that favors depth over speed, revision over momentum, and craft training over raw output. The routine is worth understanding because it's the right model for a certain kind of novel, and most writers looking at it are tempted to dismiss it before seeing what it actually produces.

The Routine at a Glance

Wake Time
Not publicly documented. Ng has been candid about how motherhood scrambles any fixed morning schedule; writing time shifts around her son's needs rather than anchoring a predictable early hour.
Writing Location
Cafes, often. She's mentioned writing outside the home to manufacture psychological distance from household obligations and get a different relationship with her material. She also writes at home when her son is at school.
Daily Output
No fixed quota. Her approach prioritizes quality of engagement over word count. Her debut took approximately six years from start to publication, with a significant portion of that time in revision rather than drafting forward.
Tools
Laptop. She's been open about the tension between social media's pull and the sustained attention a novel demands, and has discussed managing that friction deliberately.
Famous Ritual
Rereading. She returns to what she's written before she can move forward, treating revision as the real work rather than a corrective pass at the end of a draft.
Books Written This Way
Everything I Never Told You (2014), Little Fires Everywhere (2017), Our Missing Hearts (2022).

Writing Around Motherhood

Ng's son was born before Little Fires Everywhere was finished, and the experience of new motherhood moved directly into the novel's center. The book is substantially about what mothers want for their children versus what those children want for themselves, about the particular weight of a parent's good intentions, and about what a woman gives up when she makes herself entirely into a mother. Ng was writing from inside that material, with a toddler in the house and a book deadline closing in.

In interviews she's been frank about how much harder the second book was to write than the first. With Everything I Never Told You, she was working largely in private, without a readership waiting and without a child altering her schedule daily. By Little Fires Everywhere she had both. The irony that her novel about suburban motherhood was being drafted inside the actual conditions of suburban motherhood isn't lost on her. There's a quality in the book, in the way Mia Warren's artistic life keeps spilling into Elena Richardson's ordered world, where you can feel the author's own divided life somewhere behind it.

What she built, practically speaking, was a mobile practice rather than a fixed schedule. Writing in cafes solved a problem that a dedicated home office couldn't: getting out of the house meant leaving behind the visible pile of undone tasks that competes for attention during any writing session at home. Childcare covered the school-day window. The writing happened in whatever gaps those two things together created. That's a much less romantic account of the creative life than most writing advice books offer, and it's a much more honest one for anyone writing serious fiction while also raising a child.

The books themselves carry the weight of that arrangement. The domestic textures in Ng's fiction, the specific anxiety of being a mother who also needs to be an artist, the way women in her novels watch their own ambitions and those of their children collide, come from a writer who was inside that tension every day she sat down to work. You can't manufacture that quality of attention on a subject. It comes from actually living it.

The Slow Careful Draft

Six years is a long time for a first novel, and Ng has spoken about it without apology. She's said that she writes slowly, that she revises constantly, and that she tends not to move forward until what she's written feels right. This is the opposite of the write-fast-and-fix-it-later approach that's become popular in writing advice circles, and it produces a different kind of book. Ng's sentences are not shaggy. They don't have the loose, improvisational feel of a writer who drafted at speed and tightened in revision. They read like sentences that were written carefully, with full attention, and then revised more. The compression in her prose comes from that double process.

She's talked in interviews about returning to the beginning of a section before she can continue writing it. The rereading is how she re-enters the register of the book, calibrates the next sentences against the ones that came before them, and keeps the voice consistent across a draft that might span years of real time. This approach is expensive in calendar days and has no particular payoff on a weekly word count chart, but it does produce novels where the sentences on page three hundred match the sentences on page three in terms of care and precision. That kind of consistency is genuinely hard to achieve, and most writers who try to draft fast and fix it later find out the damage can't always be fully recovered in revision.

The slowness also does something less obvious. It forces the unconscious to keep working on the problem between sessions. A writer who drafts forward every day, hitting a word count and moving on, hands a lot of control to the daily output. A writer who keeps returning to what already exists, reworking it before adding more, maintains a tighter grip on what the book is at any given moment. The book doesn't run away from her. It stays close to the original intention, or at least stays accountable to it.

For literary fiction, that distinction matters. Genre fiction can often absorb a looser draft because plot momentum carries the reader through scenes that aren't working at the sentence level. Literary fiction can't absorb that the same way. In a novel like Everything I Never Told You, where the story is essentially about the years before a girl's death and everything a family refused to say to each other, the prose is doing almost all the work. If the sentences don't hold, the novel doesn't hold. Ng's slow approach protects that.

She's described the work of revision as making the novel into what it was always trying to be, once you can finally see the shape of it clearly enough to finish it.

Suburbia and Family as Material

All three of Ng's novels are set inside families under pressure in American spaces with a suburban quality to them. Shaker Heights, Ohio in Little Fires Everywhere, the small Ohio college town of Everything I Never Told You, and the near-future America of Our Missing Hearts all share a sense of domestic confinement, of characters trapped inside the structures meant to protect them. Ng grew up in Pittsburgh and later Cleveland, and she's said that growing up in those spaces gave her a direct line to the tensions her books are about, the gap between a community's stated values and its actual behavior, the way orderly surfaces hide a lot of pressure.

The suburb in her fiction is never innocent. It's where racial anxiety, class performance, parental expectation, and suppressed individual desire all converge. Little Fires Everywhere uses Shaker Heights's real progressive self-image against itself. The neighborhood is known for its planned integration and its genuine belief in its own good intentions, and the novel is interested in exactly that gap between belief and behavior. Ng is working a tradition here that includes Richard Yates and John Cheever, but her version of the suburban crack-up is filtered through the specific experience of a Chinese American woman who grew up watching her family navigate a white middle-class world that welcomed them and excluded them at the same time.

The domestic scale is a deliberate framing that lets her work on large questions through small rooms. The custody battle at the center of Little Fires Everywhere is about race, class, motherhood, and belonging, but it plays out in living rooms and courtrooms in Ohio. Our Missing Hearts scales up to a dystopian America but keeps its emotional core in a mother-son relationship. The family stays central across all three books. That consistency reflects where Ng's attention has always been, and where her prose is most charged.

The MFA Craft Foundation

Ng holds an MFA in creative writing, and while plenty of fiction writers downplay their graduate training once they have a few books out, her craft approach is visibly shaped by it. The attention to sentence-level precision, the structural sophistication of Everything I Never Told You with its backwards-running timeline and its omniscient narrator moving between the heads of an entire family, the careful management of point of view in Little Fires Everywhere as it juggles Elena and Mia's perspectives across the same events: these are techniques taught in graduate workshops, and they take sustained practice to deploy at novel length without the seams showing.

She's talked in interviews about how the MFA gave her a community of readers as much as craft tools. Writers who've been through serious workshop training read differently. They look at a scene and ask why it's structured the way it is, whether the POV choice is the right one, what the scene is doing for the book beyond its surface content. Ng still reads this way, and she brings that level of interrogation to her own drafts. That's partly why revision is so central to how she works. The workshop reader she became is always looking over the writer's shoulder, asking whether the scene is earning its place.

There's a practical point here for writers who didn't get an MFA and sometimes feel that background is a closed door. What the MFA primarily trains is a way of reading: slowly, analytically, with attention to craft decisions rather than just to effect. That reading habit is available to anyone who wants it. Ng has recommended in interviews that writers read widely in the genre they're working in, read with attention to how the effects they admire are being produced, and then bring the same analytical eye to their own pages. The credential matters less than the habit, and the habit can be built without setting foot in a workshop room.

Her body of work is a good argument for what that training produces at its best. Three novels in eight years, each one formally distinct from the last, each one working in the same emotional and geographic territory while finding a new structural approach to it. If you want to understand what kind of reader you are before you figure out what to write next, the genre quiz can help you locate yourself, but what Ng's routine ultimately demonstrates is that slow, revision-heavy, craft-conscious work is a real method for producing literary fiction that holds up over time.

Celeste Ng's Writing Routine

The Celeste Ng writing routine is harder to describe in concrete terms than Hemingway's or Morrison's because she hasn't documented it as systematically in interviews. What she has said, across conversations with NPR, Lit Hub, and various other outlets over the course of her career, adds up to a picture of a writer who works in available time rather than protected blocks, who treats revision as the central activity rather than a finishing pass, and who keeps a close relationship between her writing life and her domestic one rather than trying to wall them off from each other.

She's described writing in cafes as a way of manufacturing the psychological distance that a separate study would provide. A coffee shop creates a defined writing context. You're not at home, so you can't do the dishes. You're there with your laptop and a drink, and the social contract of the space says you're supposed to be working. For a writer whose material is so deeply domestic, the physical removal from the home seems to be how she gets enough perspective on her subject to see it clearly while she's actually writing about it.

The revision-first approach that characterizes her process means that a Celeste Ng writing session often looks less productive by external measures than it actually is. She might spend ninety minutes on two paragraphs she wrote last week, make them substantially better, and then stop. Measured in words generated, that's a slow day. Measured in the quality of the pages that will eventually exist in the finished book, it's the opposite. Her process bets on depth over coverage, and three published novels are the evidence for that bet.

Sources

The primary sources for Ng's writing habits are her own interviews. Her conversation with Lit Hub on writing about race in America covers both her craft approach and the political intentions behind Little Fires Everywhere. Her NPR interview around the publication of Everything I Never Told You in 2014 discusses the six-year drafting process and her revision practice. Her own site, celesteng.com, has accumulated interviews and event transcripts over the years where she returns to questions of process, motherhood, and craft. Additional context comes from her conversations at the time of each novel's publication in outlets including BookPage and Powell's Books, as well as her collected social media presence where she's discussed the friction between online attention and the sustained focus writing requires.

What You Can Steal

Ng's routine is useful precisely because it doesn't depend on ideal conditions. Here's what actually transfers:

  • Get out of the house to write. If domestic responsibilities compete with your attention while you're at your home desk, a cafe or library solves that problem without requiring anything more complicated. The change of environment signals the writing state the same way any pre-work ritual does, and it removes the visual distraction of everything that isn't done.
  • Treat revision as the real work, not the cleanup. Ng's slowness comes partly from counting reworked pages as productive time, not just forward drafting. A session spent making four paragraphs substantially better isn't a failed session. It may be the most important work of the week.
  • Let the book find its structure before you impose one. She's described allowing herself to write without a clear outline and then rereading to find the shape that's already there. This takes longer than plotting everything in advance, but it keeps you from forcing the novel into a container it doesn't actually want.
  • Use your own material without apology. The domestic tensions, the motherhood experience, the specific geographic and cultural context Ng grew up in: all of it went directly into the work. The autobiographical pressure behind her fiction is one reason the family dynamics in her novels feel so credible. Your real life is closer to your best material than you probably think.
  • Read like a writer. Whether you have an MFA or not, developing the habit of asking how a scene is built, why a POV choice was made, what structural decision is creating an effect you admire gives you a craft vocabulary you can apply to your own pages. Ng brings that analytical eye to her own drafts, and the finished novels show it.
