How Did Charles Dickens Write?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Dickens couldn't begin until his desk looked right. Same ornaments, same places, every morning, before the goose-quill ever touched the paper.

The Direct Answer
With a goose-quill and blue ink, at a desk arranged in a fixed order.
The objects and the blue ink are catalogued in Mason Currey's Daily Rituals, drawing on accounts of his working method.

The Tools

Dickens wrote by hand with a goose-quill pen. In his early years he used black ink, which has since faded to brown on the surviving manuscripts, but by the late 1840s he had switched to blue ink, partly because it dried faster and let him keep moving down the page. Except for letters, he wrote on one side of the paper only, which left the facing space free for the heavy revision that came later. He called his finished pages slips, and a typical day produced two to four of them.

His manuscripts back up the picture of a writer who hammered the draft into shape. The pages are dense with crossings-out, insertions, and second thoughts crammed into the margins. The blue ink was a small efficiency, but it fit the larger pattern of a man who tuned the mechanics of writing so the work itself could run without friction.

He worked seated at a desk placed in front of a window for the light. Around two thousand words on a strong day meant a quill moving steadily for hours, so the smooth-flowing blue ink and the one-sided slips were the kind of practical choices that quietly add up over a career. Small habits, repeated, were the whole point.

The Desk Had to Be Right

Before he could work, a specific set of objects had to sit in their assigned places. There was a small vase of fresh flowers, a large paper knife, a gilt leaf with a rabbit perched on it, and two bronze statuettes, one of a pair of fat toads locked in a duel and one of a gentleman swarmed by puppies. The desk itself sat in front of a window. Nothing about the arrangement was random, and he set the same pieces out the same way each morning before a word got written.

He carried the ritual with him too. The objects traveled, so the desk in a rented house or a borrowed room could be made to feel like the desk at home. That portability mattered for a man who moved between London and Kent and toured constantly, since the setup was less about the objects than about the signal they sent that work was now beginning.

The same hunger for order showed up away from the desk. Dickens was famous for tidying obsessively, straightening furniture and hating anything out of place, and the arranged ornaments belong to that same streak. Some of it was plainly superstition. He was a man of fixed habits who liked the world set up just so before he committed himself to the blank page, and the ritual gave him a small, controllable run-up into the uncontrollable part.

None of this would matter if the writing underneath were sloppy, and it wasn't. The fastidious surface, the blue ink, the placed objects, the one-sided slips, all of it propped up a working method that turned out some of the most ambitious novels in English. The neatness was scaffolding for the imagination, not a substitute for it.

No city clerk was ever more methodical or orderly than he.

Charley Dickens on his father, quoted in Mason Currey's Daily Rituals

It reads like fuss, and maybe it was, but the ritual flipped a switch. Setting the desk told his head it was time, the same way the nine o'clock start did. If you want the hours and the long afternoon walks that bracketed all this, here is the full Charles Dickens writing routine.

What You Can Steal

  • Build a start ritual. A small fixed setup signals your brain to begin, no willpower required.
  • Pick tools you actually like. Dickens cared about his ink. Friction at the pen is friction on the page.
  • Leave room to revise. Writing on one side of the sheet gave him space to attack the draft later.
  • Keep the setup portable. He took the ornaments with him so the ritual worked anywhere.
