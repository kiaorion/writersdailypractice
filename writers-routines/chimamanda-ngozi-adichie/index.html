Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 1, 2026 | 9 min read

Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie does not write every day. She's said so plainly, in interviews across two decades, and the admission tends to confuse people who expect novelists to be disciplined in a clock-punching, Trollope-gets-up-at-five kind of way. Adichie writes when the story is alive, when the characters are pulling her somewhere she hasn't been yet. When that pull is there, she goes obsessive: skipping showers, ignoring her phone, reading only poetry on breaks, eating chocolate when it stalls. When the pull isn't there, she steps away. No word counts. No morning alarm set for writing time. The work shows up when it's ready, and she's ready for it.

This sounds like the advice that gets writers in trouble, the romantic version where you wait for inspiration and end up with nothing. But Adichie's three novels, her essay collections, and her recent return from a twelve-year fiction hiatus are evidence that her approach actually works, if you understand what it requires. The practice behind Purple Hibiscus, Half of a Yellow Sun, and Americanah is a different kind of discipline, one organized around attention and readiness rather than scheduled output.

Profile

The Routine at a Glance

Wake Time
No fixed writing hour. Adichie works when the story has its grip on her, not on a preset schedule. She splits her time between Nigeria and the United States, and the rhythms of both lives shape when she sits down.
Writing Location
Wherever she is. Adichie has never publicly attached herself to a single writing room or city. She wrote Purple Hibiscus largely while living in the US, using evenings after caregiving responsibilities were done. She's described needing solitude more than any particular desk.
Daily Output
No tracked word count. When it's going well, she works in long, absorbed stretches. When it's not, she steps away from the desk rather than grinding out words that won't survive revision anyway.
Tools
Adichie has described being an eavesdropper and keeper of notebooks, collecting overheard dialogue and anecdotes she considers "fiction-worthy." Her research process for historical work like Half of a Yellow Sun involved photographs, academic texts, and family oral history.
Famous Ritual
During absorbed writing periods, she reads only poetry on breaks. She's described this as a way of staying inside the writing state, keeping the prose-brain running without pulling her too far into narrative prose she didn't write.
Books Written This Way
Purple Hibiscus (2003), Half of a Yellow Sun (2006), Americanah (2013), Dream Count (2025). Also: We Should All Be Feminists, Dear Ijeawele, The Thing Around Your Neck (stories).

The Writer Who Follows the Story

In a 2014 interview with Elle magazine, Adichie described her working state with a specificity I've never seen matched in any other writer's self-account. "When the writing is going well, I'm obsessive. I don't shower, I don't take phone calls, I hardly respond to text messages, I don't do email. I take breaks only to read, and usually I read poetry. When it's not going well, I just lie in bed and eat chocolate." That sentence contains the whole shape of her practice. Two modes, and she honors both of them.

Most writing advice is organized around continuity, the idea that you show up every day and the work accumulates over time. Adichie's practice is organized around intensity. When the story is pulling, she goes all the way in. She removes herself from the social world, guards the writing state like it's a temperature she might lose if she opens the door. The poetry break is the tell: she won't read narrative fiction while she's drafting, because it would mean handing her imagination over to someone else's voice. Poetry keeps the creative channel open without the risk of contamination.

What this requires, and what the romantic version of "wait for inspiration" leaves out, is a trained attentiveness to when the story is ready. Adichie has said her process isn't entirely conscious. She doesn't plan in the conventional sense. What she has instead is a highly developed ability to notice when material has become fiction-worthy, when a character's voice is clear enough to follow. She describes herself as "an unrepentant eavesdropper and a collector of stories," someone who records overheard dialogue and anecdotes not because she knows what to do with them yet but because something in her flags them as alive. The discipline is in the listening, not the clocking in.

Purple Hibiscus and the Distance That Made the Book

Adichie left Nigeria at nineteen, in 1997, to study in the United States. She enrolled at Eastern Connecticut State University and later transferred to complete a degree in communications and political science. She was nineteen, in America, and the distance from Nigeria was doing something she hadn't anticipated. She missed it in the particular way you miss a place you're still trying to understand, and the homesickness became the engine of her first novel.

Purple Hibiscus came out of that longing. She was writing toward Nigeria because she couldn't be there, and the emotional pressure of the distance gave the book its texture. She completed it while managing the rhythms of student life in the US: caregiving duties during the day, writing in the evenings after those responsibilities cleared. The writing wasn't stolen from pre-dawn hours or carved from a lunch break. It happened in whatever open space the day left at its end.

What's interesting about Purple Hibiscus as a first novel is how clearly Adichie understood the source of the book's energy. She's described an earlier attempt at a novel that failed because she was "writing what I thought was hip. It wasn't true." The breakthrough with Purple Hibiscus came when she stopped trying to be fashionable and started following what she actually cared about: an Igbo Catholic family in post-colonial Nigeria, a daughter working through a father's authoritarian love, a world she knew from inside. The first burst of writing went well because she'd finally found the true material. That's the version of her method that gets repeated in every interview she gives about starting a book.

The lesson inside it is one of the most useful things I've read about writing motivation. If you're writing what you think you should be writing, rather than what you actually need to write, the obsessive state Adichie describes won't arrive. The material has to matter to you specifically before the pull becomes strong enough to make you forget to eat, shower, answer your phone.

Half of a Yellow Sun and the Research She Had to Cut

Adichie's second novel took four years to write. She had been thinking about the Nigerian Civil War, the Biafra conflict of 1967 to 1970, for most of her life. Her parents lived through it. Her family's oral history was threaded through with it. She grew up, she's said, in the shadow of a war that ended before she was born, and she felt it as a kind of ancestral weight, something the generation before her couldn't quite put down.

The research process for Half of a Yellow Sun was extensive. She surrounded herself with photographs, academic texts, contemporaneous accounts, and testimony from people who had been inside Biafra. She interviewed her father. She read everything she could find. Then she had to do something harder: she had to cut most of it. In revision, she disciplined herself to remove every passage that showed off the research rather than serving the story. "I had to restrain myself," she's said. "Passages that were there simply because I wanted to show what I knew had to go."

This is a principle that runs through her whole working method. Adichie writes from what she knows and feels, and the research exists to give that material its proper context, not to become the point of the book. Half of a Yellow Sun is a novel about people surviving a war, not a history lesson delivered through fiction. The distinction matters because it's what makes the book emotionally available to readers who know nothing about Biafra. They're following Ugwu, Olanna, and Richard because those characters are alive, not because Adichie wants to teach them Nigerian Civil War history.

The book won the Orange Prize for Fiction in 2007. It's the kind of novel that gets written by someone who spent years accumulating material and then trusted herself to leave most of it out.

Americanah and the Book That Grew From a Short Story

Adichie has lived off and on in the United States since she was nineteen. That experience, accumulated over years, is what became Americanah. The novel started as a short story, then grew. She describes the process of discovering that the material wanted more space than a short story could hold, and following it into novel territory without a predetermined shape for where it would go.

The book's central character, Ifemelu, runs a blog about race in America. Adichie has been clear that the blog posts are partly autobiographical in spirit: she draws from her own experience of arriving in the US and slowly learning that she was, by American categories, Black in a way she hadn't been in Nigeria. She's described a moment early in her American years when she raised her hand in a class, claiming authorship of an essay, and noticed a flash of surprise on her professor's face. That flash, and what it revealed about expectation, stayed with her. It went into the book.

Her process on Americanah was the same as always: she followed the characters. When she wasn't sure whether Ifemelu would return to Nigeria at the novel's end, she didn't make a structural decision from above. She waited to see what Ifemelu wanted. "As the book progressed," she's explained, "she really wanted to go back, so she did." This sounds like a writer surrendering control, but it's actually something more demanding. It requires you to know a character so thoroughly that their wants feel as real as your own, then to trust those wants over your own ideas about where the plot should go.

Twelve Years, a Writer's Block, and Dream Count

Between Americanah in 2013 and Dream Count in 2025, Adichie published no fiction. She wrote essays, speeches, and her short feminist manifesto Dear Ijeawele. She became, arguably, the most widely known African writer alive. She gave a TED talk that has been viewed over sixty million times. And she couldn't write a novel.

She's described this period with a candor that's rare among writers of her stature. "I just had years when I couldn't write fiction," she said in interviews around Dream Count's publication. She tried poetry. She re-read novels she loved. She worked her way back into fiction through short stories, taking smaller steps before attempting the longer form again. The thing that finally broke the block was the death of her mother. The grief opened something. She described it as a creative channel that had been stopped up for years suddenly clearing, and she wrote Dream Count in the space that opened.

What's worth noting about this is that Adichie's approach to her blocked years was consistent with her approach to writing generally: she followed what was available rather than forcing what wasn't. She didn't manufacture productivity through discipline. She worked in the forms that were accessible, poetry and essays, and trusted that the novels would return when they were ready. For twelve years, they weren't. Then they were.

Sources

The primary sources for Adichie's working method are scattered across a decade of interviews. Her 2014 Elle interview with Keziah Weir, "Meet Beyonce's Favorite Novelist," contains the most direct account of her writing states, including the obsessive periods and the chocolate-and-lying-in-bed alternative. Her Rumpus interview from June 2014 covers the organic process behind Americanah. The JJ Marsh author interview captures her approach to planning and character discovery. The Kenyon Review conversation and the Image Journal conversation add texture to her views on writing as private practice. For the biographical details, her Wikipedia entry is well-sourced. Her 2009 TED talk, "The Danger of a Single Story," remains the best single piece of evidence for how her thinking about narrative shapes her fiction. For Dream Count and the years-long creative drought, the best accounts come from her 2025 press interviews, including coverage at BellaNaija and NPR. Her essay collections, particularly We Should All Be Feminists and Dear Ijeawele, offer a parallel view of how she works when fiction isn't available: focused, fast, and driven by a different kind of urgency than the novels.

What You Can Steal

Adichie's approach is particularly useful if the daily-word-count model has never worked for you, or if you've spent years trying to force output that feels wrong and wondering why the books aren't coming. Here's what actually translates:

  • Train yourself to recognize when the story is alive. Adichie's obsessive state isn't something she manufactures. It arrives when the material is genuinely ready. Your job is to develop the attentiveness to notice that state and go all the way in when it's there, rather than letting it pass because you had other things scheduled.
  • Collect before you write. She describes herself as an eavesdropper and story collector. The notebook habit, recording dialogue, anecdotes, images that strike you as fiction-worthy, is how the raw material accumulates before the writing begins. You're not wasting time between projects. You're stocking the pantry.
  • Read only poetry while drafting. If you're absorbed in a project and you need a break, prose fiction from other writers can pull you out of your own voice and rhythm. Poetry keeps the creative channel open without the risk. Try it for a week and see what happens to your drafts.
  • Research to depth, then cut. Adichie's discipline on Half of a Yellow Sun was knowing which research served the story and which served her ego. The rule: if a passage is there to show what you know rather than to deepen the characters or move the scene, cut it. Readers will feel the restraint as confidence.
  • Honor the dry periods without surrendering to them. Twelve years between novels. Adichie didn't stop working, she shifted to the forms that were accessible. Essays, speeches, short fiction. Staying in the work, even sideways, matters more than pushing through a novel that isn't ready.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie write every day?

No. Adichie has said she does not follow a rigid daily schedule. She writes when the story has its pull on her. When it does, she works obsessively, skipping showers and ignoring her phone, and reads only poetry on breaks. When the pull isn't there, she steps away rather than grinding out words that won't survive revision.

Where did Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie write Purple Hibiscus?

Adichie wrote Purple Hibiscus while living in the United States, largely in the evenings after her daytime responsibilities were done. She's said she began the novel as a way of understanding Nigeria after leaving it at nineteen, and that the distance from home gave the book its emotional pressure.

How did Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie research Half of a Yellow Sun?

She surrounded herself with photographs, academic accounts, and personal testimony, including her father's memories of living through Biafra. Then in revision she cut the majority of it, removing anything that showed off the research rather than serving the story. The discipline was in knowing what to leave out.

What is Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie's most famous quote about writing?

"The single story creates stereotypes, and the problem with stereotypes is not that they are untrue, but that they are incomplete." This comes from her 2009 TED talk, but it governs her fiction practice entirely. She writes toward complexity and full interiority, against the single version of any person or place.
