What Is Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie's Writing Routine?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Adichie has never sold a tidy, hour-by-hour writing schedule, and she's honest about that. What she describes instead is a working rhythm shaped by long silences, heavy reading, and a refusal to force a book before it's ready.

The Direct Answer
There's no fixed daily quota. Her routine runs on wide reading, patience, and writing when the joy is there.
She went more than ten years between novels and leaned on poetry to stay close to language, per a 2025 CNN interview around Dream Count.

The Decade of Silence Before Dream Count

Adichie published Americanah in 2013 to enormous success. Her next novel, Dream Count, didn't arrive until 2025, a gap of more than ten years. That silence wasn't a sabbatical she chose. She has spoken openly about years when she feared she might never write another novel, a prospect she found genuinely frightening. So the honest starting point for any account of her routine is that a working writer's life includes long stretches where the book simply refuses to come.

Grief ran straight through those years. Her father died in 2020, during the pandemic, and her mother died the following year. The essay that grew into Notes on Grief came out of that loss. Rather than paper over the drought, Adichie has been candid that the words were gone for a long time and that she had to wait, and work, for them to return.

Reading Is the Daily Practice

The thing she held onto through the dry years was reading, and poetry above all. She has said poetry helped her stay connected to language while her own fiction stalled, keeping her ear tuned even when she couldn't write pages of her own. If there's a genuine daily discipline in her practice, this is it. Read constantly, pay attention to how sentences move, and trust that immersion in other people's words eventually loosens your own.

I read a lot more poetry in that period because I think poetry really helps with language.

Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie, CNN, 2025

Writing for Joy, Not the Clock

When the work turns difficult, what pulls her forward is the possibility of joy rather than a daily word count. She's described the pleasure of writing as the thing that makes the hard days worth pushing through. When a passage fights her, she tends to step back, leave it alone, and come back to it later instead of grinding it out at the desk. Her rhythm bends around the state of the work rather than a stopwatch.

She also guards the writing from outside pressure. Adichie has said she keeps the work separate from publishing, marketing, and even her own family's expectations, because thinking about what a publisher or a relative might want would change what comes out. While she's drafting, she tries to stay in what she has called a private, almost magical place, writing for herself first and worrying about readers later. That protected headspace, more than any timetable, is what her routine really depends on.

What You Can Steal

  • Make reading your non-negotiable. When your own writing stalls, keep the ear alive by reading, especially poetry.
  • Let a book take the time it takes. Ten years passed between her novels, and the work was still worth it.
  • Chase joy, not a quota. Step away from a stuck passage and return, rather than forcing words.
  • Be honest about the fallow spells. Silence and doubt are part of a real writing life.
