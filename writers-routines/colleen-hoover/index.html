Colleen Hoover's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 1, 2026 | 9 min read

Colleen Hoover wrote her first novel on a borrowed laptop in a mobile home in East Texas while her son was at play practice. She was a social worker at the time, her husband drove long-haul trucks, and the story she was writing was meant to be a Christmas gift for her mother, who had just gotten a Kindle and wanted something to read. There was no plan beyond that. No agent, no query letter, no word-count tracker. She self-published Slammed in January 2012, made thirty dollars in her first month, and paid the water bill with it. Eight months later, the book hit the New York Times bestseller list. By 2022, she was selling 8.6 million print copies in a single year.

The routine behind all of that is worth understanding, because it reads like the anti-advice version of every writing craft book ever written. Hoover doesn't write in the morning. She doesn't track word counts. She goes months without writing anything, then locks herself in a hotel room and doesn't come out until a book is finished. The thing that holds the whole approach together is the conviction that writing should never feel like work, and the discipline that lives inside that conviction, protecting it at all costs, is real and specific and learnable.

Profile

The Routine at a Glance

Wake Time
No fixed early wake time for writing. Hoover writes in the evenings, typically after family obligations, from around 9 PM to midnight when at home.
Writing Location
At home, usually on a couch or at a laptop in a domestic space. For intensive sessions, she checks into a hotel alone for a week or two at a time and writes until the book is done.
Daily Output
She doesn't set daily word count goals. Her pattern is long stretches of nothing followed by weeks of writing up to sixteen hours a day. She writes in chunks of roughly 5,000 words, then spends the next session editing those before pushing forward.
Tools
Laptop. No legal pads or longhand. She needs complete silence to write, removing batteries from clocks if necessary. Music gets her into the mood beforehand but stops when the actual writing starts.
Famous Ritual
New socks. Before any long writing day or hotel sprint, she puts on a brand-new pair of socks. She buys multiple packs before locking herself away to write. "I have it in my head that fresh, new socks make me a better writer," she said in a Goodreads Q&A.
Books Written This Way
Slammed, It Ends with Us, Verity, November 9, Ugly Love, Confess, and more than twenty others.

The Borrowed Laptop

The origin story matters more than most people realize. In 2011, Hoover was working for the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children in East Texas, a federal aid program. She and her husband lived in a mobile home with their three sons. She typed out the first draft of Slammed chapter by chapter on a laptop she'd borrowed, often writing while her youngest son was at play practice and she had a spare hour. The book was written in conditions that would qualify most writers for a full exemption from the obligation to produce anything.

What's interesting is that she says she didn't experience those circumstances as obstacles in the first place. She's said in multiple interviews that she wrote Slammed because she wanted to write it, full stop. The intended audience was her mother. There was no imagined reading public, no market calculation, no career anxiety threading through the prose. A woman with a borrowed laptop wrote the story she wanted to write, for one reader she loved, in the gaps between a full-time job and three children. That specific freedom, writing without anyone watching, produced something clean and emotionally direct. You can feel that in her early books. There's no posturing in them.

After she self-published in January 2012, a single five-star review from book blogger Maryse Black changed everything. Sales spiked. Within months, Slammed and its sequel Point of Retreat were both on the New York Times list. A traditional publisher came calling. The borrowed laptop chapter of her career was over, but the sensibility it produced stayed in the work.

Evening Hours and Hotel Sprints

Once the writing became her actual career, Hoover had to figure out a practice that fit her real life. She has three sons, a husband, and a home in Texas, and mornings in that life belong to other people. So the writing moved to the evenings. When she's at home and a project is moving, she settles in after the family is done with the day, usually around nine, and works until midnight or later. The house is quiet by then. No one needs anything from her. Those evening hours have a similar logic to Morrison's 4 AM setup, just run twelve hours later.

The other mode is the hotel sprint. Hoover has described checking into a hotel alone, buying several packs of socks, and staying until a draft is finished. No interruptions, no domestic logistics, no context-switching. She orders food in, keeps the room dark, and writes. This is what she does when she needs to finish something, when the normal evening pace isn't fast enough or focused enough. The hotel room is a pressure chamber. It's also, in its own way, the room of one's own that Virginia Woolf argued every writer needs, just rented by the week rather than maintained year-round.

What strikes me about this two-mode setup is how honest it is about the nature of creative work. Some books come out in a steady trickle of evening hours. Others need a contained, total immersion to get to the end. Most writers pretend there's one mode and then wonder why half their projects stall. Hoover just uses both, depending on the book.

The No-Formula Formula

Hoover's stated approach to process is, essentially, that she doesn't have one. She's said in interviews: "My only formula is to not follow a formula. Every single book is a completely different writing experience." She's written books with no outline at all, discovering the story sentence by sentence. She's also posted a rough outline on her wall and worked from it. Ugly Love was written out of sequence, with most of Miles's chapters drafted first and Tate's chapters filled in afterward. The process shifts to fit the book rather than the other way around.

She also doesn't set word count goals or deadlines for herself. She goes months without writing a word. Then she gets motivated and writes two books in a row. This sounds chaotic, but I think it's actually a sophisticated position. What she's protecting is the emotional engine behind the work. Her rule is that writing should never feel like a job, and the moment it starts feeling like one, the work degrades. Forcing output when the motivation isn't there produces pages she'll have to throw away. Waiting until the compulsion returns produces pages she keeps.

The edit-as-you-go method is worth noting too. She writes roughly 5,000 words, then spends the next session refining those before moving on. Some writers would call this a trap, arguing that editing during drafting slows the forward momentum. For Hoover it seems to function as a warm-up, a way of re-entering the story's world before pushing it further. The chapter she finished yesterday becomes the on-ramp to the chapter she's writing today.

Writing What You Need to Read

The most important thing Hoover has said about her practice is also the simplest: write the book you want to read. She's said this in interviews going back to her early career, and it's been repeated so many times it risks losing its meaning. The substance underneath it is worth taking seriously.

When she started writing Slammed in 2011, she was writing a book she wanted to exist, with no thought for the romance market or New Adult fiction trends. When she wrote It Ends with Us, the most personal novel in her catalog, she was working from something even more specific: a story about her mother's experience with domestic violence that had never been told properly in fiction and that she felt she owed the world. Her author's note in that book describes the weight of that obligation plainly. She wrote it because no one else was going to, and because she'd been carrying the material for years without knowing what to do with it.

This is the same logic Toni Morrison used to write The Bluest Eye, and the same logic Raymond Carver used to write about working-class drinkers in Pacific Northwest taverns. The writers who produce the most durable work tend to be answering a felt need rather than filling a market gap. Hoover's output has been enormous and some of it is lighter than the best of it, but the books that hit hardest, the ones that generate the kind of reading-at-3 AM devotion her fans describe, are the ones she wrote because she had no choice.

The CoHo Effect and What It Did to the Writing

By 2022, Colleen Hoover was one of the most-sold authors in the world. Videos with the hashtags #ColleenHoover, #CoHo, and #ColleenHooverBooks had nearly two billion views on TikTok. Her fans, who call themselves CoHorts, function as an organic marketing force, sharing quotes, reactions, castings, and recommendations at a scale publishers can't manufacture. In 2020, before BookTok, she sold 237,000 units. Two years later that number was 8.6 million.

What that kind of attention does to a writer's process is worth thinking about. Hoover talked to Texas Monthly in 2024 about the writing freeze she'd been in since moving to a new house in April 2023. She'd been producing two books a year since 2012. Then she stopped, for the first time. She told the magazine she'd reached a point where she was writing for the people waiting to make negative videos about her books, and that awareness was getting between her and the work. The audience she'd spent a decade building had become, in some sense, a presence in the room.

She described wanting to get back to why she started, which is the borrowed laptop and the Christmas gift for her mother. That was a practical diagnosis of the problem, not a retreat into nostalgia. The writing had always run on internal motivation, on writing what she wanted to read. The moment external attention crowded that out, the engine stalled. Recognizing the cause is the first step toward fixing it, and Hoover has been public about working to get back there.

Colleen Hoover's Evening Writing Routine

The Colleen Hoover evening writing routine developed out of necessity, the same way most durable writing habits do. Her mornings belong to her family. The quiet hours that are actually hers start after dinner, when the house settles and no one needs anything, usually somewhere around nine at night. She works until midnight or later, in silence, on a laptop. She has said the silence is non-negotiable. Even a clock ticking in the room is enough to pull her out of the work, and she'll remove the batteries rather than leave it running.

The music question is interesting. Hoover is visibly connected to music in her books, including companion Spotify playlists for several titles, and she listens to songs that match a story's emotional register while she's building the book in her head. But the actual writing happens in silence. The playlist is pre-work, a way of inhabiting the characters and their world before she opens the document. Once she's in, the music stops. What she needs isn't atmosphere. She needs the ability to hear the characters talking.

When the evening pace isn't moving fast enough or she's behind on a deadline, the hotel sprint kicks in. She goes to a hotel alone, buys fresh socks, orders food in, and writes. Days, sometimes a week or two. No domestic logistics, no context-switching between the book and the rest of her life. The hotel room collapses the distance between her and the work. It's the same principle as Proust's cork-lined room or Kafka's late nights, the removal of everything that competes with the sentence in front of you. Hoover's version just involves a Hampton Inn and new athletic socks, which is more honest about what the thing actually requires.

What You Can Steal

Hoover's approach is useful for writers who work irregular hours, have family obligations, or find that conventional discipline frameworks produce anxiety without output. Here's what actually transfers:

  • Find your quiet window, wherever it falls. For Hoover it's evenings, not mornings. The important thing is that the time you choose is genuinely yours, whenever that falls, with the noise off and no one asking anything of you.
  • Keep a two-mode practice. Slow and steady at home works for some books and some seasons. For others, you need total immersion. Give yourself permission to use both, and let the book tell you which one it needs.
  • Write what you want to read. This sounds like a slogan but it's a diagnostic tool. If you're not sure what to write next, ask what book you're missing from your own shelf. The motivation that question generates tends to outlast any external deadline.
  • Build a small pre-writing ritual. Hoover's socks are absurd, which is exactly why they work. The specificity of the ritual signals to her brain that writing time has started. Pick something concrete, repeatable, and slightly ridiculous. The more specific, the better the signal.
  • Let the silence be the tool. Music for mood before you open the document. Silence for the actual sentence. If you've been writing with noise in the background and struggling to stay in the scene, try removing it entirely. The clock on the wall counts.

Frequently Asked Questions

When does Colleen Hoover write?

Hoover writes in the evenings, after her family obligations are done, typically from around 9 PM to midnight. She has also described intensive hotel writing stints where she checks in alone for a week or two and writes until a book is finished. She has no fixed early-morning routine and has said she doesn't follow a set schedule.

Does Colleen Hoover use an outline?

She doesn't follow a fixed process. Her stated approach is that her only formula is to not follow a formula. She has written some books with no outline at all, discovering the story sentence by sentence, and others where she posted a rough outline on her wall. The process changes with every book.

How did Colleen Hoover write her first book?

Hoover wrote Slammed in 2011 on a borrowed laptop while her son was at play practice. She was living in a mobile home in East Texas, working as a social worker, and had no intention of getting published. She self-published it in January 2012 as a Christmas gift for her mother, who had just received a Kindle.

What is Colleen Hoover's writing ritual?

Hoover is known for her new-socks ritual. When she knows she has a long writing day ahead, she puts on a brand-new pair of socks. She has said she buys multiple packs before a hotel writing sprint. She also requires complete silence to write and listens to music only before or between sessions, not during.
