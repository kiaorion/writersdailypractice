What Is Dan Brown's Writing Routine?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Dan Brown is at his desk before most people's alarms go off. He writes seven days a week, keeps the sessions long, and interrupts them on a fixed rhythm to stay sharp. The shape of his day has held remarkably steady across the years he's spent turning out bestsellers.

The Direct Answer
He's at his desk by 4 a.m., seven days a week, and writes for six to nine hours.
He goes to bed around 10 p.m. and starts the next day in pre-dawn quiet, per his 2017 Guardian interview about his craft.

The 4 A.M. Desk

Brown wakes at 4 a.m. after going to bed around 10 p.m., and he treats every day the same. Weekends and holidays included, 365 days a year. He's talked about wanting the shortest possible trip from the sleep state to the page, partly because at that hour nobody is sending him email. Those pre-dawn hours hand him a long stretch of quiet before the rest of the world wakes up and starts competing for his attention.

The consistency matters to him more than any single morning's output. Starting at the same hour every day removes the daily argument over whether to write, which is where plenty of writers lose the fight before they sit down. He's compared writing to practicing a musical instrument, something you keep sharp through steady repetition rather than the occasional burst of inspiration.

Before he starts, he fuels up. A bulletproof coffee, which is black coffee blended with butter and coconut oil, plus a green smoothie loaded with spinach, blueberries, banana, and a stack of seeds. Then he settles in. Some mornings are pure drafting. Others go to reworking the previous day's pages or outlining what comes next, depending on what the book needs and how much energy he has that day.

I'll work for between six to nine hours depending on how much energy I have. A lot of it is editing what I wrote the day before.

Dan Brown, The Guardian, 2017

The Hourly Break

Brown doesn't grind straight through. About once an hour he stops to do push-ups, sit-ups, and stretches, then goes back to the page. Early in his career a small hourglass on his desk marked the interval. Later he rigged his computer to freeze for 60 seconds every hour, which forces the pause whether he feels like taking it or not. He tends to wrap the writing around midday, having spent his freshest hours on the book.

The breaks do double duty. Getting the blood moving clears his head and, by his own account, helps him think through problems in the manuscript. He'll come back to a scene after a set of push-ups and read it differently than he did a few minutes earlier. What You Can Steal

  • Claim the quiet hours. An early start buys focus before the day's noise begins.
  • Show up daily. Seven days a week keeps the story warm and the habit automatic.
  • Break on a timer. A short stretch about once an hour resets attention better than pushing through.
  • Bank your best hours early. Front-load the hard work while your mind is freshest.
