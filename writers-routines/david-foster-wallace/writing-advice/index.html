David Foster Wallace's Writing Advice

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Wallace taught creative writing for most of his career, first at Illinois State and later at Pomona College, and his advice to students was less about inspiration than attention. He wanted them to notice how good prose is actually built.

The Direct Answer
Read closely, revise hard, and keep a usage dictionary handy.
Drawn from his teaching years and his interviews on language and craft.

Pay Attention to the Sentence

Wallace's first piece of advice was to read like a writer. That meant more than reading widely, though he did plenty of that. It meant watching how a sentence gets built and how its clauses join. He thought most readers skate right past that machinery, and that learning to see it was half the job.

His syllabi backed this up. They're demanding documents, heavy on grammar and usage, and students kept them, which is why several are online today. He held prose to a standard, and he was open about wanting his own writing to serve the reader rather than the writer's ego.

Keep a Usage Dictionary Close

Wallace was a real word collector. He kept a running file of new words and circled unfamiliar ones in his American Heritage Dictionary, a copy that now sits in his archive with hundreds of words marked. He pushed his students toward reference books, and toward a good dictionary of usage above all.

A usage dictionary is one of the great bathroom books of all time.

David Foster Wallace, Quack This Way

What You Can Steal

  • Study sentences you admire. Take one apart and work out why it lands.
  • Keep a running word list. Wallace did, and it fed straight into his prose.
  • Buy a usage dictionary. He called it essential and made his students get one.
  • Write for the reader. Ask whether a line helps them follow you or just flatters you.
