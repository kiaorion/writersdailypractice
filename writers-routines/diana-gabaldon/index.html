Diana Gabaldon's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 23, 2026 | 9 min read

When Diana Gabaldon sat down to write her first novel in 1988, she had no intention of publishing it. She was a research scientist and university professor, she'd never written fiction before, and the manuscript was explicitly practice, a way to teach herself whether she could do the thing at all. The resulting book, Outlander, sold in 1991 and launched one of the biggest historical fiction series of the last thirty years. The lesson there is uncomfortable if you spend a lot of time worrying about whether your work is good enough to share.

What makes Gabaldon's routine genuinely strange, and worth studying, is that it doesn't look like any other major novelist's process. She doesn't outline. She doesn't write in chronological order. She writes discrete scenes wherever the creative energy happens to land on a given day, then eventually assembles all those fragments into something that reads as if it were planned from the start. The Outlander books run to 800-plus pages each. All of them were built this way.

Profile

The Routine at a Glance

Wake Time
Gabaldon is a self-described night owl who writes late into the night rather than early in the morning. Her most productive sessions run from evening into the small hours.
Writing Location
Her home office in Scottsdale, Arizona, surrounded by an extensive personal research library. She describes the space as necessary for the heavy reference work her books require.
Daily Output
Variable by design. She writes in scenes, not to a word-count quota, and a single productive session might produce one tight scene or several. The goal is to complete a unit of story energy, not to hit a number.
Tools
Computer. She transitioned from early word processors and has discussed her process extensively on her own website, dianagabaldon.com, where she documents her method in detail.
Famous Ritual
Writing scenes out of chronological order, following the creative energy to whatever scene feels ready, then stitching the pieces together later. She has described this as working in "chunks."
Books Written This Way
Outlander, Dragonfly in Amber, Voyager, Drums of Autumn, The Fiery Cross, A Breath of Snow and Ashes, An Echo in the Bone, Written in My Own Heart's Blood, Go Tell the Bees That I Am Gone.

The Scientist Who Became a Novelist

Before she wrote a single word of fiction, Gabaldon had a PhD in quantitative behavioral ecology from Northern Arizona University and had spent years as a professor at Arizona State, where she edited a scientific journal and taught research methods. She came to novel-writing with the habits of a scientist, which meant she was used to building conclusions from accumulated evidence, comfortable with large and messy data sets, and patient about letting complexity resolve over time. Those aren't the usual novelist's tools, but they're exactly what you need to write a book like Outlander.

The scientific background shows up most clearly in her research process. Gabaldon doesn't gesturally sketch in the historical details and move on. She goes deep, reading primary sources, corresponding with historians and medical professionals, acquiring specialist books on 18th-century Scottish clan society, Jacobite military strategy, period medicine, and whatever else a given scene demands. The Outlander books have a specific texture of factual density that separates them from most historical romance and adventure fiction. Readers who love the series often cite the feeling that the history is real because, in Gabaldon's hands, it mostly is.

She's talked about her academic background as both an advantage and a complication. On the advantage side, she knew how to research systematically and wasn't intimidated by primary sources. She also had no idea what was supposed to be "hard" about novel writing, which meant she didn't waste time being afraid of the things that beginners are usually told to be afraid of. The complication was that scientists are trained to proceed in a certain direction. You form a hypothesis, you design an experiment, you follow the data. Fiction writing, in Gabaldon's hands, turned out to require something almost opposite: following the energy wherever it went, trusting the chaos, resisting the urge to impose structure prematurely. That took some unlearning.

No Outline, No Order

Gabaldon has been consistent about this for thirty years: she doesn't outline, and she doesn't write from beginning to end. On her website, she's explained the process in more detail than most writers ever bother to share. She starts wherever she finds a scene that feels ready to be written, even if it belongs in the middle of a book she hasn't started yet. She might write a confrontation scene before she knows how the characters got there, or an ending before she knows what the ending means. The pieces accumulate over months or years, and eventually they reach a kind of critical mass where she can see the connections between them. Only then does she start assembling them into a narrative sequence.

This is a genuinely difficult method to explain because it runs against almost everything beginners are told about structure. Plot outlines, three-act frameworks, chapter-by-chapter breakdowns, all of it assumes you know where you're going before you start moving. Gabaldon's position is that she doesn't need to know where she's going, because the characters will show her if she follows them closely enough. She describes it as trusting the story to know its own shape. The writer's job is to write what's there and let the architecture emerge.

The practical effect of this on the Outlander books is visible once you know to look for it. The series has a peculiar structural ambition, where individual scenes carry their full emotional weight regardless of where they fall in the plot sequence, because they were written when the emotional energy was right, not slotted in to fulfill a structural requirement. Dragonfly in Amber, the second book in the series, famously opens at a point in the timeline that is years after its own ending, then loops back to fill in what happened. That's an unusual structural choice for a commercial novel. It also makes complete sense if you know it was written by someone who regularly starts in the middle and figures out the sequence later.

The argument against outlining, as Gabaldon makes it, is that a fixed outline becomes its own kind of trap. Once you've committed to a structure on paper, you're accountable to it, and you'll spend energy defending the plan rather than following the story. The characters will try to go somewhere and you'll redirect them because your outline says the scene has to go a different way. She thinks this is where a lot of genre fiction goes wrong: competent execution of a predetermined structure, with all the life drained out by the execution. Writing scene by scene, following the energy, produces work that reads as if the characters have genuine interiority because they were never forced to behave according to a plan they didn't make.

Heavy Historical Research

The Outlander books are set primarily in 18th-century Scotland, with excursions into colonial America, France, the Caribbean, and several other periods and places. Maintaining the historical accuracy across nine novels, each of 800 to 1,500 pages, requires a research infrastructure most novelists don't attempt. Gabaldon has described keeping extensive notes files, bibliographies, and research documents for each book, and she's said that the research and the writing happen in parallel rather than in sequence. She doesn't finish researching before she starts writing. She researches whatever the next scene requires.

This is another place where the scientific training shows up. A research scientist doesn't study everything about a topic before forming a hypothesis. You form a hypothesis, then go look for the specific evidence it needs. Gabaldon applies the same logic to fiction: she writes toward a scene, and when the scene requires something she doesn't know, she goes and finds it. The research is driven by the writing rather than the other way around. This keeps it from becoming a form of productive procrastination, which is what historical research often becomes for writers who haven't figured out how to stop.

Her personal library has grown substantially over the decades. She's mentioned, in interviews and on her website, acquiring specialist texts on period surgery, military history, 18th-century Scottish law, and the details of Jacobite clan politics. For the American colonial sections of later books, she added material on Cherokee culture, period medicine in the colonies, and early American frontier life. The books carry all of that, but lightly. The research is the substrate. Readers feel it as authority rather than seeing it as data.

She started Outlander as private practice, with no audience and no deadline. That freedom produced something she'd never have written if she'd been trying to meet a market.

Starting as Private Practice

The origin story of Outlander matters for writers at every level. In 1988, Gabaldon decided to teach herself to write fiction. She chose to set the practice novel in 18th-century Scotland, partly because she'd recently seen a character from that era in a television rerun and found the period interesting, and partly because setting it somewhere nobody around her could fact-check felt safer for a first attempt. She wrote with no intention of showing anyone, no agent in mind, no target genre, no concept of audience. She was a scientist running an experiment on herself.

The scene she wrote first was not the opening of Outlander. She's said in multiple interviews that the first scene she completed, which she knew was good, was a scene set in a Scottish castle in the middle of the book. She wrote it because it interested her. She built outward from there, adding scenes as they came, with no particular plan for how they'd fit together. Several years and hundreds of thousands of words later, she had a manuscript that agents were competing to represent.

The private practice condition was probably essential. When you're writing for no one, you write what you actually want to write. There's no imagined reader to perform for, no genre conventions to hit, no commercial logic to apply. You follow your own curiosity, which is where the most interesting work tends to come from. Gabaldon has said she wouldn't have written the book the way she did if she'd known it was going to be read. She'd have been too careful, too conscious of doing it right. The freedom of private practice let her do something weirder and more personal than a calculated debut would have allowed.

This is transferable. The practice novel, the draft no one will see, the writing that has no audience, is a specific kind of productive space that most writers don't give themselves. The impulse to share too early collapses that space. Gabaldon kept hers for long enough that Outlander got to be fully itself before it had to be anything for anybody else.

Diana Gabaldon's Writing Routine in Practice

Gabaldon's night-owl schedule means her best writing hours are the ones most writers have already given up on. She's talked about writing late, after the obligations of the day have cleared, when the house is quiet and there's no reason to stop. This suits the chunk-based method. A single scene can run to several thousand words, and writing one requires getting into a focused state that's hard to reach in small windows between other commitments. The late hours give her longer uninterrupted stretches.

She has described her process on her website, dianagabaldon.com, in a piece titled "How I Write" that runs to considerable length and is one of the more honest accounts of a working novelist's method available anywhere. She's detailed about the chunk method, about following the energy, about the research workflow. The transparency is unusual. Most novelists with her level of commercial success talk in generalities. Gabaldon walks through the specifics because she thinks the specifics are what's actually useful.

Between Outlander books she writes novellas set in the same world, a Lord John Grey series, and various short stories. She doesn't take long gaps between projects. The writing is continuous rather than project-by-project, and the same chunk-based method applies across all of it. Any given day might see her working on three different manuscripts at once, writing whichever scene in whichever book is ready to be written. That would produce paralysis for most writers. For a novelist trained in scientific research management, it's just handling multiple data sets in parallel.

Sources

Gabaldon is unusually transparent about her process and has documented it herself. The primary source is her own website, dianagabaldon.com, where she has published extended essays on her writing method, including her explanation of the chunk-based, non-chronological approach. Her Writer's Digest interview covers the no-outline method and her background as a research scientist. Various recorded talks and convention panels, including her appearances at literary festivals and writing conferences, expand on these themes. For biographical context on her academic career before fiction, see her faculty profile history at Arizona State University, where she taught for several years before the Outlander series made full-time writing possible.

What You Can Steal

Gabaldon's method is harder to copy directly than, say, Hemingway's word-count system, because it depends on a tolerance for uncertainty that most writers have to build up slowly. But there are specific ideas worth taking immediately:

  • Write the scene that's ready, not the scene that comes next in the outline. If you have energy for chapter twelve and you're stuck on chapter four, write chapter twelve. The stuck chapter will often resolve itself once you know where the story goes.
  • Let the research follow the writing, not precede it. Write toward the scene you don't know enough to write yet, then go find what it needs. Research driven by a specific question is faster and more focused than general research conducted in advance.
  • Give yourself a private practice novel. One manuscript with no audience, no deadline, no intended genre. See what you write when you're not trying to please anyone. That's often where your real material is.
  • Stop defending your outline. If you outline heavily and the story keeps trying to go somewhere else, consider whether the outline is helping the story or managing your anxiety about the story. Those are different jobs.
  • Your background is not a liability. Gabaldon's scientific training made her a better historical novelist. Whatever expertise you're carrying from a previous career or education is material. Use it.
