How Does Don DeLillo Write?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Don DeLillo writes the way a sculptor works, one small block at a time. He types on a manual typewriter and puts a single paragraph on each page, so every set of sentences sits alone where he can see it clearly. The method sounds fussy. For him it's the whole point.

The Direct Answer
He types on a manual typewriter and puts one paragraph on every page.
DeLillo adopted the one-paragraph-per-page habit while writing The Names, so the surrounding white space would let him see each passage on its own, per his 1993 Paris Review interview.

One Paragraph, One Page

The habit crystallized when DeLillo was writing The Names in the early 1980s. He started pulling each finished paragraph onto its own sheet, even a paragraph only three lines long, then beginning the next one on a clean page. All that surrounding white space did something for him. He could look at a single set of sentences on its own, weigh the rhythm, and rework it without the pull of the passages above and below. The blank margins weren't decoration. They were a tool for concentration, and they made his rewriting sharper because nothing else on the page competed for his eye.

When I finished a paragraph, even a three-line paragraph, I automatically went to a fresh page to start the new paragraph. No crowded pages.

Don DeLillo, Paris Review, 'The Art of Fiction No. 135' (1993)

For DeLillo this is bound up with a larger idea, that writing is a concentrated form of thinking, and that he often doesn't know what he thinks about something until he's tried to put it into sentences. Isolating each paragraph gives that thinking room to happen. The page becomes a place to test an idea rather than a container to fill.

Why the Typewriter

DeLillo has typed on a manual machine for his whole career. When his old Royal started to fail in the mid-1970s, he bought a secondhand Olympia and kept it. The Olympia prints slightly bigger letters, which lets him see each word clearly as it lands on the page. He likes watching the letters come off the hammers already finished and printed, formed like small physical objects rather than loose handwriting.

That's tied to how he thinks about language. DeLillo has said the words typed on a white page have a sculptural quality, and that they match up through sound and look as much as through meaning. He builds a book sentence by sentence and listens for the rhythm that carries him through each one. For the wider picture, see the full Don DeLillo writing routine.

What You Can Steal

  • Give each idea room. One paragraph per page forces you to look at your sentences in isolation, where weak spots are easier to catch.
  • Pick a tool you can see clearly on. DeLillo chose a typewriter with bigger type so the words stayed legible and physical.
  • Treat rewriting as the main event. The whole method exists to make revision easier, not to protect a clean first draft.
  • Read for sound, not only sense. He tunes each sentence by ear, listening to how the words fall.
