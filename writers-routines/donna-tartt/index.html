Donna Tartt's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 1, 2026 | 9 min read

Donna Tartt has published three novels in thirty years. The Secret History came out in 1992, when she was twenty-eight. The Little Friend arrived a decade later in 2002. The Goldfinch, which won the Pulitzer Prize in 2014, came out in 2013. The gaps are long enough that they attract commentary of their own, as though a novelist who takes a decade between books must be doing something wrong, or suffering, or holding back. She hasn't said much to correct that impression, because she hasn't said much about her process at all. Of all the serious literary novelists working in English today, Tartt is among the most private about how she works. That silence is itself a piece of information.

What we do know is specific enough to build something from. She writes first drafts in longhand. She has spoken, in rare interviews, about hating the computer and what typing does to the feel of her sentences. She works without a rigid daily schedule or a word count target. The books take as long as they take, and she won't be hurried. If you're a writer who struggles with pace, with the gap between the book you're imagining and the pages you're producing, Tartt's routine is worth studying, because she has built a practice that treats time as a material rather than a constraint.

The Ten-Year Novel

The obvious question about Donna Tartt's process is the one everyone asks, which is why it takes ten years. The less obvious answer is that the question contains a category error. Ten years is how long the books take. Asking why is a bit like asking why a particular river takes the route it takes. The shape of the thing is the answer.

Tartt has said, in different ways across different interviews, that her novels are complete in her mind before she finishes writing them, and that the writing is the process of getting what she already knows onto the page. That sounds like it should be faster. In practice it means the opposite, because a writer who already knows the full shape of the book has a higher internal standard for each sentence. Every page has to hold up against the whole. A writer who doesn't know where the story's going can get away with rougher drafts. Tartt can't, because she already knows exactly how good the finished thing needs to be.

Compare this to a novelist who works by discovery, following the characters and finding the plot as they go. That writer produces pages faster because each page is provisional, a search rather than a construction. Tartt's pages aren't provisional. They're accumulations toward a vision she's already carrying. The pace slows accordingly, but the books arrive with an unusual internal coherence that reflects the way they were made. The Secret History is told almost entirely in retrospect, with the killer revealed on the first page. The whole novel depends on structural control that a discovery writer couldn't maintain across eight hundred pages. The ten-year timeline is what that control costs.

Writing by Hand

Tartt's commitment to longhand drafts is the most documented specific detail of her process, and it's worth thinking about what it actually does to the work. She has said she hates the computer, and the language she uses around this isn't aesthetic preference; it's about the physical sensation of writing and what that sensation produces in the sentence.

When you write by hand, you can't go back easily. Deleting a sentence in a word processor takes one keystroke. Crossing it out on paper means it's still there on the page, still visible, still part of the record of your thinking. Writers who work in longhand develop a different relationship to their own sentences because every word costs something to produce. The hand slows the mind down just enough to make the sentence more deliberate before it exists.

Tartt's sentences support this. Her prose in all three novels is dense, layered, full of subordinate clauses that build on each other. The syntax has the quality of someone who thought carefully before committing. Hemingway's short declarative sentences came partly from his habit of standing while he wrote, which kept the prose quick and muscular. Tartt's long, ornate sentences came partly from her habit of writing by hand, which gave each clause time to develop before she moved to the next. The tools shape the text. This is one of the most consistent findings across any serious study of writers' routines.

There's also the question of revision. A longhand draft, typed up later, gets a second reading built into the transcription process. Every sentence gets seen twice: once when it's written and once when it's typed. A writer who composes directly on a computer skips that intermediate pass. Tartt's work goes through it by default, which means her drafted pages arrive at the typed stage already improved by the act of copying. The slowness is doing something.

Privacy as a Working Condition

Tartt's reluctance to talk about her process isn't shyness. It reads more like a deliberate protection of the conditions she needs to work. Writers who describe their routines in detail are, to some degree, converting private experience into public material. That conversion has a cost. The routine becomes a story you tell about yourself, and stories about yourself have a way of replacing the thing they describe.

There are writers who talk constantly about their process and produce a lot of books that feel slightly hollow at the center, as though the talking ate the writing. And there are writers who keep almost entirely quiet about how they work, and whose books feel like they came from somewhere private and inaccessible, which is exactly how they feel to read. Tartt belongs to the second group. The privacy and the quality are probably connected.

Her editor at Knopf was Sonny Mehta, one of the most respected editors in American publishing, who worked with her across all three novels until his death in 2019. Mehta was known for his patience with writers who needed long timelines, and for his ability to hold a relationship with a novelist across a decade between books without putting commercial pressure on the work. That kind of editorial partnership is rare and matters more than it sounds. A writer working at Tartt's pace, on a project of that scope, needs someone in her corner who won't start counting the years out loud. Mehta was that person for three decades.

The Bennington Years and What Formed Her

Tartt grew up in Grenada, Mississippi, and attended the University of Mississippi before transferring to Bennington College in Vermont in the early 1980s. She arrived at Bennington when it had one of the most unusual literary atmospheres of any American college: a small community of writers, intensive feedback, and an expectation that serious fiction was worth taking seriously at twenty years old. She started working on The Secret History while she was a student there, in her early twenties.

Bret Easton Ellis was a classmate. Her teachers included the novelist Bret Lott and various visiting faculty who came through Bennington during those years. The milieu produced writers who thought carefully about structure and voice, which you can see in The Secret History's deeply considered architecture. The book starts with a confession and then spends five hundred pages earning it. That's a formal decision that a twenty-something writer doesn't make casually. It suggests someone who had been thinking about how novels work for a long time before she finished the first one.

What Bennington gave her, beyond the education, was the experience of being taken seriously early. She didn't spend years writing in obscurity and then get discovered. Her first novel was a massive commercial success, published when she was twenty-eight. The years since then, the decades between books, have been lived in the shadow of that arrival. It would have been easy to produce follow-up books faster and coast on the early reputation. She didn't. The pace of her output suggests someone more interested in the quality of the individual book than in maintaining visibility. That's a harder position to hold over thirty years than it sounds.

What the Silence Tells You

The most useful thing about studying Donna Tartt's routine is what it doesn't contain. There's no described morning ritual. No stated word count. No documented writing location. No published account of her daily schedule. The books exist, three of them, each enormous and carefully made, and the working life that produced them is almost entirely undocumented. For a writer trying to learn from her example, that absence is instructive in a specific way.

Most writing advice comes packaged as routine: write at the same time every day, hit a word count, protect your morning hours. That advice works for a lot of writers, and the pages on this site document dozens of writers who built their careers on exactly that kind of structured daily practice. Tartt's example suggests a different model, where the structure is internal rather than external. She doesn't appear to need a fixed hour or a fixed desk. What she has is a standard for the work and a willingness to give the work however much time it needs, which turns out to be about a decade per book.

The risk of that model is obvious: it can become an excuse for not finishing. A writer who says the book will take as long as it takes can hide behind that indefinitely. Tartt's model works because she actually finishes, and the finished books are actually good, which means the patience is genuine rather than avoidant. That distinction matters. The ten years are productive years, years of accumulation and revision and returning to the manuscript, not years of nothing punctuated by occasional guilt. You can tell from the books, which are too dense and too controlled to have been written in a final sprint.

Donna Tartt's Writing Process

Donna Tartt's writing process, as much as it can be described from what she's shared publicly, centers on three things: longhand drafts, deep structural planning before the writing begins, and a timeline measured in years rather than months. She has never described the process in the detail that some writers use to explain themselves, in part because she seems to regard the process as something that belongs to the work and shouldn't be separated from it by explanation.

The longhand drafts are the most concrete piece of the process. Tartt has said in various interviews, most notably a 2013 Vanity Fair profile published around the release of The Goldfinch, that she writes her first drafts by hand and that the physical act of writing is inseparable from the quality of what she produces. She has described disliking what happens to her sentences when she writes directly on a keyboard, a flattening of something she can only feel and not easily describe. The notebooks accumulate over years, and the typed draft that emerges from them is already a revision of what was written by hand.

Her published work shows the results of this process across three books separated by a decade each. The Secret History, The Little Friend, and The Goldfinch are all long, structurally ambitious novels with prose that has been worked at the sentence level to a degree you don't often see in contemporary literary fiction. Each book is different in tone and setting, which suggests the long timeline serves the individual book's needs rather than following a template. The decade is whatever that particular novel required.

Sources

Primary sources for Tartt's process are scarce by design. The most detailed account of her working life appears in the September 2013 Vanity Fair profile by James Wolcott, published around the release of The Goldfinch, which includes her comments on longhand drafts and her aversion to computers. Additional interview material from her 1992 book tour for The Secret History captures her early statements about the novel's ten-year composition at Bennington and afterward. Francine Prose's introduction to Tartt's work in various retrospectives touches on the formal ambitions visible in the finished books. See also Lesley McDowell's profile in The Guardian and the brief interviews collected in the Vintage paperback editions of her novels. Her own comments on painting, art, and the way a work of art reveals different things at different stages of life, collected across several interviews, give a useful picture of how she thinks about longevity and craft. What You Can Steal

Tartt's process is hardest to directly imitate because it depends on a patience that most writers have to cultivate deliberately. Here's what actually transfers:

  • Write the first draft by hand. The physical act of writing slows down your sentence construction in a way that improves it. Try it for a chapter and compare what you produce to your keyboard drafts. The difference is usually noticeable.
  • Let the book tell you how long it needs. Not as an excuse to stall, but as a genuine permission structure. If the book is taking longer than you planned, ask whether the timeline is the problem or whether the book is more complex than you first understood.
  • Protect your process from explanation. Every time you describe your routine in detail, you convert private experience into a story about yourself. That story can gradually replace the experience. Some writers talk too much about writing and too little of it as a result.
  • Build the whole before you write the parts. Tartt appears to know the shape of her novels before she finishes them. That structural clarity is what allows a long, layered book to cohere across years of drafting. If you're working on something ambitious, invest the time in understanding its architecture before you commit to the prose.
  • Find an editor who will hold your timeline without counting the years. The partnership between Tartt and Sonny Mehta lasted thirty years because Mehta understood what her books needed. The right editorial relationship is worth more than any daily routine.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does Donna Tartt take to write a novel?

Donna Tartt publishes a novel roughly every ten years. The Secret History came out in 1992, The Little Friend in 2002, and The Goldfinch in 2013. She has said the novels take as long as they take, and she won't rush them to meet a schedule.

Does Donna Tartt write by hand?

Yes. Tartt has said she writes her first drafts in longhand, preferring the physical connection between pen and paper to the distance she feels at a keyboard. She has described hating the computer and what it does to the texture of her sentences. The handwritten drafts accumulate over years before she moves to typed revision.

Where does Donna Tartt write?

Tartt splits her time between New York and Europe and has never described a single fixed writing location in any documented interview. Her extreme privacy about her process means the specifics of her working space aren't publicly known.

Why does Donna Tartt take so long to write her novels?

Tartt has compared writing a novel to giving birth: slow, consuming, and impossible to hurry. She has also suggested that her books take the time they need to be complete, and that no external pressure changes that. The ten-year gaps reflect her process, not avoidance. The finished books, dense and architecturally ambitious, show the result of that patience.
