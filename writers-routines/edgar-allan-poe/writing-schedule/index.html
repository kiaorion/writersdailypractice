When Did Edgar Allan Poe Write?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

This is the page that can't hand you a wake time. Poe left almost no record of an ordinary writing day, and his most careful biographers have said so plainly. What the record does show is a working life organized around magazine deadlines, constant moves, and money that was almost never there.

The Direct Answer
No reliable daily schedule survives. He wrote around magazine deadlines and relentless poverty.
Kenneth Silverman's landmark biography found almost no direct testimony about Poe's day to day writing hours.

A Life Too Unstable for a Routine

Poe moved constantly through the 1830s and 1840s, from Baltimore to Richmond, then to Philadelphia, then to New York. When the money ran out, he moved. When family members died, he moved again. He lived in rented rooms and boarding houses, and in his final years in a small cottage at Fordham, now part of the Bronx, with his wife Virginia and her mother, Maria Clemm. A fixed daily schedule is almost impossible to reconstruct from a life that unsettled, and honest biographers refuse to invent one. Any precise wake time you see attached to Poe is somebody's guess dressed up as fact.

The instability wasn't a phase he grew out of. It was the shape of the whole career. He wrote through his wife's long illness and early death, through bouts of his own poor health, and through stretches of genuine hunger. The wonder is not that he kept no tidy routine. It is how much finished work he produced without one.

To coin one's brain into silver, at the nod of a master, is to my thinking, the hardest task in the world.

Edgar Allan Poe, letter to F. W. Thomas, 1841

The Deadline Was the Schedule

If Poe kept to any rhythm, it was the rhythm of the magazine trade. He earned his living as an editor and reviewer, first at the Southern Literary Messenger in Richmond in the mid 1830s, then at Burton's and Graham's in Philadelphia, and later at the Broadway Journal in New York, which he briefly owned outright. Much of his fiction and criticism was written on commission and against a printer's deadline. His writing hours bent to whatever the next issue demanded rather than to any clock he set himself.

The Raven appeared in January 1845 and made him famous. It didn't make him solvent. Across roughly fourteen years as a working writer, biographers note that he cleared the poverty line only once, during his time at Graham's. That pressure, more than any morning ritual, decided when and how much he wrote. He worked because the rent was due and the next piece was owed.

What You Can Steal

  • A routine is a luxury. Poe produced lasting work with none of the stability that writing advice tends to assume.
  • Let deadlines do your scheduling. A real due date will move a page on days when willpower won't.
  • Separate the myth from the method. You can borrow how Poe built a poem without romanticizing how he lived.
  • Guard the work from your circumstances. He wrote through grief and poverty because the writing was the one thing he could still control.
