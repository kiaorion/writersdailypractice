Emily Henry's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 1, 2026 | 9 min read

Emily Henry gets up, pours cold brew, and writes. She moves around her house every couple of hours and keeps writing. That's the routine, described in her own words in a 2021 piece for Zoella, and it's worth sitting with for a moment because of how aggressively ordinary it sounds. No 4 AM alarm, no dedicated writing cabin, no elaborate ritual. By around ten in the morning she's at her desk or on her couch, and by three in the afternoon she's done. Five hours, most days of the week, in a home office in Cincinnati, Ohio. Six novels in roughly six years, all of them bestsellers.

What makes Henry's routine worth studying has nothing to do with its surface details and everything to do with the underlying philosophy. She's built a practice around finishing things, not perfecting them. Around drafting fast and revising hard. Around writing the book she most wants to read, without waiting to know how it ends before she starts. In a genre that gets dismissed as light reading, her approach to craft is one of the most intellectually honest you'll find from any working novelist.

Profile

The Routine at a Glance

Wake Time
No fixed alarm. She writes mornings through afternoon, typically starting around 10 AM. Earlier in her career, when she held a day job, she wrote starting at 5 AM before work and through weekends.
Writing Location
At home in Cincinnati, Ohio. She's described her workspace as a standing desk with a treadmill and a guest bed nearby for breaks. She moves around the house every couple of hours while continuing to work.
Daily Output
She doesn't talk publicly about word count targets. She writes until around 3 PM most days and measures progress by finishing drafts rather than hitting daily numbers.
Tools
Cold brew coffee, which she buys in bulk so she doesn't have to make decisions in the morning. She does not listen to music while writing because she finds it too distracting, though she curates playlists for each book's mood outside of writing sessions.
Famous Ritual
Drafting quickly and without fear. Henry has said she's "not afraid to write a terrible first draft" and that finishing a messy draft is always the first step, because it's easier to fix a book than to create one from nothing.
Books Written This Way
Beach Read (2020), People We Meet on Vacation (2021), Book Lovers (2022), Happy Place (2023), Funny Story (2024), Great Big Beautiful Life (2025).

The Writer Who Doesn't Outline

Most successful novelists, when asked about their process, describe some version of planning. Notecards, beat sheets, chapter-by-chapter outlines, months of preparation before a word of prose gets written. Henry does the opposite. She's said in multiple interviews that any time she's tried writing from a thorough outline, it feels like writing a book she already read, and that's not why she shows up to the page. She writes because it feels like an intense reading experience where she doesn't know what will happen next. Take that away and the whole thing deflates.

For People We Meet on Vacation, she hadn't decided which vacations her characters would take or in what order when she started drafting. She wrote both the past and present timelines more or less simultaneously, in the order they appear in the finished book. Everything about how the relationship developed between Poppy and Alex was, as she put it, essentially a surprise. The structure of the novel looks deliberate and engineered from the outside, and it is, but that engineering happened in revision, not planning.

There's a real lesson in this. Henry's anti-outline position is a theory about where story energy comes from, not a stylistic quirk or a sign of laziness. She believes that the writer's genuine curiosity about what happens next is one of the main things a reader can feel on the page. When she knows the ending before she writes the beginning, something goes slack. The sentences know the answer before the scene does. Readers are harder to fool than writers think, and they can sense when a book is going through motions it already knows by heart.

That said, Henry is clear that she's a deliberate reviser. The fast, ugly first draft is not the end point. She's described her drafts as often being "just a bunch of dialogue and tidbits of description" that need substantial rebuilding. The discovery phase and the refinement phase are kept strictly separate, and she trusts the second one to catch everything the first one missed.

Cold Brew and a Standing Desk

The physical setup of Henry's writing life is more considered than her casual descriptions suggest. She works at a standing desk with a treadmill underneath it, in a room that doubles as a guest space, and keeps a nearby bed for breaks when the standing gets old. She's described her workspace as decidedly non-photogenic, with the usual domestic disorder of someone who lives and works in the same building: dirty dishes, dust bunnies, sweatpants. The couch is also a legitimate workstation. She moves between locations throughout the day as the mood changes.

The cold brew habit is specific and worth noting. She buys it in bulk so she doesn't have to make any decisions in the morning before she's ready to make decisions. It's a small thing, but the logic behind it is exactly the logic behind every effective writing routine: remove as many friction points as possible between waking up and getting words on the page. Decision fatigue is real, and the writers who protect their mornings from small unnecessary choices are the ones who tend to produce more consistently.

The music question is where Henry surprises people. She curates playlists for each book she writes, matching songs to the emotional tone of the project and sharing them with readers. But she doesn't play those playlists while she's actually drafting. She's said she finds music too distracting, that she ends up waiting for favorite parts instead of paying attention to the sentences. The playlists are for building the world in her head before and after sessions. During the session itself, the room is quiet.

This split, the playlist as mood tool rather than background noise, is more sophisticated than it sounds. Henry is essentially doing two different kinds of creative work and keeping them from bleeding into each other. The playlist session is immersive and receptive: she soaks up the emotional register she's trying to write toward. The drafting session is active and producing: she needs her full attention on the page. Mixing them would dilute both.

Finishing as a Practice

Henry has said something in interviews that I keep coming back to: it's always easier to fix a book than to create one from nothing. She repeats this in different forms across different conversations, and it functions as the core axiom of her entire approach. If the draft exists, there's something to work with. If you keep delaying the draft until conditions are right, you have nothing.

She drafts fast by design. The first draft of Beach Read took somewhere between three and five weeks, by her estimate. That's a pace that would make most literary novelists faint. But Henry treats a fast draft as raw material, often bad raw material, full of placeholder scenes and unresolved threads, not a finished book. Speed in the first draft is a tool for getting out of her own way before the internal critic can slow things down. The revision, which takes months, is where the novel actually gets built.

She's also talked about finishing as something she had to learn was separate from quality. For years she finished drafts she didn't care enough about to revise. She kept writing them anyway because the practice of finishing is its own skill, independent of whether any particular book is worth saving. Writers who only work on projects they feel confident about end up writing very little. The confidence comes from completing things, not the other way around.

This is a harder sell than it sounds. The instinct for most writers is to protect their best ideas from bad execution by waiting until they know what they're doing. Henry's view is that you find out what you're doing by doing it, badly, all the way to the end. The good version comes after, in revision, once you know the shape of the thing you're trying to make.

Writing for the Reader You Already Are

The question Henry gets asked most often is some version of: how do you write commercially successful books without chasing commercial trends? Her answer is consistent. She writes what she most wants to read, without reference to what's selling, and she trusts that if the need she's filling is genuine, other readers will share it.

Beach Read came out of a period when Henry was dealing with grief and found herself unable to locate books that handled big, hard feelings and were also genuinely funny. She wanted to read a novel about two writers falling in love while disagreeing about whether the world was worth celebrating. That book didn't exist. So she wrote it. The novel sold before it was finished, went into multiple printings, and has been in print continuously since 2020. None of that happened because Henry studied the market. It happened because she was honest about what she needed.

This is the piece of her approach that's hardest to teach but most worth trying to absorb. Henry describes herself on her FAQ page as someone who writes books that explore hard topics with humor and end with hope, and who believes that's what fiction is for. The commercial success is real, but it's a byproduct of craft and honesty, not a goal she was aiming at directly. She's described the experience of writing as fundamentally one of self-reflection and investigation, a way of facing down fears and working through things she can't otherwise access. The reader who benefits from that process is, first, the writer.

What You Can Steal

Henry's routine is useful for writers who work full time, who feel paralyzed by the gap between their taste and their current ability, or who keep starting projects and stopping before they're done. Here's what actually translates:

  • Reduce morning friction before the session starts. Henry buys cold brew in bulk precisely so there's one fewer decision standing between her and the desk. Whatever your version of that is, do it the night before.
  • Separate the mood-building from the drafting. Playlists, research, reading in your genre, all of that is real creative work. Keep it in a different time block from actual writing, so neither one compromises the other.
  • Finish the draft before you evaluate it. A finished bad draft is something you can fix. An abandoned draft in chapter three teaches you nothing except how to abandon drafts.
  • Write toward discovery, not toward a predetermined ending. If knowing how a book ends before you write it deflates the sentences, try leaving the ending genuinely open. The revision will sort it out.
  • Write the book you need to read. The commercial instinct is to look outward at what's selling. Henry's instinct is to look inward at what's missing. Her track record suggests the inward look is more reliable.

Frequently Asked Questions

What time does Emily Henry write?

Henry typically writes from around 10 AM to 3 PM most days. In a 2023 interview she described her schedule as "mornings through afternoon, usually from like ten to like three." Earlier in her career, before she was writing full time, she started at 5 AM before her day job and worked through both weekend days.

Does Emily Henry use music while writing?

No. She's said she finds music too distracting because she ends up waiting for favorite parts rather than paying attention to the page. She does create playlists for each book, matched to the emotional register of the project, but those are for before and after sessions rather than during them.

Where does Emily Henry write her books?

At home in Cincinnati, Ohio. She uses a standing desk with a treadmill, moves to the couch when she needs a change, and has described her workspace as decidedly unglamorous. She used to write in libraries earlier in her career but shifted to being a home writer as her schedule became more demanding.

How does Emily Henry approach outlining and drafting?

She doesn't outline in the conventional sense. She's said that writing from a thorough outline feels like writing a book she already read, and that the genuine uncertainty of not knowing what happens next is part of what keeps her engaged. She drafts fast, finishes the draft however rough it is, and does the structural work in revision.
