The Great Gatsby Word Count: How Long Is It?

Kia Orion | August 2, 2026 | 5 min read

The Great Gatsby is the standing argument against writing long. At roughly 47,094 words it's less than half the length of a standard commercial novel, and Fitzgerald got the bulk of it down in a single working season on the Riviera.

The Direct Answer
About 47,094 words.
That figure comes from digital counts of the standard text and is repeated across word count surveys. It puts The Great Gatsby below the length most publishers will now call a novel. Fitzgerald began outlining in June 1922 and delivered the manuscript to Maxwell Perkins on 27 October 1924.

Under Fifty Thousand Words

47,094 is the count given by Word Counters and by Broke by Books' survey of 175 novel lengths, and reading trackers put the book at three to four hours. Set against the current market that's a novella. Literary fiction is bought at 80,000 to 100,000 words, and 47,000 from an unknown writer gets a form rejection on the word count alone.

Fitzgerald reached that length by throwing work away. He started outlining his third novel in June 1922, wrote roughly 18,000 words by the middle of 1923, then discarded almost all of it as a false start. What survived is tight partly because he had already binned a novella's worth of the wrong book before he found the right one.

Two Years of Circling, Five Months of Writing

Serious drafting resumed in April 1924. That spring the Fitzgeralds moved to the Villa Marie at Saint-Raphael on the French Riviera, and the months there were the cleanest working period of his life. He'd be at the desk by mid-morning after a swim, work through the afternoon and into the evening, then go out. His letters to Perkins from that summer are full of progress reports and word counts. The draft was finished by the end of October, and it went to Scribner on the twenty-seventh.

Perkins came back with a structural objection rather than a line edit. Gatsby himself was too vague, and the source of his money was never explained clearly enough for a reader to hold. Fitzgerald rewrote through the winter of 1924 into 1925, working hardest on chapters six and eight, and delivered the final version in February. Scribner published on 10 April 1925.

He also turned down a $10,000 offer for the serial rights so the book could reach readers sooner. For a man who tracked every story and every dollar in a handwritten ledger from 1919 onward, that decision says more about how he rated Gatsby than anything in his letters.

How Gatsby Compares

47,000 words is a fifth of Ulysses and a twelfth of War and Peace. It's shorter than most young adult fantasy novels published this year. It is also one of the most taught and most reprinted novels in the language, which makes it the cleanest available proof that length and impact are separate variables.

What made 47,000 words enough was the discard rate and the structural rebuild. Fitzgerald wrote and dumped 18,000 words of a different novel, drafted the real one in five concentrated months, then tore into two chapters again in proof at his editor's insistence. Three passes over a short book beat one pass over a long one, and that trade is available to any writer willing to cut.

If you're benchmarking a short manuscript, the useful question is whether it's short because it's finished or short because a subplot is missing. Gatsby has one narrator, one summer and one line of action. Nothing has been left out.

What You Can Steal

  • Count what you throw away. Fitzgerald binned 18,000 words of a different third novel before Gatsby existed. The discards are part of the work.
  • A short book can carry a long idea. 47,000 words, one narrator, one summer. Compression is a structural choice you make early.
  • Protect one clean working season. Five months at the Villa Marie produced the draft. Most writers get one good stretch a year and waste it.
  • Take structural notes seriously, even in proof. Perkins said Gatsby was too vague, and Fitzgerald rebuilt two chapters after the book was set.
