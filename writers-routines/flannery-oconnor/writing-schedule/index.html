What Was Flannery O'Connor's Writing Routine?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Flannery O'Connor wrote every morning, roughly nine to noon, and treated those hours as fixed. Lupus rationed her energy, so she guarded the best part of the day for fiction and let everything else wait for the afternoon.

The Direct Answer
She wrote every morning from about 9 a.m. to noon, after Mass.
Those hours were her most reliable, before the fatigue set in, a rhythm she described in Mystery and Manners.

Three Hours, Same Time, Every Day

The shape of O'Connor's day was simple, and she almost never broke it. She'd go to early Mass in Milledgeville, drive back out to the farm, and be at her typewriter by nine. She stayed until roughly noon. Lupus, the same disease that had killed her father, had stripped most of her stamina by her mid-twenties, so the morning was the one stretch she could count on. She spent it on fiction and let nothing else in.

Ask how many hours and you get two answers, both true. The window ran about three hours, nine to noon. The amount of real writing inside it was closer to two, because that was all the energy the illness left her. She didn't pad the difference. If the work stalled, she sat with the stall rather than wandering off to do something easier.

She was honest about how little actually got written on some days. Certain mornings nothing came at all, and she didn't treat that as a failure. The job was to be in the chair when something did come, so she showed up whether or not she felt ready. She put it plainly in her talk on the craft of fiction:

Every morning between 9 and 12 I go to my room and sit before a piece of paper. Many times, I just sit for three hours with no ideas coming to me. But I know one thing. If an idea does come between 9 and 12, I am there ready for it.

Flannery O'Connor, Mystery and Manners

Mornings for Fiction, Afternoons for Everything Else

The hard border around the morning is what made the rest of the day work. Afternoons belonged to reading and to the enormous stack of letters she kept up. Her correspondence alone fills a book of more than 600 pages, The Habit of Being, and she wrote those letters with genuine care. She raised peacocks in whatever time was left. None of that touched the morning, and the chattier writing never got her sharpest hours.

There's a logic to giving fiction the front of the day. First thing, before the mail and the visitors arrived and before the fatigue hit, her mind was as clear as it would get. By afternoon she was tired, so the tired hours got the work that could survive being tired. The ranking was deliberate, and she held to it for years.

She kept this up for thirteen years, through worsening illness and eventually crutches, and it produced two novels and thirty-one stories. See the full Flannery O'Connor writing routine for how the farm and the afternoon letters fit around those mornings.

What You Can Steal

  • Give your best hours to your hardest work. O'Connor spent her sharpest three hours on fiction and let admin wait for the afternoon slump.
  • Show up on the empty days too. She sat there even when nothing came, because you can't catch the good idea if you're not at the desk when it arrives.
  • Pick a window and defend it. Same time, same place. The predictability did the work that willpower couldn't.
  • Let your limits set your schedule. She had energy for two or three hours, so she built the whole day around protecting them instead of fighting for more.
