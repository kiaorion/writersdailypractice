Fyodor Dostoevsky's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 14, 2026 | 10 min read

In October 1866, Fyodor Dostoevsky had twenty-six days to write an entire novel from scratch. His contract with the publisher Stellovsky required him to deliver a new work by November 1st, or Stellovsky would own the rights to everything Dostoevsky had ever written, and everything he would write for the next nine years, without paying him a ruble. Dostoevsky was already writing Crime and Punishment in serial installments and had no spare time in any conventional sense. His friends told him the contract was a trap he couldn't escape. He hired a stenographer, a young woman named Anna Grigoryevna Snitkina, dictated The Gambler to her in twenty-six days, delivered the manuscript on time, and then proposed to her. They married the following year.

This story gets told as a colorful anecdote, but it's also an unusually clear window into how Dostoevsky actually worked. He was always under financial pressure so severe it would have stopped most people from writing at all. His routine was built for a man in a permanent state of emergency, and it produced four of the greatest novels in any language.

Profile

The Routine at a Glance

Work Hours
Nocturnal, typically writing from late evening through the early hours of the morning. He slept during the day and came alive after dark, often working past midnight and into the small hours.
Writing Location
A writing desk at home, wherever home happened to be at the time. In his later and more stable years, his study in St. Petersburg. During the European exile years of the late 1860s and 1870s, rented rooms in Dresden, Geneva, Florence, and Bad Ems.
Daily Output
Varied enormously by deadline pressure. Under ordinary conditions, a few pages per session. Under his most brutal contracts, he could produce far more, as The Gambler demonstrates. Anna Dostoevskaya's memoirs document sessions running several hours through the night.
Tools
Pen and paper for drafts, extensive planning notebooks before drafting. Strong tea was a constant, consumed throughout the night. He drank it heavily sugared and in large quantities, a habit Anna noted in her memoirs.
Famous Ritual
Meticulous pre-draft notebooks, sometimes running to hundreds of pages, where he worked out character psychology, plot logic, and thematic questions before writing a single line of the novel proper.
Books Written This Way
Crime and Punishment, The Idiot, Demons, The Brothers Karamazov, The Gambler.

The Night Schedule

Dostoevsky wrote at night. This is well documented in Anna Dostoevskaya's Reminiscences, which covers their life together from 1867 until his death in 1881 and is the most detailed account we have of his working habits. She describes him regularly sitting down to write after she had gone to sleep, working through the night, and finishing in the early morning hours. He was not especially productive in the conventional sense of a writer who rises early and gets his pages in before the day intrudes. His days were for sleeping, walking, drinking tea, and arguing. His nights were for the novels.

This pattern shows up in his literary fiction in ways that are easy to miss if you're not looking for them. The great scenes in Dostoevsky tend to happen at night or in strange transitional hours. Raskolnikov commits his crime and suffers his fever at all hours but most intensely in the disorienting middle of the night. The conversations in The Brothers Karamazov that carry the most philosophical weight often take place when the characters are exhausted, past the point where their social defenses hold. Time itself is liquid in Dostoevsky, and part of that quality comes from a writer who was genuinely uncertain, from lived experience, what hour it was when he put the words down.

Epilepsy shaped his relationship with time in ways that go beyond mere scheduling. His seizures could come without warning, and the period after a seizure, the postictal state, left him depleted for days. He had to write when his body allowed it. A fixed morning routine of the Hemingway variety would have been impossible, because his health couldn't be organized around a clock. The nocturnal schedule wasn't a preference so much as the shape his life actually permitted. You can see this as a limitation, but what it actually produced was a writer with no illusions about the reliability of any system. He wrote when he could, under whatever conditions existed, and that hardened him in ways a more comfortable routine never would have.

The Notebooks

Before Dostoevsky wrote a novel, he filled notebooks. Dozens of them. The planning notebooks for his major works have been studied extensively by scholars, and they reveal a writer who treated the act of planning as itself a form of composition. He didn't make outlines in the conventional sense. He wrote exploratory prose, invented scenes he later discarded, tried out character voices, argued with himself about the meaning of what he was doing, and circled back to the same problems from different angles over months.

The notebooks for Crime and Punishment, which began appearing in serialized form in The Russian Messenger in January 1866, show a writer who changed the fundamental structure of the novel at least twice before committing to the first-person narrator. Early drafts were in the first person, told from Raskolnikov's point of view as a kind of confession. Dostoevsky abandoned this and switched to third person, which gave him the freedom to show what Raskolnikov couldn't see in himself. That shift, documented in the notebooks, is one of the most consequential decisions in nineteenth-century fiction, and it happened in a planning document, not in the draft.

The notebooks for The Brothers Karamazov are even more extensive. Kenneth Lantz, in The Dostoevsky Encyclopedia, notes that Dostoevsky spent considerable time working out the theological arguments in the planning stages, particularly around the Ivan and Alyosha sections, before those arguments appeared in dialogue. He was thinking his way into positions on questions that genuinely mattered to him, using the fictional characters as philosophical proxies, with the plotting a byproduct of that work rather than the goal. The planning stage was where he did the intellectual work. The drafting was where he turned that work into living people.

For anyone trying to understand how to write dense, philosophically complex fiction without losing the reader, this is the most useful part of Dostoevsky's process. You can write characters who hold ideas seriously without making your novel an essay, but only if you've already done the essay work before you started the novel. Dostoevsky's notebooks are where that essay work happened.

Debt, Deadline, and The Gambler

The twenty-six-day dictation of The Gambler was the most extreme version of what Dostoevsky's entire career looked like under the surface. He spent much of his adult life in debt, partly because he gambled compulsively during the years he lived in Europe, and partly because after his first wife and his brother Mikhail died in 1864, he took on Mikhail's debts as a matter of family honor. His own income from writing was frequently pledged in advance to creditors. He sometimes signed contracts for novels he hadn't started, accepting advances he'd already spent, and then had to produce the work under a kind of financial gun.

What's remarkable is that this pressure, which would have broken most writers, seemed to function for Dostoevsky as a kind of accelerant. The Gambler was written in the same calendar year as Crime and Punishment. He was working on both simultaneously, finishing Crime and Punishment installments for the magazine while dictating The Gambler to Anna in morning and afternoon sessions. The two novels couldn't be more different in scale and ambition, but both show the same intensity. There's an argument to be made, and Joseph Frank makes something like it across his five-volume biography, that the pressure of the deadlines was inseparable from the quality of the writing. A man who can be ruined if he stops writing has a different relationship to the page than a man who has time to reconsider.

Anna's account of the dictation sessions in her Reminiscences is worth reading closely. She describes Dostoevsky pacing while he dictated, stopping to think, sometimes asking her to read back what she'd written. He'd taken on the task as a purely practical matter, to satisfy the contract, and yet even in those sessions the work was careful. He wasn't dumping words. The speed forced a kind of directness, and the resulting novel is lean in a way his longer works are not. If you want to understand how deadlines can work for a writer rather than against one, The Gambler is the exhibit. If you want to understand your own relationship to pressure, knowing what kind of writer you are first will help you use that knowledge correctly.

The gambling addiction that produced The Gambler as subject matter is also part of the story. Dostoevsky lost money at roulette in Baden-Baden, Wiesbaden, and other European gambling towns throughout the late 1860s. He wrote about the psychology of the gambler from the inside, which is why the novel feels nothing like a morality tale. It feels like a confession. That quality, the sense that the author knows exactly what the character is feeling because he's felt it himself, runs through all the major novels, and the nocturnal, crisis-driven routine was the container that made it possible. Extreme living conditions produced extreme psychological insight, and the writing process was designed, half by choice and half by necessity, to keep those conditions in the room.

Epilepsy and the Feverish Quality of the Prose

Dostoevsky suffered from epilepsy throughout his adult life. His seizures were severe, and the experience of them left traces in the work that scholars have been examining for over a century. He described the aura before a seizure, the few seconds of strange clarity that preceded the attack, as a feeling of extraordinary harmony and beauty. He gave that experience to Prince Myshkin in The Idiot, and through Myshkin he tried to think through what it means to have access to a state of consciousness that feels like grace but comes packaged with suffering and disability.

The more practical effect of the epilepsy on his writing life was unpredictability. His health didn't follow a schedule. Some periods were worse, some better, and the years after his return from Siberian imprisonment in 1854 seem to have brought more frequent attacks than the earlier period. Anna's memoirs record instances where a seizure would incapacitate him for several days. He'd come back to the manuscript depleted, with deadlines still pressing, and have to find a way back into the work from a standing start. That experience of repeatedly returning to the work after involuntary absence probably shaped his understanding of how to hold a complex novel in memory across months and years.

You can see it structurally in the major novels. Dostoevsky didn't write with the smooth forward momentum of a writer who works every day without interruption. His novels move in bursts and spirals. A scene will be returned to from multiple angles, a character's psychology circled back to several times as new information changes what you thought you understood. Readers sometimes mistake this for looseness or sprawl, but it's actually closer to the experience of a mind that keeps having to re-enter its own obsessions from different points of entry. The notebooks were partly what made this coherent. They gave him a fixed external map to navigate back to when the terrain of the novel had gone dark.

Fyodor Dostoevsky's Writing Routine in Practice

The picture that emerges from Anna Dostoevskaya's Reminiscences and from the memoirs of others who knew him is of a man who kept irregular hours but was deeply attached to the work itself. He drank tea through the night while he wrote, strongly brewed and heavily sweetened, and he smoked cigarettes. The physical setting was spare wherever he was; the years of European exile produced a series of rented apartments that were never comfortable and often cold.

He'd typically spend part of his working session rereading what he'd written before, not to revise heavily but to find his way back into the rhythm of it. Then he'd continue. Anna records that he sometimes came to wake her in the night to read her a passage he'd just finished. He needed the audience, even briefly, even in the middle of the night. He was a writer who needed to hear the prose out loud to know if it was working. The dictation sessions for The Gambler weren't foreign to his temperament; he'd always had some version of that oral test available to him in his wife.

In the later years, particularly during the writing of The Brothers Karamazov from 1878 to 1880, he was more settled in St. Petersburg and slightly less harried by immediate financial crisis. But the basic pattern held: night hours, strong tea, extensive prior planning, and a constant pressure of deadlines both real and self-imposed. He died in January 1881, less than two months after delivering the final installment of The Brothers Karamazov to the magazine. He'd been working on the novel for nearly two years. He was fifty-nine years old.

Sources

The most essential primary source is Anna Dostoevskaya's Reminiscences (originally published in Russian as Vospominaniya, English translation by Beatrice Stillman, 1975), which covers her life with Dostoevsky from 1866 until his death and includes detailed accounts of his work habits, the dictation of The Gambler, and his nocturnal schedule. Joseph Frank's five-volume biography, Dostoevsky (Princeton University Press, 1976–2002), is the standard scholarly life in English and provides extensive context for his working conditions, finances, and the composition of each major novel. Kenneth Lantz's The Dostoevsky Encyclopedia (Greenwood Press, 2004) is a useful reference for specific compositional details, including the planning notebooks. For the notebooks themselves, The Notebooks for Crime and Punishment, edited and translated by Edward Wasiolek (University of Chicago Press, 1967), is the most accessible edition in English.

What You Can Steal

Dostoevsky's routine is most useful to writers who don't fit the conventional morning-pages mold, who work under pressure, or who are trying to write philosophically complex fiction without losing their readers. Here's what transfers:

  • Find your actual productive window, not the culturally approved one. Dostoevsky wrote at night because his health and temperament required it. If your best thinking happens at midnight, structure your writing life around that, not around advice written for morning people.
  • Do the intellectual work before you draft. His planning notebooks weren't outlines; they were a separate layer of composition where he argued through the ideas the novel needed to contain. Writers who skip this phase often produce first drafts that are thin because the thinking wasn't done yet.
  • Pressure can accelerate rather than destroy. The twenty-six-day dictation of The Gambler worked because the deadline forced a directness he might not have found otherwise. A real deadline, with real consequences, is one of the most effective writing tools there is.
  • Write from your actual psychological material. The gambling addiction, the epilepsy, the debt, the imprisonment in Siberia, all of it went directly into the novels without being tidied up first. The intensity in Dostoevsky comes from a writer who used what his life actually contained rather than keeping a careful separation between the writing life and the lived one.
  • Read the work aloud, or find someone to read it to. Dostoevsky regularly read passages to Anna and others. He needed to hear the prose. If a sentence sounds wrong read aloud, it is wrong, regardless of how it looks on the page.
