How Many Words a Day Did Gabriel Garcia Marquez Write?

Kia Orion | August 2, 2026 | 5 min read

The best answer comes from the man himself, and it is smaller than almost anyone expects. Five or six hours at the desk, and at the end of it a short paragraph he would probably destroy the following morning.

The Direct Answer
Four or five lines on a good day, by his own account.
He worked from nine in the morning until two or three in the afternoon and told the Paris Review in 1981 that a short paragraph was the most he could manage, and that he usually tore it up the next day. As a young journalist the pace had been far faster.

The 1981 Answer, in His Own Words

In the Paris Review interview published in the winter of 1981, Peter H. Stone asked Garcia Marquez about how much faster he had been as a young newspaperman. The reply is the clearest daily output figure anyone has from him.

On a good working day, working from nine o'clock in the morning to two or three in the afternoon, the most I can write is a short paragraph of four or five lines, which I usually tear up the next day.

Gabriel Garcia Marquez, The Paris Review, 1981

Notice what the number measures. It counts the sentences that survive the following morning. The five-hour window stays fixed either way, and on the days the paragraph goes in the bin the yield lands somewhere near zero. Most writers would call that a failed session. He called it a working day.

The Younger Man Wrote Much Faster

In the same interview he described his years at El Espectador in Bogota: at least three stories a week, two or three editorial notes every day, film reviews on top of that, and then the novels at night after everyone had gone home. He said he liked the noise of the Linotype machines, which sounded like rain, and that silence stopped him working.

So the four-or-five-lines figure is a late-career number, and the drop is the interesting part. He said he found writing harder than before because he had become conscious of every word. Speed was what he traded away for control, and he seemed to think it was a fair price.

Eighteen Months for One Hundred Years of Solitude

The book that made him took roughly eighteen months of daily work, beginning in 1965. The household bill for that period is documented in The Fragrance of Guava, his 1983 book-length conversation with Plinio Apuleyo Mendoza. He sold the car, ran up credit with the butcher and the baker, and owed nine months of rent by the time he finished.

The finished novel runs 422 pages in the standard English translation. Eighteen months of daily work on a 422-page book comes out under a page a day, and that assumes nothing was thrown away. For this writer that is a bad assumption. Whatever came out of the typewriter each afternoon, a fair share of it never reached the manuscript.

The novel has since sold well past fifty million copies and been translated into more than forty languages, which makes the arithmetic worth sitting with. Under a page a day, for a year and a half, on credit.

What You Can Steal

  • Fix the hours and let the output float. Nine until two or three was the commitment. What came out of it varied, and sometimes it came to nothing.
  • Count what survives. A paragraph that still holds up the next morning is worth more than two thousand words you will cut in revision.
  • Expect to get slower. He was faster at twenty-five than at fifty and said so plainly. Rising standards cost you speed.
  • Work with noise if noise works. Silence stopped him. The Linotype racket was part of the setup, and he missed it when it went.
