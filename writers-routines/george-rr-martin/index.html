George R.R. Martin's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 1, 2026 | 10 min read

George R.R. Martin writes his novels on WordStar 4.0, a DOS word processing program that was released in 1987, on a computer that has no internet connection. This is not an affectation or a publicity stunt. He's been doing it for decades, and when he explained why in a 2014 interview with Conan O'Brien, the answer was straightforward: the program does exactly what he needs and he has no reason to change. The separate, offline computer means nothing can interrupt him. No email, no Twitter, no browser tab he can drift into. Just the writing.

People treat this as an eccentric quirk, a piece of trivia about a famous slow writer. I think they're missing what it actually reveals about how he works. Martin has built a physical architecture around his process. Two machines, two modes, a hard wall between the writing state and the connected world. Most writers manage distraction by willpower. Martin manages it by making distraction structurally impossible. That's a meaningful distinction, and it's probably part of why his books are so dense with world, character, and consequence. He's in the work when he's in the work, and nowhere else.

Two Computers, One Rule

The two-machine system is the most concrete thing we know about Martin's writing process, and it's worth taking seriously as a piece of craft infrastructure. Most writers today work on a single laptop that is also their email client, their social media portal, their news feed, and their research browser. The machine they write on is the same machine they use to avoid writing. Martin solved that problem a long time ago by refusing to let it exist.

WordStar 4.0 has a green cursor on a black screen and a menu system that has not changed since the Reagan administration. It can't display images, load a webpage, or ping a server. When Martin is writing on his DOS machine, the only thing that machine is capable of doing is word processing. That constraint is a feature. A 2014 conversation with Conan O'Brien brought this to wider attention when Martin explained the setup, and the clip went around the internet the way things do. What got less attention was the logic behind it: Martin described the writing computer as a place where he can be fully in the work.

This maps onto something we know from cognitive research. Switching tasks, even briefly, carries a cost that is larger than most people expect. A 2001 study by Joshua Rubinstein at the American Psychological Association found that task-switching costs can eat up as much as 40 percent of productive time. Martin's setup doesn't require him to resist the urge to check his email during a writing session. The computer literally cannot send or receive email. The urge has nowhere to go, so it fades, and the writing continues.

I've written about distraction management in the context of a lot of writer routines on this site, and Martin's is the most architecturally complete version I've come across. It's not about discipline. It's about design.

Gardeners and Architects

Martin's most quoted piece of craft theory is his distinction between two types of writers. Architects plan everything before they build. They work from blueprints. Gardeners plant seeds and see what grows. Martin says he's a gardener, and he means it literally: he begins a book with a general sense of where it ends up, some characters, some set pieces, and then he follows them. The characters deviate from his plans. He follows the deviations.

This matters to understanding his routine because a gardener and an architect are doing fundamentally different things when they sit down at the keyboard. An architect is executing. They know what comes next. A gardener is discovering. They're writing to find out. Discovery writing, which is what Martin does, requires a different kind of attention than execution writing does. You can't rush it. If you push a gardener through their pages the way you'd push a contractor to hit a deadline, the quality collapses, because the quality comes from genuine surprise, from the writer finding something on the page that they didn't know was there before they wrote it.

The long gaps between Martin's books are partly a product of the scope and complexity of the A Song of Ice and Fire series, which by any reasonable measure is one of the most plot-dense works of fantasy ever written. But they're also a product of the gardener's method. When Martin is unsure where a chapter is going, he can't just consult an outline. He has to sit with it, try different approaches, throw out drafts. That takes time in a way that execution writing doesn't. The fans who are frustrated by The Winds of Winter's absence are, in some sense, asking a gardener to grow crops on an architect's schedule.

The Weight of the World

Martin grew up in Bayonne, New Jersey, the son of a longshoreman, and started writing science fiction in the 1970s before transitioning to fantasy. By the time he conceived A Game of Thrones in the early 1990s, he'd spent years writing for television on shows including Beauty and the Beast, and he specifically chose to write a novel because he wanted to do things a TV budget couldn't handle: armies of thousands, dragons, scenes across continents. The first book in the series was published in 1996, when Martin was 48 years old.

What's interesting about Martin's development as a writer is that his slow, exploratory, gardener's method didn't fully show its consequences until the series got very long. The first three books came out in 1996, 1998, and 2000. A five-year gap followed before A Feast for Crows in 2005, and then six more years before A Dance with Dragons in 2011. Each book had grown in size and complexity, and the gardener was now tending a garden that covered multiple continents, tracked dozens of point-of-view characters, and had to maintain internal consistency with thousands of established facts. His DOS machine with its green cursor was still doing the same thing it had always done. The scope of what it needed to process had become enormous.

Martin has talked about this with unusual candor. In blog posts on his LiveJournal (which he has used since 2005 and still updates), he's been open about the difficulty of the later books, about storylines that weren't working, about the weight of reader expectation. He once wrote that he was not your slave and that the book would be done when it's done, which landed badly with some fans but reads, to me, like a writer being honest about the limits of his method when applied at scale. The gardener can't harvest before the crop is ready.

What Slow Writing Actually Looks Like

There's a version of the Martin story that treats his slowness as a failure of discipline, a writer who got famous and stopped trying. I find that reading unpersuasive, and not because I'm being charitable. Martin updates his blog regularly. He runs a theater company in Santa Fe, the Jean Cocteau Cinema, which he bought in 2012 and has operated since. He's written short fiction and edited anthologies. He's consulted extensively on the Game of Thrones television series and its spinoffs. The man works constantly. The slowness is specific to the novels, and the novels are slow for reasons that are structural, not personal.

When a gardener is writing a long series, they accumulate constraints as they go. Every fact established in book one becomes a thing the gardener has to work around in book six. Every character arc has to resolve, or deliberately not resolve, in ways that feel earned by what came before. Martin has created a world of such density, with so many moving parts across so many point-of-view characters, that the freedom to follow the garden wherever it leads has become genuinely difficult to exercise. There are too many established plants. A new choice touches twenty other things.

This is what he means when he talks about chapters that just won't work, about having to go back to the beginning of a sequence and try again. The gardener's method is magnificent for generating rich, character-driven, surprising fiction. It runs into real trouble at 1.5 million published words with more to come. What we're watching with The Winds of Winter is a methodological limit being tested in public, which is uncomfortable but also genuinely interesting if you care about how books are made.

George R.R. Martin's WordStar Writing Routine

Martin's WordStar writing routine is not a morning routine in the conventional sense. He doesn't have a published wake time, a fixed number of hours at the keyboard, or a target word count he aims for each session. What he has is a dedicated machine, a specific program, and a physical separation between the writing state and the connected world. The routine is built around the environment, not the schedule.

When he described the setup to Conan O'Brien in 2014, he said WordStar had everything he needed for writing: it let him move text around, it let him search, it let him save. He didn't need a word processor that could also browse the internet or run a spreadsheet. The simplicity of the tool is part of the point. A program that can only do one thing pulls your attention toward that one thing. In an era when most writing software is embedded in ecosystems of notifications, cloud syncing, and ambient connectivity, Martin's DOS machine is a deliberate act of removal.

The Santa Fe house, where he lives with his wife Parris McCallum, has been his base since the early 2000s. He's spoken fondly of New Mexico in interviews, of the light and the quiet and the distance from the entertainment industry centers on the coasts. The Jean Cocteau Cinema, which he reopened in 2013 after it had been closed for years, gives him a community project and a reason to leave the house that has nothing to do with the novels. That separation matters too. A writer who never stops thinking about the book eventually stops being able to think about the book freshly. The theater, the blog, the occasional convention appearance, these are the things that let him come back to the DOS screen with something still to give.

Sources

The most widely circulated account of Martin's writing setup comes from his 2014 appearance on Conan O'Brien's show, where he described using WordStar 4.0 and keeping his writing machine offline. His LiveJournal blog, which he has maintained since 2005, is a consistent primary source for his thoughts on writing, the challenges of the later books, and his life in Santa Fe. His 2014 interview with Rolling Stone, "George R.R. Martin: The Rolling Stone Interview," covers his background, his television years, and his approach to the series in depth. Cecelia Holland's 2011 profile in The New Yorker and various convention panels from San Diego Comic-Con across the 2010s add further texture. For the gardener/architect distinction, Martin has explained it at length in writing panels; a representative version appears in his 2007 WorldCon remarks. See also: George R.R. Martin on Wikipedia.

What You Can Steal

Martin's routine is unusual in that its lessons are mostly architectural rather than behavioral. The takeaways don't require you to adopt WordStar. They require you to think about the structure of your writing environment.

  • Separate your writing machine from your internet machine. Martin uses two computers. You can approximate this with a cheap Chromebook in offline mode, a typewriter, a dedicated word processor, or a browser extension that kills connectivity during your writing hours. The specific method matters less than the hard separation.
  • Know which type of writer you are. Gardeners and architects both produce good work, but they require different conditions and different timelines. If you're a gardener writing on an architect's schedule, you'll produce flat, airless prose. Find the method that matches your actual process, not the one that sounds more professional.
  • Let the characters deviate. Martin's most interesting choices, the deaths that shocked readers, the turns that nobody saw coming, came from following his characters rather than his outlines. The outline is a map, not a contract. When a character tells you something truer than your plan, follow the character.
  • Use old tools without shame. WordStar has no grammar checker, no style suggestions, no auto-complete. It gets out of the way. If the tool you're most comfortable with is one other people think is obsolete, keep using it. Comfort and familiarity in your tools lower the activation cost of getting started, and getting started is the main problem.
  • Give yourself a life outside the book. Martin runs a cinema, writes a blog, travels to conventions. A writer who has no other life eventually has no other perspective, and perspective is what makes fiction feel true. The things you do when you're away from the manuscript are feeding it, even when it doesn't feel that way.

Frequently Asked Questions

What software does George R.R. Martin write on?

Martin writes on WordStar 4.0, a DOS word processing program from 1987. He runs it on an old computer specifically dedicated to writing, with no internet connection. He explained the setup in a 2014 interview with Conan O'Brien and has said the program does everything he needs.

Does George R.R. Martin write every day?

Martin has said he doesn't write on a rigid daily schedule. He writes when he can, not by the clock or a fixed word count target. He's been candid about the difficulty of working on the later books in the series, and the long gaps between volumes reflect a genuinely exploratory, discovery-based process.

What does George R.R. Martin mean by being a gardener not an architect?

Martin divides writers into two types. Architects plan before they build. Gardeners plant seeds and discover what grows. Martin says he's a gardener: he has a broad sense of where the story goes, but his characters often deviate from his outlines, and he follows those deviations rather than overriding them. It's how A Song of Ice and Fire got its famous unpredictability.

Where does George R.R. Martin live and write?

Martin lives in Santa Fe, New Mexico with his wife Parris McCallum. He's been based there since the early 2000s. He keeps his writing machine physically separate from his internet machine, and he also owns and operates the Jean Cocteau Cinema, a movie theater he reopened in Santa Fe in 2013.
