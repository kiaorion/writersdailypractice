How Does George Saunders Revise?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

George Saunders has one of the most quoted revision techniques in modern fiction, and it sounds almost like a party trick until you sit down and try it. He imagines a meter in his forehead and lets it tell him, line by line, whether the prose is working. Here's how the method actually runs.

The Direct Answer
He reads line by line with an imaginary P/N meter and nudges each sentence toward positive.
The method anchors his 2021 craft book A Swim in a Pond in the Rain and boils down to intuition plus iteration.

The Meter in His Forehead

When Saunders sits down to revise, he pictures a gauge mounted just above his eyes. One side reads P for positive, the other N for negative. He runs his eye down the page one line at a time and watches which way the needle swings. That swing is the whole game. It tells him, in the moment, whether a sentence is pulling its weight, and he trusts it more than any rule he could name.

There's a meter in my head with P on one side for positive and N for negative. My idea is that the meter responds when I read prose.

George Saunders, on The Ezra Klein Show

The hard part is reading his own work as if a stranger wrote it. He's talked about trying to see the page the way someone would if they'd just found it on a bus seat, with no attachment and no memory of the effort behind it. Isak Dinesen's line 'without hope and without despair' is the mood he's chasing. Ego off, panic off, just an honest reaction he can do something with.

Watch the Needle, Fix the Line

His summary of the process is two words: intuition plus iteration. When the needle dips into N, he doesn't stop to diagnose why. He nudges the sentence instead, a cut, a swapped word, a clause moved to the front, then reads again and checks the needle. Watch, adjust, watch, adjust. On a stubborn story that loop can run through hundreds of drafts over months or even years.

The physical routine is old-fashioned on purpose. He works on a printed hard copy with a pencil he keeps sharpening, marking the page as he reads, then enters the changes and prints a fresh copy. A short story might go through three or four full passes in a single day. He's compared the slow accumulation to a cruise ship turning. No single edit feels dramatic, and yet thousands of tiny corrections quietly change the story's whole direction.

Saunders is blunt that the first draft barely matters. Its only job is to exist so there's something to react to. All the work he cares about happens afterward, in the reading and the nudging, which is why he says the revision is where a writer's real personality finally shows up on the page.

He also refuses to outline. He'd rather not know where a story is going, because the surprise of discovering it is what keeps the prose alive as he writes. You can see how that fits the rest of his practice in the full George Saunders writing routine.

What You Can Steal

  • Read your draft cold. Pretend a stranger wrote it, then react honestly.
  • Trust the gut before the analysis. Mark the dip first, explain it later, or never.
  • Revise in many small passes. One sentence at a time beats one heroic rewrite.
  • Let the fixes compound. Tiny course corrections add up to a different story.
