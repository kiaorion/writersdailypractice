What Is George Saunders's Writing Routine?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Saunders is refreshingly ordinary about his schedule, and quick to admit it has gotten looser over the years. His writing day is short, tactile, and built around reading his own pages back to himself. Here's what a typical one looks like.

The Direct Answer
On an ideal day he writes from about 8am to 2 or 3pm, then stops.
He described the rhythm in an interview with Sixty Six, and says he keeps it gentler now than he once did.

The Shape of a Writing Day

Saunders's ideal day isn't a marathon. He gets up around eight, sits down, and works until early afternoon with a break somewhere in the middle. That's it. He tends to settle into the same spot each morning to make starting automatic, and the hours stay modest by design, because the work he's doing is intense reading and re-reading rather than racing to hit a big word count.

I would get up at 8 or something and sit down and work until 2 or 3, usually with a break in there.

George Saunders, interview with Sixty Six

What he's mostly doing in those hours is reading. Saunders drafts a little and revises a lot, running the same pages through his head again and again to catch where the prose sags. So the roughly six-hour ceiling isn't laziness. It's the point where his ear gets tired and he stops trusting his own judgment, which he treats as a hard signal to quit for the day.

The mechanics are deliberately analog. He works on a printed hard copy with a pencil he sharpens almost compulsively, marking up the page as he reads, and only turns to the laptop once he's gotten a paragraph where he wants it. The pencil keeps him slow, and slow is the point. It forces him to feel every sentence instead of skimming past it.

He's also candid that the schedule has relaxed with time. Where he once needed a tight structure to make himself show up, he now says he craves the work enough that he doesn't have to police the clock as strictly. The habit does the enforcing for him, the way brushing your teeth doesn't need a pep talk.

A Small Daily Practice

Alongside the big projects, Saunders has talked in his Story Club newsletter about keeping a daily writing streak, giving himself a small, non-negotiable amount of new fiction to write each day. The specific number gets cited as around 500 words. The exact figure matters less than the mechanism. A modest daily minimum keeps the engine warm and takes the drama out of sitting down, because you're never starting completely cold.

His routine also bends around teaching. Saunders has been on the faculty of the Syracuse MFA program since 1997, and although he and his family live in California, he returns to campus several times each fall to lead the third-year workshop, a famously selective room that admits a handful of fiction writers out of hundreds of applicants. For more on how the writing and the teaching fit together, see the full George Saunders writing routine.

What You Can Steal

  • Keep the writing day short. A focused five or six hours beats a punishing twelve.
  • Go analog to slow down. Pencil on paper makes you feel each sentence.
  • Set a minimum you can always hit. A small daily quota kills the cold start.
  • Let the habit replace the willpower. Craving the work beats forcing it.
