Gillian Flynn's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 1, 2026 | 9 min read

Gillian Flynn wrote Sharp Objects at night, at a kitchen table in Chicago, while still working full time as a TV critic at Entertainment Weekly. She didn't announce she was writing a novel. She just wrote one. When the magazine laid her off in 2008, she had already been at this for years in the margins of a day job, and the book that came from those margins sold millions of copies and eventually got adapted for HBO. The second novel, Dark Places, followed the same pattern. Gone Girl came after that, and by the time it hit in 2012 it was one of the most talked-about thrillers in decades.

Flynn's story gets told a lot as a layoff-to-success arc, a journalist who found her real calling when her job disappeared. That reading is too tidy. The actual sequence is more instructive: she was already doing the work before she lost the job, at night, without an audience, without a deadline she'd been handed by anyone else. The layoff just removed the thing that was making her write in the dark. What matters about her routine is the shape it had before the breakthrough, when there was no reason to keep it going except that she needed to.

The Secret Novel

Flynn spent ten years as a TV critic at Entertainment Weekly, where she was good at the job and took it seriously. She wrote about prestige television before anyone called it that, during the years when The Sopranos and The Wire were redefining what the medium could do. She understood narrative structure from watching hundreds of hours of it and breaking it down for readers every week. That background shaped her fiction in ways she's talked about in interviews: her plots are engineered, her reveals precisely timed, her act structures visible to anyone paying attention. Ten years of criticism taught her how stories work at the mechanical level, and that knowledge went directly into the novels.

What she didn't do was talk about the novels while she was writing them. Friends and colleagues at the magazine apparently didn't know she was working on Sharp Objects until it was already sold. That secrecy wasn't strategic exactly, but it was effective. Writing a first novel in private removes a particular kind of pressure. Nobody is waiting, which means you're free to write the book badly for as long as you need to before it becomes the book it's going to be. Many writers kill their projects by narrating them to other people before the project is strong enough to survive outside attention. Flynn bypassed that problem by not narrating anything.

The kitchen table suited the secrecy. A private study would have been a declaration. A kitchen table is just where you sit after the dishes are done and the house goes quiet. The writing happened inside the ordinary life, not separately from it, and that allowed it to build for years without anyone treating it as a project that could succeed or fail. When it finally emerged as an actual book, with an agent and a deal and a pub date, it had already survived the hardest part, which is the long private period when the only evidence that it matters is that you keep coming back to it.

Night Writing and Why It Works

Flynn has been consistent about this across many interviews: she's a night writer. The late hours suit her for the same reason the early morning hours suited Morrison, the critical voice is quieter when the day is over. The brain is tired enough to stop second-guessing and just produce. Whatever instinct tells you the sentence is too dark or the character is too broken has used up most of its energy by ten o'clock at night, and what's left is something looser and more honest.

This matters a lot for Flynn specifically, because the material she works with requires that honesty. Her protagonists are not sympathetic in the conventional sense. Camille Preaker in Sharp Objects is a self-harming alcoholic journalist. Amy Dunne in Gone Girl is a sociopath who frames her husband for her own murder. Libby Day in Dark Places falsely testified against her brother as a child and has spent her adult life lying about it. Writing those characters convincingly requires getting to a place where the author isn't flinching away from the character's worst impulses. Flynn has said in interviews that she finds compelling villains more interesting to write than virtuous protagonists, and the night schedule gives her access to the part of her mind that agrees.

She's also said something useful about darkness as a subject, that she doesn't pretend to the reader that she doesn't have a dark turn of mind, and doesn't see the point in pretending otherwise. That's not a persona. Every book she's written is animated by genuine curiosity about what people are capable of when the social contract stops working. Writing at night, in a quiet house, gave her the conditions to follow that curiosity without interruption.

The Outline as Infrastructure

Flynn's most discussed habit among writers is her approach to outlining. She has said, in multiple interviews across her career, that she outlines to the point where every scene is accounted for before she writes any prose. The outline tells her what happens, who's in the scene, what each character wants, and what has to change by the end of it. By the time she starts drafting, the structural decisions are already made.

For thriller writers, this discipline is almost a professional requirement. A psychological thriller depends on the reader not knowing certain things at certain times and knowing others at exactly the right moment. Controlling that information sequencing requires knowing how the whole book ends before you know how any single scene should read. You can't stumble into the right withheld information because you don't know yet what the revelation will be. Flynn's outlines are what allow her plots to feel inevitable in retrospect. The twist in Gone Girl, the shift in perspective at the midpoint, didn't happen because Flynn discovered it while drafting. She built the whole structure around it before the prose existed.

Writers who resist outlining often frame it as a freedom argument, the outline constrains spontaneity, the story should surprise its author. Flynn's argument is the opposite. Knowing the structure means the drafting can be spontaneous at the sentence level because the architecture isn't in question. The scene exists. What's not yet decided is exactly how the dialogue will run or which detail will carry the weight. The outline frees her to write badly for a while because she knows the scene will eventually do what it needs to do, since she decided what that was before she started typing.

Drafting Without Editing

Flynn separates the generative phase from the editing phase completely. When she's drafting, she doesn't go back. She finishes the whole thing first. This is a different discipline from outlining, though they work together, and it addresses a different failure mode. The outline solves for not knowing where you're going. The no-editing rule solves for the trap of perpetual revision, the writer who has been working on chapter one for six months because it still isn't quite right.

The logic is straightforward once you accept it. A chapter that needs revision can only be revised correctly once you know what comes after it. You might fix chapter one based on what it should be setting up, but if you don't yet know what it's setting up, you're revising toward a version of the story you're imagining rather than the one that actually exists. Flynn's method skips this problem by getting the whole draft out first. Now revision has real material to work with. The shape of the story is on the page, not in a partially drafted state where every decision is provisional.

What this requires is a tolerance for bad writing in a drawer. The first draft doesn't need to be shown to anyone and probably shouldn't be. Flynn has described her first drafts as rough, which given the precision of her published prose suggests she's willing to let the early version be genuinely ugly. That willingness is one of the things writers most struggle with. The gap between what you want the writing to be and what it currently is produces a paralysis that looks like perfectionism but functions as avoidance. Getting a full draft done, however rough, closes that gap into something workable.

Gillian Flynn's Writing Routine in Practice

Flynn's actual schedule, in the years she was building her career, was whatever was left after the day job. She worked in television journalism for a decade at Entertainment Weekly, covering the kind of prestige TV she'd later help define the audience for, and wrote fiction in the remaining hours. The layoff in 2008 changed the math but not the practice itself. By then she'd already published two novels and had a third in progress.

After the layoff, she wrote full time from home in Chicago. Her husband Brett Nolan was working; she had children and a house. The writing happened during school hours and then again in the evenings when the house settled. She's talked about Chicago as the right city for the kind of fiction she writes, flat and gray in winter, a city that doesn't perform warmth it doesn't feel, and the Midwest bleakness runs through all three of her novels whether or not the action is set there. The place shaped the palette.

The outline comes first, always. Flynn has said in interviews with outlets including The Guardian and Entertainment Weekly itself that she doesn't start writing a book until the outline is complete enough that she knows every beat. For Gone Girl, which was published in June 2012 by Crown Publishers, that outline had to account for a dual-narrative structure, a time jump, and a midpoint revelation that completely reframes the first half of the book. Building that structure in outline meant she could draft with confidence, knowing every planted detail was going somewhere. The prose could be playful, the characters could surprise her at the sentence level, because the larger architecture held.

What You Can Steal

Flynn's routine is built around two disciplines most writers avoid, and one condition most writers dismiss. Here's what's worth taking:

  • Find your real hour, not your aspirational one. Flynn writes at night, not at 5 AM. She's said this about herself directly. If your brain works at ten o'clock at night, that's when you write. The hour that produces the actual pages is the only one that counts.
  • Write the book before you announce it. The secrecy around Sharp Objects protected it. Writing without an audience removes the pressure to justify what you're making before it's made. Nobody can tell you it won't work if nobody knows it exists yet.
  • Outline until the structure is complete, then draft. The Flynn two-phase approach, full outline before a single page of prose, solves two problems at once. You never don't know where you're going, and you never revise backward into a story you haven't written yet.
  • Get the whole draft out before touching revision. A chapter one you've been revising for six months is still chapter one. A bad draft of the full book is a book you can now actually fix. Those are very different situations, and only one of them has a clear path forward.
  • Don't flinch from the material. Flynn's advice here is implicit in everything she's said publicly: if your subject matter makes you uncomfortable, that discomfort is pointing at something real. The dark turn of mind is an asset, not a liability. Write toward it.

Frequently Asked Questions

When does Gillian Flynn write?

Flynn is a night writer. She prefers to work late, after her family is asleep, when the house is quiet. She wrote Sharp Objects and Dark Places at night while still working full time as a TV critic at Entertainment Weekly. The late hours are where her most productive work happens.

Does Gillian Flynn outline her novels?

Yes, obsessively. Flynn has said she outlines to the point where she knows every scene before she starts drafting. The outline functions as a complete architectural blueprint. The prose-drafting phase fills in that structure rather than inventing one alongside it.

Does Gillian Flynn edit as she writes?

No. Flynn gets the whole draft out before going back to revise. She separates the generative phase from the editing phase entirely. Mixing them, in her view, is how writers stall. A bad full draft is fixable. A perpetually revised opening chapter is a trap.

Where did Gillian Flynn write Sharp Objects?

At the kitchen table in her Chicago apartment, mostly at night, while still employed as a TV critic at Entertainment Weekly. She wrote the novel without telling most people it was happening, and it was already sold before colleagues at the magazine knew she'd been working on it.
