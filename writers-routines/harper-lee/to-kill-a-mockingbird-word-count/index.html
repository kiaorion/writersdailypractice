To Kill a Mockingbird Word Count: How Long Is It?

Kia Orion | August 2, 2026 | 5 min read

To Kill a Mockingbird lands in the middle of what publishers still buy, somewhere around 100,000 words. The unusual part is the ratio of rewriting to writing. Lee spent two and a half years turning a set of anecdotes into that book.

The Direct Answer
Around 100,000 words.
Counts disagree more than usual here. The two figures repeated most often are 100,388 and 99,121 words. Lower estimates near 88,000 also circulate. No official count exists, so the honest answer is a book in the 90,000 to 100,400 band. The 1960 first edition ran 281 pages.

The Count, and Why It Wobbles

100,388 is the figure most sites give, and it's the one Broke by Books uses in its survey of novel lengths. Reading-length trackers come in slightly under at 99,121. Estimates around 88,000 turn up too, usually traceable to counts of school or abridged editions rather than the full text. A gap of 12,000 words between the low and high figures is wider than you'd normally see for a book this famous.

The reason is mundane. Publishers don't release official counts, so every number online comes from someone running a particular digital file through a counter, and To Kill a Mockingbird has more classroom editions in circulation than almost any other American novel. Some carry study apparatus. Some are trimmed. For benchmarking, anything in the 90,000 to 100,000 band tells you the same useful thing: this is a normal length literary novel, the kind an agent will read from an unknown writer today without blinking at the number.

One Year to Write, Two and a Half to Rewrite

In December 1956 Lee opened a Christmas card from her friends Michael and Joy Brown containing a note and a year's wages. They were giving her twelve months of salary so she could quit the reservations desk at Eastern Air Lines and write full time. Charles J. Shields records the gift in his biography Mockingbird, and it's the hinge the whole career turns on.

She delivered a manuscript to J.B. Lippincott in 1957, then titled Go Set a Watchman. Her editor Tay Hohoff thought the manuscript read as a series of anecdotes rather than a finished novel, and also thought the writer behind it was the real thing. The two of them worked through draft after draft for two and a half years. The book was retitled Atticus, then To Kill a Mockingbird, and Lippincott published it on 11 July 1960.

The spark of the true writer flashed in every line.

Tay Hohoff, Harper Lee's editor at J.B. Lippincott, on the first manuscript

Run the arithmetic. Roughly 100,000 finished words across three and a half years from the day she went full time. That's under eighty words a day of net output, and it produced a Pulitzer Prize and forty million copies sold. Lee worked slowly on purpose, revising sentences as she went rather than pushing forward and fixing later, which is exactly what the prose reads like.

How It Compares

100,000 words is the top of the sweet spot. Literary and upmarket fiction sells at 80,000 to 100,000, and that band has barely moved in sixty years. The Great Gatsby at 47,094 is less than half of it. Stephen King's It at around 445,000 is four and a half times over. To Kill a Mockingbird sits precisely where a debut novelist should be aiming.

The figure worth stealing is the revision ratio rather than the word count. A complete draft existed in 1957. The book readers got in 1960 came out of two and a half more years of structural rebuilding with an editor willing to tell her the manuscript wasn't a novel yet. Most writers spend that ratio the other way round, and it shows on the page.

What You Can Steal

  • Aim for 80,000 to 100,000 on a literary debut. To Kill a Mockingbird sits at the top of that band and has for sixty years.
  • Budget more time for revision than for drafting. Lee wrote a complete manuscript in a year and then spent two and a half years rebuilding it.
  • Find the editor who will say the hard thing. Hohoff told Lee the manuscript was a set of anecdotes. That sentence is what made the book.
  • Eighty words a day is enough. Slow, revised-as-you-go output across three and a half years produced a hundred thousand words worth keeping.
