How Many Words a Day Does Haruki Murakami Write?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Murakami runs his daily output like a metronome. The number is fixed, and the discipline is in hitting it on good days and bad days alike.

The Direct Answer
About ten manuscript pages, roughly 1,600 words a day.
Ten Japanese manuscript pages, which he equates to sixteen hundred English words, per Novelist as a Vocation.

Ten Pages, Every Day

Murakami's rule is ten pages of Japanese manuscript paper a day. Each sheet holds four hundred characters, so ten pages comes to roughly four thousand characters, which he describes as the equivalent of about sixteen hundred English words. The figure stays the same whether the writing is flowing or grinding, which is the whole point. He's said his rule is to produce those ten pages a day, no more and no less.

What makes the rule hold up is the brake he puts on himself. On days when the words are coming easily he still stops at ten. On days when they aren't, he forces himself to reach the quota anyway. The cap protects tomorrow's session as much as it shapes today's, because it keeps him from burning out the well on a single good morning.

He's tied this directly to a habit he picked up from another writer. He stops each day at the point where he still feels he could keep going, so the next day's work starts with momentum already in hand instead of a cold engine.

I stop every day right at the point where I feel I can write more. Do that, and the next day's work goes surprisingly smoothly. I think Ernest Hemingway did something like that. To keep on going, you have to keep up the rhythm.

Haruki Murakami, What I Talk About When I Talk About Running

Why a Steady Number Beats a Big One

Murakami treats a novel like a long-distance event, so a sprint is the wrong instinct. A modest, repeatable number is what lets him keep the same pace for the six months to a year a book takes. He compares it to getting a flywheel spinning at a set speed and then letting momentum carry the rest, and he's clear that reaching that steady speed takes as much concentration and effort as he can manage.

Across a typical novel, sixteen hundred words a day adds up fast. A few months of unbroken ten-page mornings is enough to draft a long book, which is how a writer with no dramatic word-count records still produces doorstop novels on a regular cycle. The output isn't loud. It's relentless.

It's worth saying that the number is for first-draft writing, not the finished article. Murakami revises heavily afterward, so the daily ten pages are raw material, the clay he'll shape over many later passes. The quota's job is to make sure there's always clay.

The deeper logic is rhythm. Murakami links the daily count to the same idea that drives his running, where keeping a steady cadence matters more than any single fast day. He says the important thing for long-term projects is to keep up the rhythm, and once the pace is set, the rest will follow. A fixed ten pages is how he sets that pace and keeps it humming across a whole book. For the rest of the picture, see the full Haruki Murakami writing routine.

What You Can Steal

  • Set a number you can hit on a bad day. Murakami's ten pages works because it survives low-energy mornings.
  • Stop while you still have gas. Ending mid-flow makes the next session start faster.
  • Treat the quota as a floor and a ceiling. Don't overshoot on good days or undershoot on bad ones.
  • Think in pages, not perfection. The goal is consistent volume, which you can revise later.
