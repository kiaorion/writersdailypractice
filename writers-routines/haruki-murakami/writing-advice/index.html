Haruki Murakami's Best Writing Advice

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Murakami doesn't sell inspiration. His advice is closer to athletic training, built on showing up, concentrating hard, and lasting longer than the next person.

The Direct Answer
Train focus and endurance like muscles.
After talent, he ranks focus and endurance as a novelist's key qualities, per What I Talk About When I Talk About Running.

Focus First, Then Endurance

When Murakami gets asked what a novelist most needs, his answer starts with talent, which he calls a prerequisite no one can really control. Then he gets to the part writers can actually use. After talent, he ranks focus, the ability to pour your limited gifts onto whatever matters most in the moment. He concentrates for three or four hours every morning, shutting out everything else, and says strong focus can compensate for an erratic or even modest supply of talent.

Behind focus comes the quality he prizes most for a working novelist. A book isn't written in a week, so the real test is whether you can hold that concentration for half a year, a year, even two. He likens it to breathing, where focus is holding your breath and endurance is the slow, quiet breathing that lets you keep going at the same time.

After focus, the next most important thing for a novelist is, hands down, endurance. If you concentrate on writing three or four hours a day and feel tired after a week of this, you're not going to be able to write a long work.

Haruki Murakami, What I Talk About When I Talk About Running

The encouraging part is that he sees both as teachable. Unlike raw talent, focus and endurance can be acquired and sharpened through training, the same way running every day builds a runner's body. You add a stimulus, keep it up, and slowly raise the bar, almost without noticing the limit move. He points to Raymond Chandler, who sat at his desk and concentrated every single day even when he wrote nothing, as a writer quietly building the stamina the job requires.

Dig for Your Own Spring

Murakami is honest that he isn't one of those writers for whom sentences well up like water from a natural spring. Some authors finish a work with little visible effort. He isn't one of them. He describes having to pound the rock with a chisel and dig a deep hole before he reaches the source of his creativity, which means writing for him is closer to manual labor than to waiting for the muse.

He frames that as an advantage, oddly enough. A writer who relies on an effortless spring is in real trouble when it dries up. A writer who knows how to dig can always start a new hole and find a fresh vein. The skill of working for it is more durable than the gift of having it handed to you, which is the most useful idea in his advice for anyone who doesn't feel naturally gifted.

Pull it together and his advice reads less like inspiration and more like a training plan. Show up every day, concentrate hard for a few focused hours, and build the endurance to keep doing it for as long as the book demands. He treats finishing a novel as closer to manual labor than to inspiration, the kind of grueling, dynamic work that happens inside you even when nothing is moving on the outside. None of it depends on feeling ready, which is exactly why it works.

What You Can Steal

  • Train focus daily. A few hours of total concentration, repeated, beats rare bursts of genius.
  • Build endurance on purpose. Treat sustained writing as fitness you grow, not a mood you wait for.
  • Show up even when nothing comes. Like Chandler, sit at the desk whether the words arrive or not.
  • Dig instead of waiting. If sentences don't flow naturally, work at the rock until you hit the source.
