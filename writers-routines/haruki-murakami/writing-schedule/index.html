What Time of Day Does Haruki Murakami Write?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Murakami is a morning person who treats the early hours as sacred. When he's deep in a novel, he's at the desk before dawn and done with the hard part before most people have eaten breakfast.

The Direct Answer
Early morning, starting around 4 a.m.
He writes five to six hours from 4 a.m., per his 2004 Paris Review interview.

The 4 a.m. Start

When Murakami is working on a novel, he gets up at four in the morning and writes for five to six hours straight. He chases the quiet of that hour because it's when his focus is sharpest and nobody is awake to interrupt the work. By the time the rest of the household stirs, the day's hardest task is usually behind him.

The afternoon is built around the body, not the page. He runs ten kilometers or swims fifteen hundred meters, sometimes both, then reads and listens to music. He treats the exercise as part of the writing, a way to keep the physical engine strong enough for the long haul of a book. Then he winds down early and is in bed by nine, which is what makes a 4 a.m. alarm survivable night after night.

When I'm in writing mode for a novel, I get up at four a.m. and work for five to six hours. In the afternoon, I run for ten kilometers or swim for fifteen hundred meters (or do both), then I read a bit and listen to some music. I go to bed at nine p.m.

Haruki Murakami, Paris Review interview, 2004

Why the Repetition Matters

The clock isn't the point on its own. Murakami keeps to the same shape every single day because the sameness does something to his head. He calls it a form of mesmerism, a way to drop into a deeper state of mind that long fiction demands. The routine becomes a trigger, and the trigger does the work of getting him under.

He's been candid that holding this pattern for the six months to a year a novel takes is its own kind of training. He's compared writing a long book to survival training, where physical strength matters as much as artistic sensitivity. The schedule isn't there to look disciplined. It exists because a novel is a months-long effort, and you can't sustain that on bursts of inspiration.

In his running memoir he sketches the same lifestyle from a different angle. He describes himself as a clear morning person who gets up before five and goes to bed before ten, knocking out the important work first when his concentration is highest, then saving low-concentration errands and exercise for later. He's kept some version of this pattern going for decades, and he credits it for how much he's been able to produce.

There's a tradeoff he's open about. A life arranged around early mornings and early nights leaves little room for nightlife, and it can strain friendships when you keep turning down invitations. He decided long ago that the relationship he most needed to protect was the one with his readers, so the schedule won.

Two small notes keep the timing honest. The crisp 4 a.m. start is specifically how he describes his clock when he's deep in writing mode on a novel. In the more general account from his running memoir he puts wake-up at before five and bed at before ten, which is the everyday baseline he settles into between books. Either way, the principle holds. Early, quiet, and over before the world is fully awake. For the wider picture, see the full Haruki Murakami writing routine.

What You Can Steal

  • Front-load the hard work. Put your real writing in your sharpest hours, before the day fills up.
  • Protect a quiet window. Murakami's 4 a.m. start is really about uninterrupted time, not the number on the clock.
  • Keep the shape identical. Same start, same length, every day, so the ritual carries you instead of willpower.
  • End the day early. A 9 p.m. bedtime is what makes the 4 a.m. start sustainable.
