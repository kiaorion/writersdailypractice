How Did Hilary Mantel Write Wolf Hall?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Wolf Hall reads like Mantel simply remembered Tudor England, but the seamlessness took years and a lot of stationery. To keep hundreds of real people in the right place on the right day, she built a card catalogue and a wall of postcards before she wrote a line of the finished scenes.

The Direct Answer
She researched and drafted it over roughly five years, using index cards to pin every real figure to the historical record.
Mantel kept a card catalogue arranged by character so she never placed a historical figure somewhere they couldn't have been.

Five Years and a Card Catalogue

Mantel spent roughly five years researching and writing Wolf Hall, working to match her fiction to the historical record at every turn. Her main tool was a card catalogue arranged alphabetically by character. Each card tracked where a real historical figure was on the dates that mattered, so she could check at a glance whether a scene was even possible before she wrote it.

You really need to know, where is the Duke of Suffolk at the moment? You can't have him in London if he's supposed to be somewhere else.

Hilary Mantel, on researching Wolf Hall

The discipline was the point. Mantel wanted readers dropped inside Henry VIII's court with imperfect information and the wrong expectations, moving forward in time the way the real people did, without the comfort of hindsight. Getting the facts pinned down first was what let her write with that kind of confidence.

The Wall of Postcards

Alongside the catalogue, Mantel kept a large board in her kitchen while she worked on the Cromwell books. On it she laid out the shape of Thomas Cromwell's story, with the chronological line and the flashbacks worked out on postcards. Each postcard stood for a single scene, and everything that belonged to that scene got pinned behind it: small observations, descriptions, statistics, stray lines of dialogue. Only once a scene was fully assembled did she take it to the laptop and write it as fiction.

The method paid off. Wolf Hall won the 2009 Booker Prize, and Mantel carried the same approach through the trilogy. When the sequel, Bring Up the Bodies, won in 2012, she became the first woman to win the Booker Prize twice, and the first author to win it for two books in one series.

What You Can Steal

  • Build a fact spine before you invent. Mantel logged where every real person stood so the fiction never broke the record.
  • Give each scene its own card. One card per scene, with the details pinned behind it, let her see the whole shape at once.
  • Separate structure from prose. She fixed chronology and flashbacks on the board first, then moved to the laptop to write.
  • Research long, then trust it. Five years of groundwork let her write from inside the period instead of explaining it.
