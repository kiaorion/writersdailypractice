Hilary Mantel's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 23, 2026 | 10 min read

Hilary Mantel won the Booker Prize twice, for Wolf Hall in 2009 and Bring Up the Bodies in 2012, becoming the first woman and the first British author to do it. The achievement tends to overshadow the stranger facts of her working life: that she spent the better part of twenty years researching and circling the Cromwell trilogy before she could write it, that she did the writing early in the morning before her rational mind could argue against her choices, and that she did all of it in a body that had been fighting endometriosis since her twenties. The Booker feels like a recognition of genius. The routine is something more transferable: the story of how a writer works when time and health are both unreliable.

Most writers who study her work focus on the prose. The present-tense narration of the Cromwell books, the intimacy of the "he" construction that places you inside Cromwell's consciousness without announcing it, the way the sixteenth century feels immediate and alive rather than costumed. What produced that prose is worth understanding, because it came directly from how she worked. Writing first, before the critical voice woke up, was the condition the style required.

The Routine at a Glance

Wake Time
Early morning, writing first thing before the day's demands could crowd it out. She advised other writers to start before the rational mind fully takes over.
Writing Location
At home, often in Budleigh Salterton, Devon, where she and her husband Gerald McEwen lived for a period while she was completing the Cromwell trilogy.
Daily Output
She did not keep a fixed word-count quota. Progress on the Cromwell books was slow by design, often circling the same material for months before it found its shape.
Tools
Index cards and notebooks for research accumulation across years. Computer for drafting. She kept extensive notes on characters, dates, and relationships to manage the scale of the historical material.
Famous Ritual
Writing before the rational mind could intervene. In her Reith Lectures and in multiple interviews, she returned repeatedly to the idea that the first hours of the day, before self-criticism sets in, are the most valuable ones a writer has.
Books Written This Way
Wolf Hall, Bring Up the Bodies, The Mirror and the Light, Fludd, A Place of Greater Safety.

Writing Before the Rational Mind Wakes Up

Mantel was consistent on this point across decades of interviews and public talks. She believed the morning hours, specifically the time before full wakefulness had restored the internal critic, were when her best material came. Her Reith Lectures, delivered for the BBC in 2017 under the title "Hilary Mantel: The Day Is for the Living," describe the writer's relationship with the dead past as a kind of trance, and the conditions she recommended for accessing it were early morning ones: before the phone, before the obligations, before the thinking self had fully arrived.

This isn't unusual advice, but the reasoning behind it is. Mantel was talking about the psychological state that historical fiction requires, not the kind of productivity a business book writer would chase. To write from inside Thomas Cromwell's mind in the present tense, to make the reader feel they are moving through a room in 1535 and not reading about it, you have to suppress the part of yourself that knows the outcome, that keeps wanting to explain, that's tempted to ornament. The early morning was when that suppression came easiest. The rational part of her was still half-asleep, and the book could breathe.

In her 2003 memoir Giving Up the Ghost, Mantel writes with real candor about her relationship to her own mind, about the way illness and time and the body's demands had shaped her sense of what writing was. She describes a self that had learned to work around its own resistance rather than try to overpower it. The morning hours were part of that accommodation. If you wait for the right conditions, she suggests, you wait forever. You go to the desk when the desk is most likely to give something back.

Writers who want to produce historical fiction with any real immediacy need to understand what Mantel understood: the past doesn't feel present when you're standing at a scholarly distance from it. The prose has to carry the reader into it, and that kind of writing is harder to generate when the analytical brain is running at full capacity. The morning habit was a technical decision in service of a specific effect on the page, not a superstition.

Historical Research as Method: The Cromwell Trilogy

The Cromwell project is one of the most extraordinary long-game research efforts in twentieth-century British fiction. Mantel began thinking seriously about Thomas Cromwell in the 1980s, and Wolf Hall was published in 2009. That's roughly two decades of material accumulation, note-taking, index cards, and false starts before the book found the form it needed. The story she told in various interviews was that the research had to reach a certain density before the fictional imagination could begin to move freely inside it. You can't invent convincingly in a period you don't know. You have to know it so well that the invention feels like memory.

The index cards were the physical infrastructure of this. Mantel kept notes on historical figures, on the sequence of events at the Tudor court, on what contemporaries recorded about Cromwell's appearance, speech, and methods. She has described the notebooks and cards as a way of thinking rather than just a way of filing. The act of transferring something from a primary source onto a card was itself part of processing it, making it hers rather than someone else's fact.

What the research gave her was freedom. This sounds like a paradox until you've tried it. Writers who are anxious about getting the period wrong can't move. Every sentence becomes a question about accuracy. Mantel did her research until the anxiety dropped, until she knew Tudor England well enough to trust her instincts about what a scene needed. Then she could write fast and freely from inside the material rather than carefully around it. The years of preparation were what made the present-tense immediacy possible.

There's a lesson here that applies beyond historical fiction. The writers who produce the most alive prose in any genre tend to be the ones who know their subject well past the point where they could defend every claim in a footnote. Mantel's research method was extreme in its duration, but the principle scales down. Know the world before you write the story. The freedom to invent comes from the security of knowing.

Writing Through Chronic Pain

Mantel was diagnosed with endometriosis in her twenties, after years of being told by doctors that her symptoms were psychological. She wrote about this in detail in Giving Up the Ghost, her 2003 memoir, describing how the misdiagnosis and the treatment she eventually received had altered her body permanently, causing weight gain, joint problems, and near-constant pain. She lived with this from the time she was a young woman all the way through the decades when she was writing the books that made her famous.

The practical consequence was that she couldn't always work long hours at a desk. She had to fit her writing around what her body would permit on any given day, which made the morning-first habit something more than a preference. If she waited until she felt well enough to write at peak capacity, she might wait days at a time. The discipline of going to the desk early, before the day's pain had fully announced itself, was the only consistent window she had. She went when she could, rather than when she was ready.

What's striking about the Cromwell books, given all of this, is how much physical energy they contain. The Mirror and the Light, published in 2020, runs to nearly eight hundred pages. The prose is not the output of a writer who was conserving effort. Mantel was working around her limitations, finding the hours, and producing at a scale most healthy writers never reach. The illness didn't make the books possible, but it shaped the conditions that shaped the routine.

This part of her story gets underplayed when people discuss her career, possibly because writing-through-illness narratives tend to veer into either inspiration or complaint. Mantel avoided both. Her account of chronic pain in Giving Up the Ghost is some of the sharpest, most unsentimental writing in the memoir, and it connects directly to her understanding of the body's relationship to creative work. You learn things about time and attention when your body makes its claims unpredictable. Writers who haven't had that kind of teacher should take the lesson anyway: work in the window you have, and don't assume tomorrow's window will be wider.

Her Advice on Writing

Mantel gave writing advice publicly across her career in interviews, lectures, and in a widely-read piece in The Guardian called "Ten Rules for Writing Fiction," published in 2010. Several of her rules get at the same thing from different angles: get to the desk before the critical self arrives, trust the story to know more than the writer does at any given moment, and resist the urge to polish the opening at the expense of finishing the draft. Rule one of her ten rules was blunt: "Are you serious about this? Then get an accountant." A joke, but also not. She'd spent years watching writers talk about writing instead of doing it.

The advice that appears most often across her interviews is the instruction to write before the rational mind takes over. In the 2012 Paris Review interview conducted by Larissa MacFarquhar, she described the writing process as something closer to listening than to composing. She was waiting for Cromwell to show her what he did, not deciding it in advance. That kind of receptivity requires the analytical brain to stay quiet long enough to hear what's there, which is why the morning hours mattered to her. They were the hours before her own judgment could interfere.

She also had firm views on research and the imagination's relationship to it, and on the dangers of explaining too much. Her Cromwell books do very little explicit explaining. She doesn't stop the narrative to orient the reader in sixteenth-century politics, trusting instead that the story's momentum will carry them through. This came from a conviction she expressed in several interviews: that readers are smarter than most writers give them credit for, and that the explanatory instinct is usually the writer's anxiety speaking rather than the reader's need. Knowing which genre you're writing in matters here, because the tolerance for immersion versus explanation varies. Mantel's view was that historical fiction earns the most trust from readers when it commits completely to the period's logic and lets the reader catch up.

Her ten rules in The Guardian are worth reading in full, but the spine of her advice is one idea: the writing comes first, the understanding of what you've written comes after. Don't wait to know what you're doing. Start, and you'll find out.

Hilary Mantel's Morning Writing Routine

Mantel's morning writing practice was less a fixed schedule than a philosophy about when the mind is most usable. She went to her desk early, before email and obligations could establish themselves, and she worked from that first-morning state of partial consciousness that she found more productive than the fully alert one. The Reith Lectures, which she delivered on BBC Radio 4 in 2017, show how deeply she'd thought about the mechanics of historical imagination, about what it means to make the past alive in prose, and those lectures are the best record of the mental state she was cultivating when she went to the desk each morning.

During the years she was completing the Cromwell trilogy, her husband Gerald McEwen managed much of the practical administration of their household, a fact she acknowledged in interviews as something that made the long project possible. The scale of the research and the writing required protection from interruption. She was not working with a Morrison-style compressed window of two predawn hours. She was working with days she'd arranged, over time, to give the project what it needed. The morning was the entry point, but the writing could run as long as the material allowed.

The physical reality of chronic illness meant her output was irregular by necessity. There were days when she could write for several hours and days when she couldn't manage much at all. What held the practice together across the good days and bad ones was the habit of going to the desk first, before anything else could claim the morning. Writing first meant making the work the day's first commitment rather than fitting it into whatever time remained after the other commitments had taken their share. By the time The Mirror and the Light appeared in March 2020, Mantel had been living with Cromwell for over three decades. The morning practice, repeated across all those years, is what accumulated into those eight hundred pages.

Sources

The primary sources for Mantel's writing practice are her 2003 memoir Giving Up the Ghost (Fourth Estate), which discusses her illness, her early writing life, and her relationship to her own mind; her 2017 BBC Reith Lectures, "Hilary Mantel: The Day Is for the Living," available via the BBC website, in which she discusses the historical imagination and the conditions it requires; and the Art of Fiction No. 226 interview with Larissa MacFarquhar in The Paris Review, published in 2012, where she discusses the Cromwell project, her research method, and her approach to writing. Her "Ten Rules for Writing Fiction" piece in The Guardian (February 2010) and her collected Reith Lectures published as Mantel Pieces (2020) by John Murray are also primary sources. Secondary context comes from profiles in The New Yorker, The Guardian, and The Times across the years of the trilogy's publication.

What You Can Steal

Mantel's routine is most useful for writers working on large, slow projects that require both deep research and a particular quality of imaginative absorption. Here's what transfers:

  • Go to the desk before you're fully awake. The rational, critical self wakes up slower than the imaginative one. If you write in that first window, you're ahead of your own resistance. This is especially true on days when the material feels hard.
  • Do the research until the anxiety drops. Mantel spent decades on the Cromwell material before she could write freely inside it. Your project probably doesn't require decades, but it requires enough knowledge that invention feels natural rather than risky. If you're still anxious about getting it wrong, you haven't done enough research yet.
  • Use index cards or some physical system for accumulating material over time. A searchable digital system works fine, but the principle is the same: the research has to live somewhere your imagination can browse. A stack of cards you can shuffle is different from a folder you have to remember to open.
  • Write in the window you have, not the window you'd prefer. Mantel wrote around chronic pain by going early, when her body was most cooperative. If your life isn't giving you three-hour uninterrupted stretches, stop waiting for them. The hour you have right now is the one that produces the book.
  • Trust the story to be smarter than you are. Mantel described waiting for Cromwell to show her what happened next rather than deciding it herself. The analytical brain is useful for revision. In first draft, it usually just blocks traffic. Write past your own understanding of what the scene is doing, and trust that the understanding will arrive later.
