Isaac Asimov's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 14, 2026 | 9 min read

The number is almost impossible to sit with: somewhere close to 500 published books, covering science fiction, popular science, history, the Bible, Shakespeare, biochemistry, mathematics, and a dozen other fields. Isaac Asimov wrote across nine of the ten major categories of the Dewey Decimal System. He started writing seriously in the 1930s and kept going until he died in 1992. By the time most writers are congratulating themselves on finishing their second novel, Asimov had already written a hundred books and was still picking up speed.

The volume was possible because of a routine so consistent and so deliberately structured that writing became, as he put it himself, his greatest pleasure. The talent was real, but the talent alone wouldn't have produced 500 books. Asimov treated every day at the typewriter like a factory shift. He showed up, he worked, he didn't wait for anything. The books came because there was nowhere else he'd rather be, and because he'd built a life with no exits to distraction.

Profile

The Routine at a Glance

Wake Time
Around 6:00 a.m., sometimes earlier. He was at his typewriter by 7:30 a.m. most mornings and considered any later start a minor failure.
Writing Location
A windowless office in his apartment on West 66th Street in New York, which he chose specifically to eliminate the temptation to look outside. The room had his typewriter, his books, and nothing else worth watching.
Daily Output
Typically between 1,000 and 4,000 words a day, depending on the project. He worked fast by any standard. On good days he could produce a complete short story before lunch.
Tools
Manual typewriter for decades, then an IBM electric, and finally a word processor in his later years. He typed his own drafts, produced his own clean copies, and rarely revised extensively because he wrote slowly enough in his mind to get it right the first time through.
Famous Ritual
Seven days a week, no exceptions. Asimov did not take days off from writing. He wrote on holidays, while traveling, on his birthday. If he missed a day, he felt the loss. Weekends had no separate meaning in his calendar.
Books Written This Way
Foundation, I, Robot, The Gods Themselves, The Caves of Steel, and roughly 490 other books across fiction and nonfiction.

Writing as a Factory Job

Asimov was explicit, in both of his major autobiographies and in dozens of interviews, that he approached writing the way a tradesman approaches a craft. He showed up at a fixed time, he worked for a fixed number of hours, and he stopped when the day's output was done. There was no mysticism attached to it, no talk of the muse or of waiting for the right state of mind. Writing was work, and work happened on a schedule.

In In Memory Yet Green (1979), his first autobiography covering his life through 1954, Asimov described how early this attitude took root. Even as a young man in Brooklyn, writing his first science fiction stories on a typewriter set up in the family candy store, he was already producing steadily rather than sporadically. The stories went out to magazines, came back rejected, went out again. He kept records of submissions and rejections. He tracked what he'd sent where. The whole operation had the character of a small business, run with the discipline of someone who intended to make it work.

The factory mentality scaled directly into his output. By the time he was publishing at full speed in the 1950s and 1960s, Asimov was keeping multiple projects in motion simultaneously, switching between them as mood or deadline demanded, always with something in the typewriter. He didn't labor over any single book to the exclusion of everything else. If a chapter wasn't going well, he'd move to a different project and come back. The result was that almost every day produced something finished or nearly finished, which in turn kept the psychological momentum going. Stopping output, for Asimov, would have felt like stopping breathing.

Writers who romanticize the slow, agonized production of a single masterpiece tend to look at Asimov's volume and assume the quality must have suffered for it. Some of it did. Asimov wrote a lot of middling nonfiction alongside the great fiction. But The Gods Themselves, which won both the Hugo and Nebula Awards in 1973, was written inside that same factory routine. The discipline didn't produce uniform mediocrity. It produced a body of work large enough to contain both the failures and the genuine classics.

The Windowless Room

Asimov's office in his West 66th Street apartment had no windows by design. This wasn't an accident of the building layout. He chose the arrangement deliberately and described it with satisfaction in his memoirs. No windows meant no weather to watch, no street to observe, no sky to drift toward when the paragraph got difficult. The only thing in the room worth paying attention to was the page in the typewriter.

Most writers fetishize their workspace in the opposite direction. The ocean view, the garden window, the coffee shop with just the right ambient noise. Asimov's view was that all of that was distraction wearing a romantic disguise. A beautiful view is lovely, but it's lovely in a way that competes with the work. He wanted a space that made the work the most interesting thing available, and the simplest way to accomplish that was to make everything else boring.

The windowless office is an extreme version of something every productive writer eventually figures out: the environment has to be designed around the writing, not around what's comfortable to sit in or pleasant to look at. Some writers accomplish this through headphones. Some through software that blocks the internet. Asimov accomplished it architecturally, by choosing a room where there was literally nothing to look at except what he was creating. There's a logic to this that most productivity advice dances around without quite saying directly: distraction management works better when it's structural rather than willpower-based. He didn't have to resist the view. There was no view.

Why He Never Waited for Inspiration

Asimov was openly contemptuous of writers who waited to feel inspired before they sat down. In I. Asimov: A Memoir (1994), published the year after his death, he returned to this theme in a way that reads less like advice and more like a personal conviction he'd held so long it had become axiomatic. Writing was its own inspiration. You couldn't think your way into a good story from the outside. You had to get into the work and let the work generate its own momentum.

This isn't a new idea. Most working writers land on some version of it eventually, usually after years of waiting for inspiration and discovering that it doesn't arrive on schedule. What was unusual about Asimov was how early he reached this conclusion and how strictly he lived by it. He wasn't a writer who disciplined himself reluctantly into a daily practice. He seems to have genuinely preferred being at the typewriter to almost anything else, which meant the practice didn't require much discipline at all. The habit was its own reward.

"I write for the same reason I breathe, because if I didn't, I would die."

The quote above comes from Asimov's own statements about his relationship to writing and appears in accounts of his life from multiple sources. It's worth taking literally. He wasn't being melodramatic. When health problems in the late 1970s and 1980s limited his ability to travel and engage with the world the way he once had, he wrote more, not less. The typewriter was where he lived. The books were evidence of a life fully inhabited.

Aspiring writers who wait for inspiration are often really waiting for the fear to go away. The blank page is uncomfortable, and waiting feels safer than starting. Asimov's approach was to eliminate the waiting state entirely. Sit down. Type. The fear doesn't go away before you start. It goes away, if it goes away at all, after you're already several paragraphs in. The routine made that transition automatic.

Multiple Projects at Once

One of the less-discussed features of Asimov's routine was his habit of working on several books simultaneously. At any given point in his career he might have a science fiction novel in progress, two or three nonfiction books at various stages, a collection of short stories coming together, and correspondence with editors scattered across multiple publishers. He kept track of all of it. His filing system was legendary among the people who knew him, and his ability to re-enter a project after weeks away from it was something he attributed to keeping thorough notes and writing clean, clear prose that was easy to pick back up.

The multiple-projects approach solved a problem that plagues writers who work on one thing at a time. When a chapter isn't going well, the only move available is to sit there until it goes well. Asimov's alternative was to switch. If the robot novel was stuck, he'd go write a chapter of the biochemistry guide. If the biochemistry guide felt flat, he'd draft a few pages of the history of the Byzantine Empire. The variety kept the output steady even when any individual project hit resistance. He was always building on something, which meant he was always accumulating momentum.

For writers interested in science fiction writing, the Asimov model is worth studying in detail because it produced the most influential body of work the genre has ever seen, and it did so through a routine that was almost aggressively ordinary. No wilderness retreats, no artists' colonies, no waiting for the vision. Just a man in a windowless room, typing fast, switching projects when one got stuck, and coming back seven days later to do it again.

The implication for your own practice is that breadth doesn't have to come at the expense of depth. Asimov was not a shallow thinker working quickly across thin subjects. The Foundation series is genuinely complex. The Gods Themselves has more ideas in it than most writers fit into a decade of work. He went deep because he spent so much time at the typewriter that depth accumulated naturally. Volume and quality, in his case, were products of the same discipline rather than opposites pulling against each other.

Isaac Asimov's Daily Writing Routine

By his own account in In Joy Still Felt (1980), his second autobiography covering 1954 to 1978, Asimov was typically at his typewriter by seven-thirty in the morning and stayed there until well into the evening, often working past ten at night. The hours varied by project and by what else the day demanded, but the core habit was unbroken: he sat down every day, including Sundays, including holidays, including days when he was sick or tired or had already produced more words that morning than most writers manage in a week. Writing was the baseline condition, not something he reserved for favorable circumstances.

He also didn't revise much by the standards of most literary writers. This wasn't sloppiness. He described thinking through his sentences before he typed them, composing in his head at a pace slightly slower than his fingers could execute on the keyboard, so that by the time the words appeared on the page they were already close to what he wanted. The clean first drafts weren't the product of reckless speed. They were the product of mental preparation that happened before the typing began. He used the time between typewriter sessions, eating dinner, riding the elevator, lying awake in the early morning, to work out the next section in his mind.

The typewriter itself was important to him. He wrote about his relationship to various machines with something close to affection. The manual typewriter of his early career gave way to an IBM Selectric, and eventually in the 1980s he adopted a word processor, which he found liberating in practical terms but which he also described with slight ambivalence. The machine was a tool, and the tool mattered because it mediated the connection between his mind and the page. Whatever he was typing on, he typed fast. Visitors to his office described the sound of sustained, rapid keystrokes, the sound of a man for whom there was no friction between the thought and the word.

Knowing your writing genre and the conventions of your field mattered to Asimov too, because he wrote across so many of them. He understood what a science fiction story needed structurally and what a popular science book needed in terms of pacing and explanation. The variety of his output was possible partly because he'd internalized enough conventions that he could move between genres without losing his footing. The routine gave him the hours. The craft knowledge gave him the direction once he sat down.

Sources

The primary sources for Asimov's writing habits are his own autobiographies. In Memory Yet Green (Doubleday, 1979) covers his life from 1920 to 1954 and contains detailed accounts of how he structured his early writing years, his habits around submission and rejection, and how the routine developed while he was still a young man. In Joy Still Felt (Doubleday, 1980) covers 1954 to 1978, the period when his output scaled into its most productive phase, and includes his accounts of the New York apartment, the windowless office, and the daily schedule. I. Asimov: A Memoir (Doubleday, 1994), published posthumously, is a more reflective look at his life and work and contains his clearest statements on his relationship to writing as a daily practice, including his views on inspiration, discipline, and volume. These three books are unusually reliable sources because Asimov was a meticulous autobiographer who took facts seriously and wrote about his own habits with the same precision he brought to his nonfiction.

What You Can Steal

Asimov's routine is the most extreme version of the factory approach in the literary record. Most writers won't replicate it exactly, but the underlying principles transfer across any scale:

  • Show up seven days a week, or as close to it as your life allows. Asimov didn't reserve writing for weekdays or for when inspiration struck. The daily habit was what made the volume possible, and the volume was what made the great books statistically likely.
  • Design your workspace to make writing the most interesting thing available. The windowless office was a distraction management strategy, not a decorating preference. Whatever your version of that looks like, the goal is a space where starting feels easier than not starting.
  • Work on more than one project at a time. When one gets stuck, the others keep moving. The output stays consistent even when any single book is giving you trouble, and consistent output is the only thing that actually builds a body of work over years.
  • Compose in your head before you type. Asimov's clean first drafts came from thinking through sentences before executing them, not from a gift for effortless prose. Time spent mentally drafting while doing something else is writing time, even if there's no keyboard in front of you.
  • Treat the writing hour as non-negotiable, not as something to earn. Asimov didn't write after he'd finished everything else. He wrote first, and everything else arranged itself around that. The routine only held up for sixty years because it had structural priority in his day, not because the days were always easy.
