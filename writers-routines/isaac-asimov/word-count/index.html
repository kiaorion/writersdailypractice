How Much Did Isaac Asimov Write a Day?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Asimov never kept a word chart the way Hemingway famously did, and he didn't need one. He measured himself in finished books instead of daily tallies, and the books stacked up fast, close to 500 of them by the end of his life. So the honest answer to how much he wrote a day takes a little arithmetic.

The Direct Answer
By most estimates, several thousand words on a working day, often in the 4,000 to 5,000 range.
He never advertised a strict daily quota. That figure is reverse-engineered from his output and his typing speed, which he clocked himself at 90 words a minute.

The Math Behind the Number

One thing to be clear about up front, Asimov never announced a Hemingway-style daily target. He didn't sit down aiming for a set number and stop the moment he hit it. The figures people quote are reverse-engineered from two things we can actually verify, the size of his catalog and the speed of his hands on the keys.

He tracked books, not words on an index card, and the books kept coming. Work backward from his catalog, somewhere around 500 titles over roughly forty productive years, and the daily average alone lands well into the thousands. On a good day, when the writing was flowing and nothing interrupted him, his output climbed into the four-to-five-thousand-word range and sometimes past it.

The typing was a big part of it. Asimov was genuinely fast, and he didn't agonize over individual sentences. He put the words down, kept his hands moving, and left the tidying for a later pass. It's worth remembering these were finished, publishable words, not throwaway drafts. A typical Asimov book ran in the neighborhood of 70,000 words, and he turned them out year after year with no ghostwriter and no research team doing the heavy lifting for him. When the pace dipped, it was usually because a subject needed reading and fact-checking first, which happened often in his science books. The fiction, which mostly lived in his head already, tended to pour out faster.

I type 90 words per minute on the typewriter; I type 100 words per minute on the word processor.

Isaac Asimov

Volume Over Polish

At 90 words a minute, a single uninterrupted hour would be more than 5,000 words of raw typing. Asimov obviously didn't hold that pace, since thinking eats time and revision eats more. What the speed really bought him was momentum. He'd rather get a complete draft down and clean it up afterward than polish one paragraph all afternoon. Stretched across a writing day that could run fourteen hours, even broken bursts of that speed pile into a serious page count.

It helps to picture what he skipped. He moved past a rough opening line instead of hunting for a perfect one, and he started today's chapter without rereading yesterday's ten times first. He kept the momentum pointed forward, and that forward motion is what separates an ordinary writer from a prolific one.

None of it required a gift for speed that ordinary writers lack. Asimov argued the real difference was simpler than that. A prolific writer, in his view, is just someone who writes, while plenty of would-be writers spend their energy talking about the book they mean to start someday. He spent that same energy at the keys instead, day after day, and the totals took care of themselves.

What You Can Steal

  • Judge yourself by finished work. Asimov counted books shipped, not numbers on a chart.
  • Reduce the friction. Learning to type fast shortened the distance between his brain and the page.
  • Draft now, fix later. His speed came from refusing to perfect every line on the first pass.
  • Give the work hours, not minutes. Those page counts came from a long day, not a lucky sprint.
