How Many Words a Day Did Jack Kerouac Write?

Kia Orion | August 2, 2026 | 5 min read

The scroll gives us the only Kerouac number anyone remembers, and the arithmetic behind it is startling. It is also misleading, because those three weeks in April 1951 sat on top of three years of earlier drafts.

The Direct Answer
Roughly 6,000 words a day for three weeks in April 1951.
The On the Road scroll draft is commonly cited at around 125,000 words, typed on a 120-foot roll of taped tracing paper in about three weeks. That pace was a sprint he repeated a handful of times, never a daily habit.

Doing the Scroll Math

In April 1951, in a Manhattan apartment, Kerouac fed a 120-foot roll of taped tracing paper into his typewriter and produced the draft that became On the Road. He typed it single-spaced, with no margins and no paragraph breaks, over roughly three weeks. The draft is commonly cited at around 125,000 words.

Divide it out and you land near 6,000 words a day, sustained for twenty days. Stephen King's stated target is 2,000. Hemingway averaged around 500. For three straight weeks Kerouac was running at triple King and twelve times Hemingway.

Treat the 6,000 as derived rather than reported. Kerouac never published a daily count, and the 125,000 figure shifts a little depending on which scholar you read. The order of magnitude holds up, which is why the story has outlived almost everything else about him.

The Three Weeks Were Not the Whole Book

Howard Cunnell's introduction to the 2007 edition, On the Road: The Original Scroll, is the source that punctures the legend. Kerouac had been working on versions of the material since 1948, across at least two substantially different earlier drafts. The scroll was a transcription sprint more than an act of invention. Three years of notebooks and abandoned manuscripts sat behind it, and the speed came from knowing the story cold.

Then came six more years. He kept revising, cutting sections and changing names, and Viking did not publish until 1957. The famous number covers the fastest three weeks of a nine-year project, which is the detail the myth always drops.

He Did It Again, Three More Times

The scroll was no one-off. Grove Press describes The Subterraneans as written across three days and three nights. The Dharma Bums came out of a stretch between 26 November and 7 December 1957, typed onto a ten-foot teleprinter roll in the same continuous format. Big Sur was composed over ten days in the autumn of 1961, also on a roll.

Four books, four sprints, spread across a decade. The pattern is a writer who could not work at a steady daily rate and did not try. He accumulated material in notebooks for months or years, then emptied himself onto the roll in one burst. Between bursts the output sat closer to zero than to 6,000, and the drinking filled a good deal of the gap.

What You Can Steal

  • A sprint runs on stored material. Kerouac's three weeks worked because three years of notebooks preceded them. A blank-brained sprint just produces bad pages faster.
  • Delete the friction that breaks flow. The taped roll existed so he would never stop to change a sheet of paper. Find your equivalent and remove it.
  • Fast drafting and fast publishing are different things. Three weeks of typing, six years of revision, then a book.
  • A sprint is not a schedule. He wrote four books this way in a decade and very little in between. Judge the method by the decade.
