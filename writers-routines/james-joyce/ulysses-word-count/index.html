Ulysses Word Count: How Long Is Joyce's Novel?

Kia Orion | August 2, 2026 | 5 min read

Ulysses has a reputation for being unfinishable, and length is part of that. At roughly 265,000 words it's about three standard novels in one volume, and the difficulty per word is higher than almost anything else in English.

The Direct Answer
Roughly 265,000 words.
The most repeated figure is 265,222 words, from Broke by Books' survey of novel lengths, and general reference sources round it to 265,000. Counts move between editions because the text itself is contested. Joyce wrote the novel from 1914 to 1921, and Shakespeare and Company published it in Paris on 2 February 1922 at 732 pages.

The Number, and Why Ulysses Resists One

265,222 words is the count that circulates most widely, and rounded figures near 265,000 show up everywhere else. The first edition ran 732 pages across eighteen episodes. Any published count is a digital count of one particular edition, because no publisher prints an official one.

Ulysses varies more than most books because the text is genuinely unsettled. The 1922 first edition carried well over 2,000 errors, and later editors had to guess which oddities were Joyce's deliberate work and which were the printer's. Hans Walter Gabler's 1984 corrected edition set off an argument among Joyce scholars that has never really closed. Two respectable editions can differ by more than formatting.

The more revealing number is the vocabulary. Joyce works with roughly 30,030 distinct words across those 265,000. A 90,000 word commercial novel typically runs on a small fraction of that range. The ratio is why the book reads slowly even for people who know what's happening.

Seven Years for 265,000 Words

Joyce began in Trieste in 1914 and finished in Paris in October 1921. The novel ran serially in the American journal The Little Review from March 1918 to December 1920, until an obscenity prosecution shut the serialisation down. Seven years for 265,000 words averages out to about 104 words a day.

Frank Budgen, who watched Joyce work in Zurich, recorded a morning when Joyce turned up pleased with himself. He had written two sentences that day. The words had been there from the start, he explained. The day had gone into the order.

That's the method behind the count. Joyce kept notebooks alongside the manuscripts, collecting phrases, proper names, overheard scraps and foreign words, then dropping them into the developing text wherever a passage needed density or a particular sound. The book accumulated in layers instead of running forward in a line, which is exactly why a seven year timeline produced a hundred word daily average and a novel nobody has managed to imitate.

I have the words already. What I am seeking is the perfect order of words in the sentence.

James Joyce, recorded by Frank Budgen in James Joyce and the Making of Ulysses (1934)

How Ulysses Compares

Literary fiction is bought at 80,000 to 100,000 words. Ulysses is about three of those. Against the real giants it's mid-sized: War and Peace runs somewhere past 560,000, The Lord of the Rings lands near 480,000, and Stephen King's It is around 445,000. What sets Joyce apart is density rather than bulk.

For benchmarking, the pace is the number to take. A writer producing 500 words a day would clear 265,000 in about eighteen months. Joyce needed seven years because he was reorganising, layering and inserting rather than drafting forward. If your own project is moving at a hundred words a day, the count by itself says very little. The better question is whether your method is doing work the number can't show.

What You Can Steal

  • Track vocabulary, not just volume. Joyce used about 30,030 distinct words in 265,000. Density is a craft decision you can make at any length.
  • Keep a collection notebook running beside the draft. Phrases, names and overheard lines gathered in parallel gave Joyce material to insert when a passage went thin.
  • A slow daily average can still finish a big book. A hundred words a day for seven years is Ulysses. Consistency beats bursts over a long project.
  • Decide whether you are drafting or layering. The two work at different speeds, and judging a layering process by drafting numbers will only make you feel behind.
