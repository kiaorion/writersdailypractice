J.K. Rowling's Best Writing Advice

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Rowling distrusts tidy rules for writing and says so on her own website. What she offers instead is blunter and more useful: read like your life depends on it, accept that most of the job is grind, and learn to take a punch.

The Direct Answer
Read constantly, expect perspiration over inspiration, and survive rejection.
Her most consistent advice, set out on jkrowling.com, centers on reading, discipline, and resilience rather than tricks or formulas.

She Hates Writing Rules

On her own site, Rowling opens by saying she can't stand lists of must-dos, in writing or in life. That's worth holding onto before anyone hands you a numbered system. Her guidance isn't a method to copy. It's a description of what the work actually demands, drawn from someone who spent years failing at it before she didn't. She'd rather you find your own way of working than follow hers.

Read, Then Read More

Her first real instruction is simple and non-negotiable. You can't be a good writer without being a devoted reader. Everything else she says assumes you've already filled your head with thousands of other people's sentences.

There's no shortcut around this. Wide reading is how you absorb rhythm, structure, and what a finished book is supposed to feel like from the inside. It teaches you what's been done, what's worn out, and where the gaps are. Writers who don't read tend to reinvent clumsy versions of things better books solved long ago.

The Job Is Mostly Perspiration

Rowling is clear that writing is far more perspiration than inspiration. The image of the author waiting for a muse to descend is a comforting fantasy and a useless one. The real work is showing up and producing words on days when nothing feels magical, then doing it again the next day, and the day after that.

Her own track record backs this up. The first book took years to finish while she was raising a child with little money, grabbing whatever writing time she could. None of that happens on inspiration alone. Discipline, the willingness to sit down and grind out pages whether or not you feel like it, is what gets a manuscript over the line.

She also values planning as part of that discipline. The detailed grids and notebooks behind the Potter books weren't decoration. They were the scaffolding that let her keep seven interlocking plots straight across thousands of pages, and they were built by doing the unglamorous work of sketching, listing, and tracking long before the fun parts got written.

Learn to Survive Rejection

She's said openly that rejection and criticism are part of a writer's life. Her own first book was turned down by a string of publishers before Bloomsbury took it on. The skill that mattered wasn't avoiding rejection, which is impossible, but recovering from it fast enough to keep going. She also draws a line between feedback that's worth acting on and noise that isn't, and that distinction is itself part of the advice.

Informed feedback is useful and necessary, but some of the greatest writers were rejected multiple times. Being able to pick yourself up and keep going is invaluable if you're to survive your work being publicly assessed.

What You Can Steal

  • Read more than you write. She treats reading as the foundation, not a luxury.
  • Stop waiting for inspiration. Treat writing as a job you clock into, because that's mostly what it is.
  • Separate useful feedback from noise. Take informed criticism, ignore the rest, keep moving.
  • Plan to be rejected. Build the habit of picking yourself up so the first no doesn't end the project.
