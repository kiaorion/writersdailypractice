How Much Does Joyce Carol Oates Actually Write?

Kia Orion | August 2, 2026 | 5 min read

Ask how many words a day Joyce Carol Oates writes and you will find plenty of answers online, none of which came from her. Across six decades of interviews she has never given a daily word count or a page quota. What she has given, repeatedly, is a correction: she is not the fast writer everyone assumes.

The Direct Answer
No daily word count. Roughly 250 books across six decades.
Oates has never published a words-per-day figure. Her bibliography runs to about 65 novels and close to 50 story collections, and she has said in multiple interviews that she can spend a full day on a single page.

The Number She Has Never Given

The Paris Review put the schedule question to her directly in its Art of Fiction interview, published in 1978. Her answer named a time of day and nothing else. No target and no floor. Twelve years later, in a 1990 conversation with BOMB, she was asked about her reputation for speed and pushed back on it hard.

The closest thing to a number in the record comes from a 1969 Commonweal interview, where she said she wrote twenty to twenty-five pages when the evening went well, then immediately added that she did not do that every day. Maybe a couple of days a week. That describes a good session. Reading it as a quota gets her wrong.

I have a reputation for writing fast, but I don't. I can spend an entire day on one page; I keep sifting it through till I get it right.

Joyce Carol Oates, interview with BOMB, 1990

She said much the same thing to The Guardian in 2016, describing the evolution of a novel as slow and telling the interviewer that people think she writes quickly when she does not. Two interviews, twenty-six years apart, making the same correction.

What the Career Total Actually Looks Like

The volume is real even if the daily number is imaginary. Her bibliography holds roughly 65 novels published under her own name and the pseudonyms Rosamond Smith and Lauren Kelly, alongside close to 50 short story collections, a dozen novellas, a dozen poetry collections, and more than twenty books of essays and memoir. Celestial Timepiece, the Oates bibliography maintained by librarian Randy Souther at the University of San Francisco, counted around 255 book entries as of May 2025.

Wikipedia's summary line says 58 novels and carries no citation, which is worth knowing if you see that figure quoted at you. Its own bibliography page lists more. Counts for a writer this productive drift depending on whether you fold in novellas, YA titles, and the pseudonymous books, so treat any single total as approximate.

Blonde (2000) is the longest of them at 738 pages in the Ecco first edition, and she has said the manuscript ran to about 1,400 pages before cuts. No publisher-issued word count exists for it or for any of her novels. The word counts you will find on aggregator sites are page counts multiplied by 250, which is a guess wearing the costume of data.

Longhand Is Why a Daily Number Would Mislead

Oates drafts by hand before she types. She told the Paris Review in 1978 that her last several novels had been written in longhand first, and that the typewriter had come to feel like a formal, impersonal object. In the BOMB interview she compared the cycle of writing, typing, and retyping to embroidery.

A longhand page and a typed page are not the same unit of work, which is part of why she measures in hours and finished books. A day spent sifting one page through four passes produces almost nothing countable and can still be the day that saves the chapter. A word count would flatter the wrong days.

The output arrives through duration rather than pace. Sixty-plus years of morning sessions, held around whatever her Princeton teaching week demanded, accumulate on their own without anybody keeping score.

What You Can Steal

  • Stop looking for her number, because there isn't one. Any site quoting a Joyce Carol Oates words-per-day figure invented it. The honest answer is that she works in hours and lets the count fall where it falls.
  • Volume can come from duration instead of speed. Six decades of daily morning sessions will out-produce almost any sprint, and Oates is the proof.
  • Draft by hand if revision is where your work improves. Longhand slows the first pass, which is exactly what makes the second pass shorter.
  • Judge the day by whether the page got better. Oates will spend a whole day sifting one page. A word count cannot see that kind of progress, so keep a measure that can.
