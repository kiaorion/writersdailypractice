Khaled Hosseini's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 23, 2026 | 9 min read

In 2001, Khaled Hosseini was waking up somewhere around five in the morning to write a novel he thought nobody would ever publish. He was a practicing internist at Kaiser Permanente in Mountain View, California. His days belonged to patients. The only hours that didn't were the ones before the clinic opened, so he took them, hunched over a kitchen table in the dark, writing the book that would become The Kite Runner.

The story of how that book came to exist is one of the more instructive things in contemporary fiction. Hosseini wasn't a person with an ideal writing life. He had a demanding day job, a wife, a young child, and a manuscript he was drafting for himself because he wanted to read it, not because he had any reason to believe a publisher would want to print it. What made the routine work was a decision, made repeatedly, to show up before the day had a chance to ask anything else of him.

Profile

The Routine at a Glance

Wake Time
Between 4:30 and 5:00 a.m. During the writing of The Kite Runner, he described waking at about "4:45, 5:00 in the morning" and writing for roughly three hours before heading to the clinic. (Academy of Achievement interview, 2008; HuffPost, 2013)
Writing Location
The kitchen table for The Kite Runner. For A Thousand Splendid Suns, a rented "little bunker" near his home: a single room with a chair and a desk, at roughly $300 a month. Now a small home office, door closed, shades drawn.
Daily Output
He aims for two to three pages per day. His minimum bar for a productive session: at least three good sentences and a clear idea of what comes next. He doesn't track word counts.
Tools
He writes on a computer and says he cannot write longhand. He also can't write with music playing. The desk in his current office has a model 1965 Mustang on it, his children's drawings near the monitor, and an Afghan rug as a mouse pad.
Famous Ritual
Writing the first draft fast, privately, and for himself alone. Nobody reads it until it's at least half done. He has said writing a first draft is "difficult and laborious" and almost always disappointing. The actual work happens in revision, across multiple complete drafts.
Books Written This Way
The Kite Runner (2003), A Thousand Splendid Suns (2007), And the Mountains Echoed (2013), Sea Prayer (2018).

Writing Before the Day Job

Hosseini spent most of 2001 writing The Kite Runner in three-hour windows before his clinic day started. In a 2013 piece for HuffPost, he described the math plainly: "I cut down on my sleeping time. I awoke daily at 5 AM, wrote from then until roughly 8 AM." The Academy of Achievement interview from 2008 puts the wake time slightly earlier, around 4:45. Either way, the pattern was the same. Up before dawn, a few hours at the table, then off to see patients.

He kept this up for roughly a year. The Kite Runner was published in May 2003, and Hosseini kept practicing medicine for another eighteen months after that, until the demands of the book tour and his second novel made it impossible to be fully present with patients. He formally left medicine in December 2004. But the novel that launched his career was written inside the constraints of a working physician's schedule, before anyone could claim his attention.

The circumstance has a lot in common with Toni Morrison writing at four in the morning before her sons woke up, or Franz Kafka keeping his notebooks in the hours after his insurance office closed. The pre-existing obligation isn't an obstacle to the writing life; it's the container that gives the writing its shape. When time is genuinely limited, you stop waiting for inspiration. You go to the table and you work.

What's easy to overlook is that these early hours were Hosseini's natural relationship with the work from the start, not a temporary sacrifice. He told the Academy of Achievement that medical training had given him a particular kind of discipline. You don't wait to feel ready. You don't reschedule because your energy is low. You show up at the patient's room, and you do your job. He brought the same ethic to the kitchen table. The manuscript was the patient. You showed up.

The Private First Draft

When Hosseini talks about first drafts, the word he uses is "disappointing." In his 2012 interview with The Daily Beast, he said: "I find that writing a first draft is very difficult and laborious. It is also often quite disappointing. It hardly ever turns out to be what I thought it was, and it usually falls quite short of the ideal I held in my mind." He's been consistent about this across a decade of interviews. The first draft is a sketch. It exists so he has something to revise, and revision is where the actual writing happens.

The practical implication is that he protects first drafts by keeping them private. His wife Roya, an investment lawyer and his primary reader, doesn't see the manuscript until it's at least half finished. Other readers come even later. The logic is simple: early feedback on a rough draft plants seeds of doubt that can kill a book before it has a chance to become what it's trying to be. The first draft needs to stay irrational, private, and fast, because the moment external judgment enters the room, the writer starts writing for someone else instead of the story.

He also doesn't outline. In the Daily Beast piece, he said he doesn't find outlining useful and doesn't like "the way it boxes me in." He described his process as walking through a very dark room with a flashlight that only shows three feet ahead. He has no idea where the room ends or what's in the corners. He just follows the light. This means he'll sometimes spend six weeks writing seventy-five pages that don't work, then trash them and start over. He's said there are dead drafts of all three of his novels sitting on his desktop. The waste is part of the process, and treating it as waste is the mistake. Those dead drafts are how he figured out what the book actually needed to be.

The approach shows up in the work. The Kite Runner builds its emotional weight slowly, through accumulated domestic detail, before the story tips into its larger reckoning. That accumulation doesn't come from plotting. It comes from a writer following a character through a dark room, discovering what's there. Literary fiction written from an outline tends to feel engineered. Hosseini's books feel found.

Revision as the Real Work

Hosseini has put his revision count on record. In a Writer's Digest interview from August 2013, he described his method of working over a manuscript through multiple complete drafts until the characters finally come to life: "It really is through reviewing the story and writing a second, and then a third, and a fourth, and often a fifth or sixth draft, that slowly, that working over finally stirs a character to life." For And the Mountains Echoed, he worked for approximately three years across six to seven drafts. A Thousand Splendid Suns, with its multiple narrators and competing timelines, was rewritten five times from different narrative perspectives before he found the one that worked.

His framing is precise: "Writing for me is largely about rewriting. A first draft is really just a sketch on which I add layer and dimension and shade and nuance and color." The revision is the construction of the actual book. The first draft tells him what he's writing. The subsequent drafts write it. This is a distinction a lot of newer writers struggle to make, because it means accepting that the thing you spent months producing is really just raw material. Hosseini has said his characters in first drafts tend to be flat. They only become real when he writes through them again, and again, and again.

The reader in this process is Roya. She gives feedback that Hosseini has described as "honest, direct, and unburdened." She's not a writer, which he seems to value specifically. A reader who doesn't know the internal logic of the story can tell him whether it worked without getting tangled in the mechanics of how it was built. He revises until they both agree it's ready for his publisher, Riverhead Books. By that point, what began as a disappointing sketch at a kitchen table before dawn has become the finished book.

Writing Afghanistan from the Outside

Hosseini left Kabul at age eleven when his father was posted to the Afghan Embassy in Paris. The Soviet invasion in December 1979 made returning impossible, and in 1980 the family sought asylum in the United States, settling in San Jose. He wouldn't set foot in Afghanistan again until 2003, at age thirty-eight, arriving on a UN aid mission just as The Kite Runner was going to press. He had written the entire book from memory, from twenty years of distance, in a kitchen in California.

In a 2005 interview with Terry Gross on NPR's Fresh Air, he described what he was trying to recover in the novel's opening sections: "I kind of wanted to re-create that lost time and era in at least the first third of this book and to kind of bring life, a sweeter, more innocent Kabul that I remember from my childhood." The pre-Soviet city, the kite tournaments, the specific class dynamics of Kabul's neighborhoods. These were things he'd carried with him since he was a child, details that had never needed to be written down because they were simply there. Writing the novel was the act of finding out how much of that memory was still usable.

For the Taliban-era sections, which he hadn't witnessed firsthand, he worked differently. He'd spent years at Afghan social gatherings listening to people who had lived through that period. He hadn't been collecting material consciously. But when he sat down to write, he found he had accumulated enough specifics from those conversations to build the scenes. He told an interviewer for Aftaab Magazine in June 2004 that his perspective would always be that of the Afghan exile, "always touched by that, always marked by my distance to Afghanistan." He treated that distance as the condition of the work rather than a problem to solve.

The emotional directness that makes his books land the way they do is inseparable from this position. An exile writing about a place they loved as a child has a particular kind of access to grief, to guilt, and to the idealized version of a place that memory preserves. He's said there was always an undercurrent of survivor's guilt in his writing life, the awareness of having gotten out while others stayed and suffered. That guilt runs through the fabric of The Kite Runner more explicitly than almost any first novel I can think of. It's not just a theme; it's the emotional engine. And it came directly from the specific circumstances of his life, carried in his memory across two decades, written out at a kitchen table before the city of Mountain View woke up. Khaled Hosseini's Writing Routine: The Full Picture

The morning routine that produced The Kite Runner ran on a simple structure: wake between 4:30 and 5:00 a.m., write until roughly 8:00 a.m., go to work. Three hours in the dark before the day made any demands. Hosseini has talked about those sessions as genuinely solitary in a way his afternoons never were. There were no other claims on that time. The manuscript got whatever he had that was sharpest and freshest, before the clinic took the rest.

After he left medicine and the novels became his full-time work, the routine shifted. In a 2013 Goodreads interview, he described dropping his children at school at 8:00 a.m., exercising, then sitting down to write from about 9:30 until 2:00 in the afternoon. The total writing window expanded from three hours to closer to four or five, but the structure stayed the same: a defined block, a closed door, and then it's over. He doesn't write in the evenings. Afternoons end the session, and whatever didn't get written waits until tomorrow.

The workspace for A Thousand Splendid Suns was a deliberate upgrade from the kitchen table. He rented a small room near his home, minimal furniture, no distractions, specifically because the second book felt bigger and more complicated than the first. The "little bunker" gave him a physical separation that the kitchen never had. By the time he was working on And the Mountains Echoed, the bunker had become a home office, small and spare, with the door closed and shades drawn. The conditions changed, but the underlying logic stayed consistent across all three novels: find the quiet space, protect the time, write toward the story instead of waiting for it to arrive.

Sources

The most direct account of Hosseini's early-morning physician-writer routine comes from his Academy of Achievement interview, conducted July 3, 2008 in Kailua-Kona, Hawaii, where he describes his 4:45-5:00 a.m. wake time and the process of writing before seeing patients. Noah Charney's "Khaled Hosseini: How I Write" for The Daily Beast, published November 7, 2012, is the best single source on his current process: the 9:30-2:00 p.m. writing block, his two-to-three-page daily target, his refusal to outline, and his view of first drafts as sketches. His 2013 Goodreads interview adds detail on revision, dead drafts, and the role of Roya as his first reader. Terry Gross's Fresh Air interview, broadcast August 11, 2005 on NPR/WHYY, is the primary source on his account of writing Kabul from memory and his survivor's guilt. His HuffPost piece from 2013 confirms the 5:00 a.m. wake time and his description of cutting into his sleeping hours to write the first book. Writer's Digest, August 2013, contains his detailed account of working through five to six drafts to build character.

What You Can Steal

Hosseini's routine is particularly useful for writers with day jobs, because the whole structure was designed around a day job. The specific lessons that transfer:

  • Find the three hours before the world claims you. Hosseini wrote an entire first novel in the pre-dawn slot before his clinical day. The hours don't have to be in the morning, but they have to exist somewhere. Look for the window that doesn't belong to anyone yet, and put the manuscript there.
  • Write the first draft for yourself only. Keep it private until at least the midpoint. External judgment too early will turn you into an audience member instead of a writer, and you'll stop following the story to start managing how it reads.
  • Expect the first draft to be a disappointment, and go in anyway. Hosseini describes the first draft as a sketch, not a book. If you know in advance that it's going to fall short of the ideal, you can stop treating the disappointment as evidence that something's wrong. It's evidence that you're working.
  • Revise completely, not patchwork. He rewrote A Thousand Splendid Suns five times from scratch, from different perspectives, until the structure worked. Rewriting means going back to the beginning, not fixing paragraphs. The book you're writing in draft two is often a different book than the one in draft one.
  • Your specific constraints are your material. The exile's distance, the doctor's discipline, the three hours stolen before dawn. Hosseini wrote the book that only he could write, from the exact circumstances he was living in. The limitations are not obstacles. They're the shape of the work.
