Kristin Hannah's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 1, 2026 | 9 min read

Kristin Hannah writes in longhand, on yellow legal pads, starting in a chair beside the fireplace in her bedroom. She runs three or four miles first. Then she settles in, and she writes until five in the afternoon. She has been doing this, more or less, since she sold her first novel in the early 1990s, and the stack of legal pads that produced The Nightingale, The Great Alone, and Firefly Lane is the physical record of a practice she built before she had any reason to believe it would work.

The routine is worth studying partly because Hannah came to writing sideways. She was an attorney first, a graduate of the University of Puget Sound School of Law in 1986, and she practiced general law in Seattle for seven years before a high-risk pregnancy forced her to slow down. She picked up a story she'd been carrying in the back of her mind and gave herself a deadline: sell the novel before her son started kindergarten, or return to law. The book sold. The legal pads stayed. What she built in those early years, a daily practice rooted in discipline and emotional exposure, is the same one she uses now, thirty-plus novels later, writing from a waterfront home on an island in Puget Sound.

The Routine at a Glance

Wake Time
Early morning. She runs three to four miles first, then begins writing once she's home.
Writing Location
A chair beside the fireplace in her bedroom to start. If the weather cooperates, she moves to the patio or a lawn chair on the deck of her waterfront home near Seattle. She has an office but rarely uses it.
Daily Output
She writes fairly solidly from mid-morning until around five in the afternoon, with breaks for lunch and email. She writes every day when she's in a draft.
Tools
Yellow legal pads and longhand cursive, a habit that traces back to her years as a practicing attorney. She's written every novel this way.
Famous Ritual
She doesn't begin drafting until she has a lengthy written synopsis with a clear beginning, middle, and end. The synopsis is how she knows the story is ready. Only then does she pick up the legal pad and write the first sentence.
Books Written This Way
The Nightingale, The Great Alone, Firefly Lane, The Four Winds, Fly Away, The Women, and more than twenty others.

The Attorney Who Became a Novelist

Hannah's legal background shapes her writing process in ways she's talked about openly. Law school trained her to research obsessively, to build arguments from evidence rather than intuition, and to treat revision as the real work rather than the first draft. Those habits transferred. When she began writing fiction seriously in the early 1990s, she brought the same structured approach she'd used to prepare cases: gather the facts first, understand the terrain, then construct the story from the ground up.

The yellow legal pad is the most visible remnant of that era. She started writing on legal pads because she wanted to be able to work outside on her deck, and a laptop wasn't the portable tool it is now. The habit stuck, and it became part of how she thinks on the page. Longhand slows a writer down. It forces every sentence to be considered before it's committed, because crossing out and rewriting by hand costs more effort than hitting delete. For someone who came from a profession built on precise language, that friction turns out to be a feature.

There's also something worth noting about the deadline she gave herself when she started. She didn't ease into writing as a hobby or a side pursuit. She set a real, stakes-bearing condition, sell before kindergarten or go back to the law, and that built urgency into the practice from the beginning. The muse wasn't part of the equation. She sat down and wrote because the alternative was returning to a career she'd already decided to leave. Writers who are waiting for the right conditions might consider what Hannah built in: a version of accountability sharp enough to make daily work feel necessary.

Research as the Foundation

Before Hannah writes a word of draft, she researches. For some books, that phase runs for years. The Nightingale, published in 2015, grew out of research she'd begun for an earlier novel, Winter Garden, when she came across the story of Andrée de Jongh, a 19-year-old Belgian woman who established the Comet Escape Line during World War II, a secret network that helped Allied servicemen escape over the Pyrenees to Spain. That discovery sent Hannah deeper into the French Resistance, into memoirs written by women who had hidden Jewish families, into firsthand accounts from downed airmen who'd been sheltered by strangers at enormous personal risk.

She traveled to France and walked the routes her characters would take. She read everything she could find about daily life under German occupation. Then she wrote a first draft, went back, realized she couldn't describe a road or a tree without looking it up, and returned to the source material. In a 2015 interview with the American Booksellers Association, she said that every single scene had to be built on what was actually happening historically, and that adhering to the factual timeline was a constant pressure throughout the drafting. About 300 pages in, she told her editor she needed another year. She'd felt something she described as the magic of the story, and she wanted to give it the space it required.

This is the part of Hannah's process that gets overlooked when readers focus on her emotional impact. The crying scenes, the devastating endings, the way her books make people put them down and sit quietly for a moment, all of that is built on a foundation of meticulous factual work. The emotion lands because the world it lives in is accurate. Research and feeling aren't competing values in her practice. The research is what makes the feeling trustworthy.

Emotion as a Craft Standard

Hannah has said in several interviews that she cries while writing. She's described finishing the last fifty pages of The Nightingale repeatedly during revisions and finding that the emotional weight of the ending never diminished no matter how many times she'd been through it. That, she's said, is how she knew readers would respond. The scene that still moved her after a dozen passes was the scene that was working.

This is a more specific piece of craft thinking than it first appears. Hannah isn't describing a writer who gets swept away by her own prose. She's describing a writer who uses her own emotional response as a diagnostic tool, a way of checking whether a scene is doing what it needs to do. If she reads a scene and nothing happens in her chest, the scene isn't ready. Writing that doesn't move the writer probably won't move the reader. The test is repeatable and personal, which makes it more reliable than any structural checklist.

What this requires, practically speaking, is that Hannah has to stay genuinely inside the story even during revision, which is the phase when most writers have read their own work so many times they've gone numb to it. She's described the revision process as returning to the emotional core of each scene before she touches the prose. She goes back to why she cared about this moment, not just what's happening in it, and she works from there. That's a different kind of revision than line editing, and it's the kind that produces the books people describe as impossible to put down.

The Synopsis That Must Exist Before the Draft

Hannah doesn't outline in minute detail, but she has a firm rule about what has to exist before she writes the first sentence of a novel: a lengthy synopsis with a clear beginning, middle, and end. She's talked about this in interviews and on her website FAQ. The synopsis is more like a thorough account of the emotional arc than a scene-by-scene breakdown, the character transformations, and the major story beats. Once that document is solid, she picks up the legal pad.

This is worth dwelling on because Hannah has also said the middle of the book is always the hardest part. Every book, every time, the middle comes for her. The synopsis is partially what she builds against that. When you're two hundred pages in and you've lost the thread, the synopsis is the compass. It tells you what the book is supposed to be doing, even when the draft has drifted. Writers who skip this phase tend to discover the middle problem when they're already inside it, with no map and no way back to shore.

The three-month research and planning phase she describes before each draft, coming up with the idea, researching it, and formulating a loose plan, is where the synopsis gets built. By the time she starts writing, she already knows the emotional destination. She knows who these people are when they arrive at the end. The daily writing, then, is less about discovery and more about finding the truest path to a place she can already see. That's a calmer way to work than flying blind, and it shows in the structural confidence of her books. Her plots don't meander. They accumulate.

What You Can Steal

Hannah's routine is practical across almost every life circumstance. Here's what translates:

  • Write to a synopsis, not into the unknown. Spend real time before the draft building a document that tells you where the book ends up and why it matters. The synopsis is what you hold onto when the middle gets difficult, and the middle always gets difficult.
  • Use your own emotional response as a quality check. If a scene doesn't move you after several passes, it's probably not working. Read back through your draft looking for the moments that still land, and then ask what the flat scenes are missing that those scenes have.
  • Let the research run as long as it needs to. Hannah spent months inside archives and memoirs before she began each historical novel. Rushing the research phase to get to the draft is one of the most common mistakes in ambitious fiction. The story can only be as rich as what you've absorbed.
  • Work until five. A writing session that runs from mid-morning to late afternoon, with real breaks built in, produces more usable pages than a two-hour sprint followed by guilt. Treat it like a workday, because it is one.
  • Find your threshold ritual. For Hannah, it's the morning run, then the fireplace chair. The specific elements don't matter. What matters is a consistent sequence your body and mind learn to associate with the writing state. Build that sequence and protect it.
