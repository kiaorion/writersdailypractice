Lee Child's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 14, 2026 | 9 min read

Every September 1, Lee Child opens a new Word document and types the first sentence of a Jack Reacher novel. He has no outline. He doesn't know what the book is about, who the villain is, or how it ends. The date is fixed. The rest is a complete blank. And then, over the next several months, one of the best-selling thriller series in the world comes out of that blank, written in a single forward push from an author who insists he doesn't know what's coming any more than the reader does.

The routine is worth studying carefully because it contradicts almost every piece of craft advice given to genre writers. Plan your story. Build your outline. Know your ending before your beginning. Child does none of it. He's sold over a hundred million copies. The contradiction is interesting, and there's a reason it works, but you have to look at the whole practice before you can see it.

Profile

The Routine at a Glance

Start Date
September 1, every year, without exception. The date commemorates the day in 1995 when Child was made redundant from his job as a television presentation director at Granada TV in Manchester, which forced him to write his first novel.
Writing Location
Primarily in his apartment in Manhattan, where he lived for many years. Child has described writing at a desk, working through the afternoon and evening hours rather than the early morning.
Daily Output
Roughly 1,800 words per day during the writing period. Each Reacher novel runs around 100,000 words, and Child typically completes a draft in four to five months, finishing in late winter or early spring.
Tools
Microsoft Word on a laptop. Child has mentioned drinking coffee while writing, and historically smoked cigarettes during much of his career. He works in a single document from page one straight through to the end.
Famous Ritual
Writing without an outline, starting each book cold on September 1 as a deliberate act of commemoration and discipline. He does not know the plot, the villain, or the ending when he starts. Andy Martin documented an entire Reacher novel being written this way in real time.
Books Written This Way
Killing Floor, Die Trying, Tripwire, The Visitor, and every Reacher novel since 1997, through Make Me (the subject of Andy Martin's 2015 book) and beyond.

Writing Without an Outline

Child has explained his anti-outline position in dozens of interviews, and the reasoning is more considered than the provocative shorthand makes it sound. He argues that a reader turning the pages of a thriller wants the sensation of not knowing what happens next. If you want to produce that sensation honestly, as a writer you have to actually not know what happens next. An outline, in his view, destroys the thing you're trying to make. You can't fake surprise. You can manufacture its surface appearance, but readers who read a lot of genre fiction feel the difference between a writer who was genuinely discovering and a writer who was executing a plan.

This isn't a romantic idea about inspiration. Child treats it as craft. He's spoken about building forward momentum by letting each scene generate the next scene, the way a real investigation unfolds. Reacher arrives somewhere, encounters a situation, responds, and the response creates the next problem. Child follows that chain rather than laying it out in advance. The logic stays tight because the logic comes from character rather than from a plot grid, and Reacher's character is so thoroughly established after twenty-seven books that Child can trust him to react consistently to almost anything.

There's also a practical argument here about reader energy. Child writes the fast bits fast. When the action is moving, he moves with it. When things slow down, he slows down. An outline creates obligations: you've committed to a scene, so you write it even if something more interesting has occurred to you in the meantime. Writing without one means you can follow the better idea the moment it appears, because there's no plan to abandon. The book you actually write is always more interesting than the book you planned to write, and Child has built his whole method around keeping that option open.

The September 1 Ritual and Starting Cold

The date matters as much as the method. Child was let go from Granada Television on September 14, 1995, after eighteen years. He was in his early forties, had no savings, had two daughters at home, and had never published a word of fiction. By the time he cleared out his desk and got home, he'd decided to write a thriller. He started shortly after, and Killing Floor sold to Putnam in 1996. September 1 is a few weeks before the anniversary of the redundancy, and Child chose it as the fixed start date for every subsequent book partly as an act of recognition, a way of staying connected to the moment that made him a writer rather than a television executive.

What the fixed date does practically is remove the decision from the equation. Writers lose enormous energy to the question of when to start. The blank page is intimidating, and there are always legitimate-seeming reasons to wait: more research, a better idea, a clearer concept. Child sidesteps all of that by making the start date non-negotiable. On September 1, he opens the document. Not when he feels ready. The date is the trigger, not the feeling.

Starting cold, without plot or plan, makes the date even more important. If Child prepared extensively in August, the September 1 start would just be the moment he began executing a fully formed idea. Instead, he comes to the page with almost nothing. He's talked about starting with only a scenario or an image, the kind of thing that could fit in a sentence. A man gets off a bus in a small town. Someone is being watched. A woman is in trouble and there's nobody else around. Reacher shows up, and the book starts, and Child finds out what happens by writing it.

One Draft and Trusting Instinct

Write the fast bits fast. A car chase written quickly reads quickly, and a chase labored over reads slow.
Lee Child's often-repeated rule of thumb on pace

Andy Martin spent the better part of 2014 sitting with Child as he wrote Make Me, the twentieth Reacher novel, and the resulting book, Reacher Said Nothing: Lee Child and the Making of Make Me, published in 2015, is the closest thing we have to a real-time documentary of a professional thriller being written. What Martin observed was a writer who moved steadily forward with very little revision and very little doubt about the sentence at hand. Child would write a scene, finish it, and move on. He wasn't looping back to fix what he'd written the day before. The forward motion was the whole point.

Child's position on revision is specific: he revises sentences as he writes them, getting each one close to right before moving to the next, which means the first draft is already fairly clean by the time he reaches the end. He's not a one-draft writer in the sense that the first version goes directly to his editor. But he's not a heavy reviser in the conventional sense either. The heavy lifting happens in real time, in the act of composition, rather than in subsequent passes over a rough draft. By the time he types the last sentence, he knows the book is essentially done.

This approach demands a specific relationship to your own instincts. When you're writing without an outline and you can't look at a plan to check whether you're on track, the only guide you have is whether the current scene feels right. Child writes with a lot of conviction, and the Reacher books read that way. There's no hedging in the prose. Sentences arrive at their point and stop. Actions happen in real time, with the weight and consequence of actual events. That confidence on the page comes directly from a writer who committed to each sentence before moving to the next, rather than deferring the commitment to a revision pass that would resolve all the uncertainty later.

The Discipline Behind the Apparent Looseness

The no-outline method can look like a license for chaos, and writers who try to imitate it without understanding the underlying structure usually end up with exactly that. Child's process looks loose from the outside because the scaffolding is invisible. The structure comes from character rather than plot architecture, but it's just as rigid. Reacher is a fully formed human being with a consistent psychology, a physical presence, a set of values, and a way of moving through the world that Child knows as well as his own habits. When you have a character that thoroughly understood, you don't need a plot outline the same way. You just need to put that character in a situation and ask what he does next. The character generates the plot.

Child also maintains strict discipline around the September 1 date and around his daily output during the writing period. He isn't writing whenever inspiration strikes. He writes on a fixed schedule, producing roughly 1,800 words per day, and he pushes through even when the scene isn't cooperating. The discovery happens inside the work session, not before it. If a scene is stuck, the solution is to keep writing into it, because the answer is somewhere ahead, not behind. Going back to research or think or plan is how you stop discovering. Child doesn't stop.

What makes the Reacher novels feel propulsive is partly the plots and partly Reacher himself, but a lot of it comes from this forward-only approach to composition. Child writes at the speed of someone who wants to find out what happens, and that energy transfers to the prose. You feel it in the chapter lengths, the sentence rhythms, the way action scenes move in short declarative bursts while the slower scenes build pressure through specificity rather than through pace. He told Martin in one of their recorded conversations that the advice he gives writers is to write the fast bits fast. If the action is happening, your prose has to happen at the same speed or the reader gets ahead of you. An outline slows that down. It makes the writer feel like a manager rather than a participant.

Lee Child's Writing Routine

Lee Child's writing routine is built around a single annual commitment and a principle of forward motion that governs everything else. On September 1, he opens a blank document and writes the first sentence of a new Reacher novel. He has no notes, no outline, no beat sheet. He has a character he's spent decades with, and a reader's appetite for the next problem. That's the whole starting inventory.

During the writing period, which runs from September through roughly January or February, Child works in his Manhattan apartment through the afternoon and evening hours, producing around 1,800 words per day. He drinks coffee. He has spoken about having smoked for much of his career while writing, a habit that has since changed. The session ends when the day's word count is done. There's no minimum or maximum, just the steady accumulation of a book that doesn't know its own ending yet.

Andy Martin's Reacher Said Nothing documented this process with unusual precision, following Child through the writing of Make Me from the first sentence on September 1, 2014, through revision and delivery. What Martin found was a writer with a calm relationship to uncertainty. Child wasn't anxious about not knowing the plot. He treated the daily discovery as the interesting part, not the stressful part. The book would arrive the way all twenty-something Reacher novels had arrived: one scene at a time, forward only, with no map and no safety net, finished sometime in winter, ready to publish the following autumn.

Sources

The primary source for Lee Child's writing process is Andy Martin's Reacher Said Nothing: Lee Child and the Making of Make Me (Bantam Press, 2015), which documents Child writing an entire Reacher novel in real time over the course of 2014. Child has also discussed his method extensively in interviews with the Guardian, where he's spoken about the September 1 start date, the no-outline policy, and his approach to pace and revision. His New York Times profiles and interviews given during book tours for Make Me (2015), Night School (2016), and The Midnight Line (2017) cover the same ground. Child spoke directly about his process and the origins of the September 1 ritual in a 2016 interview with The Times of London. For context on the Granada Television redundancy that launched the series, see reporting around the publication of Killing Floor (G.P. Putnam's Sons, 1997), his debut novel, and multiple retrospective profiles on the twentieth anniversary of the series.

What You Can Steal

Child's method is more portable than it looks, but it requires being honest about what actually keeps you from writing. Most of the things we call preparation are avoidance with better branding. Consider these:

  • Fix your start date and treat it as non-negotiable. The question of when to start consumes more creative energy than almost any other question. Pick a date, mark it in your calendar, and make the date the answer. Child has started every Reacher novel on September 1 for nearly thirty years. The ritual removes the deliberation.
  • Trust character over plot. If your character is fully realized, the plot can come from asking what that person does next. You may not need an outline as much as you need a character you know deeply enough to follow.
  • Write the fast bits fast. Child said it directly: the action has to happen on the page at the same speed it happens in the story. If you write slowly through a chase scene, you'll produce a slow chase scene, no matter how exciting the underlying events are. Match your pace to your content.
  • Commit to each sentence before moving to the next. The heavy revision Child does happens in real time, sentence by sentence. The goal is a draft that's already close to right, not a rough mess you'll clean up later. That discipline, applied daily, is what makes one-draft writing possible.
  • Let the discovery happen inside the work session. When a scene isn't cooperating, the answer is usually to keep writing into it. The breakthrough comes from forward motion, not from stopping to plan. Child's whole method is a bet on this, and it's produced more than twenty-seven novels. The evidence is hard to argue with.
