How Many Words a Day Does Lee Child Write?

Kia Orion | August 2, 2026 | 5 min read

Child is one of the few thriller writers whose calendar is better known than his word count, and the calendar is the better guide. The daily number floats. The September 1 start date has not moved in thirty years.

The Direct Answer
Around 1,800 words on a working day. One book a year.
Child starts every Jack Reacher novel on September 1 and finishes in late winter or early spring. A Reacher novel runs roughly 100,000 words, and he barely rewrites, so the first draft is close to the book you buy.

The Number and Where It Comes From

The figure attached to Child in most roundups is about 1,800 words a day. It fits everything else on the record, though he talks about the work in seasons far more often than in daily counts, and he has never presented 1,800 as a target he hits or misses.

Run the arithmetic and it makes sense. A Reacher novel comes in near 100,000 words. At 1,800 a day that is about 56 working days. He starts on September 1 and finishes somewhere in late winter, which is roughly 180 days. So he writes on something like one day in three, and on the days he writes he produces a full session's worth.

That gap between the daily number and the calendar is the whole story of his method. The 1,800 lands on the days the book is ready to move, and the days in between belong to something else.

September 1, Every Year

Child began his first novel, Killing Floor, in September 1994 after Granada Television made him redundant at forty. He spent three pounds ninety-nine on paper and pencils to start it. Every Reacher novel since has begun on the anniversary. The series ran to 31 books by 2026, the later ones co-written with his brother Andrew, and has sold well past 100 million copies worldwide.

Andy Martin, a Cambridge academic, sat alongside Child through the writing of Make Me, the twentieth Reacher novel, starting on 1 September 2014, and published the account as Reacher Said Nothing in 2015. It is the closest thing anyone has to a verified log of a bestselling thriller being written in real time. Child types with two fingers, works into the afternoon and evening, and does not outline.

The First Draft Is the Draft

Child does not rewrite in any serious way. The 1,800 words he produces on a working day are, give or take a light edit, the 1,800 words that reach print. That single fact reframes the whole number.

Set his pace next to a writer who drafts 3,000 words a day and then cuts half of them in revision. The 3,000-word writer nets 1,500. Child nets close to 1,800. On the measure that counts, words that survive to publication, the slow-looking thriller writer is ahead.

It also explains why he can afford the days off. A writer who expects to rewrite treats the first draft as raw material and needs volume to work with. A writer who expects the first draft to stand needs the sentence right the first time, which takes longer per sentence and yields fewer sentences per year.

What You Can Steal

  • Measure the words that survive. A modest daily number with no rewriting beats a big one with heavy cutting.
  • Give the book a start date. September 1 has done more for Child's output than any word target. A fixed date removes the decision about when to begin.
  • Working days do not have to be every day. Roughly one day in three, at a real session's worth, still finishes a hundred-thousand-word novel inside six months.
  • Slow down if you plan to trust the draft. The no-rewriting approach only works when you are willing to spend the time at the sentence.
