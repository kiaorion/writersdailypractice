Leo Tolstoy's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 14, 2026 | 10 min read

Tolstoy wrote War and Peace in six years, from 1863 to 1869, and his wife Sophia copied the manuscript by hand at least seven times in full. Copied by hand, by candlelight, after the children were in bed, into the same notebooks her husband would tear apart again the next morning with his revisions. If you want to understand how Tolstoy worked, that image is your starting point. He was a man who generated enormous volume and then remade it, over and over, until the prose felt like something that had simply always existed.

The myth around Tolstoy tends to run toward the saint or the monster. The peasant reformer. The count who gave away his estate. The husband who made his wife miserable. All of that is true and all of it is beside the point if you're interested in the writing. What the routine reveals is something simpler: a man who'd built his life around a fixed morning window at his desk, and who'd spent decades turning that habit into the two greatest novels in the history of literary fiction.

Profile

The Routine at a Glance

Wake Time
Around 8 or 9 a.m. Tolstoy was a late riser by the standards of his era, though he was at his desk by mid-morning and generally worked through the early afternoon.
Writing Location
His private study at Yasnaya Polyana, the family estate in the Tula region, about 120 miles south of Moscow. He returned there for almost every serious writing project across his adult life.
Daily Output
Variable and hard to pin down. He set no fixed word count. He wrote in dense bursts, sometimes making very few net additions to the manuscript because so much was crossing out and rewriting on the same pages.
Tools
Quill pen, later steel pen nib. He wrote in Russian longhand on loose sheets. Sophia would then copy his drafts, often struggling to decipher his notoriously difficult handwriting.
Famous Ritual
Evening walks around the estate, often for an hour or more, which he used to think through the problems the morning's writing had left unresolved. He was also a passionate horseman and rode regularly as part of his daily life at Yasnaya Polyana.
Books Written This Way
War and Peace, Anna Karenina, The Death of Ivan Ilyich, Resurrection.

The Morning Hours at Yasnaya Polyana

Yasnaya Polyana is a birch-forest estate that had been in the Tolstoy family since before Leo was born. He grew up there, left for Kazan University, served in the army in the Caucasus, traveled to Western Europe twice, and kept coming back. By the time he married Sophia Behrs in 1862 and started working on what would become War and Peace, Yasnaya Polyana had calcified into the place where work happened. He would spend the rest of his life there, with a few notable exceptions, and every major book came out of the same low-ceilinged study in the main house.

The morning routine at Yasnaya Polyana was structured in the way wealthy nineteenth-century Russian households were structured, which is to say there was domestic infrastructure around the writing that most writers today don't have. Servants, a large property to walk, a wife managing a household of children. What Tolstoy built inside that infrastructure was a protected morning block, roughly from breakfast through early afternoon, during which he went to his study and the rest of the house understood he was not to be disturbed. This wasn't unusual for the era or the class, but the discipline with which he maintained it across decades of family chaos, health crises, and the enormous philosophical upheavals of his later life was real.

His biographers note that the morning hours were genuinely productive in the conventional sense only during his great novel-writing periods, roughly 1863-1877 when he was finishing War and Peace and then working on Anna Karenina. Before and after those years, the morning study time was more likely to be spent on letters, diaries, educational tracts, or staring at the pages he couldn't make work. But the habit held even when the output didn't. He showed up. The study was where he went in the morning. That fact remained true across six decades of writing.

Sophia and the Copy System

The relationship between Tolstoy's routine and his wife's labor is worth examining honestly. Sophia Andreyevna Tolstaya was educated, literary, and intelligent, and she spent large parts of her marriage doing work that modern editors would recognize as manuscript preparation and copy-editing. Every night, after the children were settled, she would transcribe the day's pages in her own hand, working from Tolstoy's cramped and heavily revised draft, creating a clean copy. The next morning, Tolstoy would revise that clean copy, and the cycle would begin again.

This system served Tolstoy practically in ways that went beyond the obvious. Having a clean copy to work from each morning meant he was always revising from a legible text rather than his own tangle of insertions and deletions. The act of Sophia copying forced a version of the draft into existence that could be read with fresh eyes. In some ways it was a pre-industrial version of what modern writers do when they print a manuscript and edit on paper: the physical separation from the original draft creates distance. Tolstoy's distance happened because another person had rendered the text in their own handwriting, which made it slightly foreign, slightly readable as a stranger's work.

What Sophia's diaries reveal is that this arrangement carried real weight on her end. She was copying out pages she sometimes disagreed with, in a marriage that grew increasingly difficult as Tolstoy's spiritual convictions hardened, while also raising their many children and managing the estate. Her own diaries are a significant document in their own right, and the picture she paints of the copying sessions is one of exhaustion and complicated feeling. The honest accounting here is that Tolstoy's routine was made possible partly by labor that wasn't his, and that knowing this changes how you read the myth of the solitary genius at his desk.

The copy system was retired when Tolstoy began working with the typewriter in his later years, and when his circle of disciples began handling correspondence and transcription. By then the great novels were done. Sophia had copied War and Peace and Anna Karenina into existence, and what came after was written in a different, leaner manner that reflected both Tolstoy's changed artistic values and the changed conditions of his household.

The Diary as a Daily Writing Engine

Tolstoy kept a diary for most of his adult life, beginning in his twenties and continuing, with gaps, into old age. This is one of the more underexamined facts about his writing practice. The diaries weren't the books, but they were where the writing muscles stayed warm during the years between major projects, and they were where he worked out the moral and philosophical questions that eventually drove the novels. Reading them alongside the fiction, you can sometimes catch an idea forming in the journal and then showing up, transformed, three or four years later in a character's mouth.

What the diaries reveal about his process is that Tolstoy thought by writing. The diary was where he argued with himself, recorded what he'd observed, tried on positions he later rejected, and kept a running account of his own moral failures with a severity that is almost painful to read. He held himself to standards that he knew he couldn't meet, and the gap between the ideal and the actual is visible on every page. This is also, not coincidentally, the engine that drives his fiction. Pierre Bezukhov in War and Peace and Levin in Anna Karenina are men living in exactly that gap, trying to be better than they can manage and failing in ways that feel real because they came from a mind that understood the failure from the inside.

There's a practical argument for keeping a diary that Tolstoy's example illustrates well. A daily writing habit that exists outside the manuscript means the writing muscle doesn't go cold between projects. When Tolstoy sat down to resume work on a novel after months away, he wasn't starting from a cold stop. He'd been writing every day, in the diary, and the transition back to the fiction was a shift in register rather than a restart. Any writer who's tried to return to a manuscript after a long break knows how much that distinction matters.

Revision as a Way of Life

The thing people know about Tolstoy's revision process is the number: War and Peace was rewritten multiple times in full, over six years. What people don't always know is what the revisions were actually doing. Tolstoy was changing structural things: chapter order, the balance between the war sections and the domestic scenes, the degree of historical analysis versus narrative. He was also changing sentences. The surviving manuscript pages show continuous revision at the line level, insertions and deletions that crowd the margins and sometimes become illegible even to Sophia, who was used to his hand.

A.N. Wilson, in his 1988 biography, describes Tolstoy's approach to revision as something close to a method. He would finish a draft, hand it to Sophia for copying, and then attack the copy the next morning as if it had been written by someone else. The emotional distance that came from seeing Sophia's handwriting rather than his own allowed him to read more coldly, and cold reading is where revision actually happens. Writers who revise in the same session they draft are often too close to the choices they've just made to see them clearly. Tolstoy's system built in a twenty-four-hour gap and a physical transformation of the text that functioned like a mandatory cooling-off period.

The revisions also changed in character between War and Peace and Anna Karenina. By the time he started the later novel in the early 1870s, Tolstoy was a different kind of writer than he'd been at thirty-five. His sentences tightened. The structural ambitions scaled down from the panoramic sweep of 1812 to the tighter, more claustrophobic world of society adultery and its consequences. If you put a page from each novel side by side, you can feel the difference. The revision process between books had taught him something he couldn't have learned any other way: that epic scale is a trap you grow out of.

The Tension Between Writing and Belief

After finishing Anna Karenina in 1877, Tolstoy went through a spiritual crisis that he documented in A Confession, written around 1879-1882. He concluded that his life as a wealthy nobleman and celebrated author was morally indefensible, and he spent the remaining thirty years of his life trying to live out an ethical program that was in direct conflict with most of what his earlier writing had valued. He rejected the novels. He said that War and Peace and Anna Karenina were the products of a man who hadn't understood what actually mattered. He turned to shorter moral tales and religious writing instead.

This is one of the stranger chapters in the history of any writer's relationship to their own work. The man who wrote "The Death of Ivan Ilyich" in 1886 was, by his own account, a convert who'd turned his back on art. The story doesn't read that way. It's as technically controlled and humanly precise as anything he ever wrote, and its moral argument, that a man can spend his whole life building something hollow, is more felt than preached. The renunciation of art produced some of his most artful work, which should tell you something about the gap between what a writer believes about writing and what they actually do when they sit down.

His morning routine survived the conversion, more or less, though what happened inside the study changed. The correspondence alone could fill most of the morning by the later years; Tolstoy had become a kind of secular prophet with a global following, and the letters that arrived from around the world demanded real attention. The fiction came more slowly and with more conflict. He was no longer writing to discover; he was writing to teach. The diaries of the final decades record a man who still needed the desk, still needed the hours, but who was using them differently than he had when the great novels were coming.

Sources

The essential biography is Henri Troyat's Tolstoy, published in French in 1965 and translated by Nancy Amphoux, which draws extensively on letters, diaries, and family accounts to reconstruct Tolstoy's daily working life across all periods. A.N. Wilson's Tolstoy: A Biography (1988) covers the writing process with particular care, especially the revision habits and the later spiritual years. Rosamund Bartlett's Tolstoy: A Russian Life (2010) provides the most recent scholarly account and is especially good on the Yasnaya Polyana context and the family dynamics around the writing. Sophia Tolstaya's diaries, available in translation as The Diaries of Sophia Tolstoy, are a primary source that corrects and complicates the official Tolstoy legend at almost every point. Tolstoy's own A Confession and the diaries collected and translated in various editions give the autobiographical record from his own hand.

What You Can Steal

Tolstoy's routine is less portable than Hemingway's in the obvious sense: not many writers have a family estate and a spouse doing daily manuscript transcription. But the deeper principles inside his practice are available to anyone. If you're not sure which tradition you write in, take the genre quiz and then come back to these. Here's what actually transfers:

  • Keep a daily diary that has nothing to do with your current project. Tolstoy's diary kept the writing muscle warm between novels and gave him a space to develop the moral questions that eventually drove the fiction. The diary and the novel fed each other without getting tangled up in each other.
  • Build a mandatory gap between drafting and revising. The reason Sophia's copies worked was the physical and temporal distance they created from the original. If you can't hand your pages to someone else overnight, the second-best option is printing them and coming back the next morning. Cold reading is where real revision begins.
  • Let the revision change structure, not just sentences. Tolstoy rearranged chapters, rebalanced whole narrative threads, and reconceived the book's shape multiple times across the six years of War and Peace. Most writers under-revise at the structural level because it's painful. He treated structural revision as the main event.
  • Show up to the desk even during the fallow periods. Tolstoy's study time continued through years when the novels weren't coming, sustained by letters, diaries, and essays. The habit didn't depend on productivity to stay alive. That's what made it last sixty years.
  • Notice what your convictions do to your prose. Tolstoy's later work was shaped by his belief that art should teach, and you can feel the belief in the sentences, sometimes productively and sometimes not. The relationship between what you believe and what you write is worth examining directly, because it's always there whether you examine it or not.
