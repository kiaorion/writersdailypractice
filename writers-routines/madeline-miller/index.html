Madeline Miller's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 14, 2026 | 10 min read

Madeline Miller spent roughly ten years writing The Song of Achilles. Somewhere in the middle of that decade, around the five-year mark, she finished a draft and decided it wasn't working. So she put it aside and started over. The book that won the Women's Prize for Fiction in 2012 -- previously known as the Orange Prize -- was built on top of the wreckage of a previous version that will never be read by anyone. That's the fact most worth sitting with when you study her process, because it tells you almost everything about how she works and why her prose sounds the way it does.

Miller's routine doesn't fit the productivity templates. She has no famous word-count quota, no five-hundred-words-before-breakfast rule. What she has instead is a willingness to stay inside a book for as long as it takes to get it right, to hold it up to the light and accept the verdict even when the verdict is hard. Her method is slow, patient, and scholarship-driven. The controlled lyricism in her sentences comes directly from a writer who spent years reading the Iliad before she trusted herself to retell it.

The Ten-Year Build and the Draft That Got Thrown Out

Miller has talked in multiple interviews about the timeline of The Song of Achilles. The book took approximately ten years from conception to publication, and for a significant stretch of that time she was working on a version that ultimately didn't survive. She described this in her conversation with NPR and elsewhere: she had a draft, she looked at it honestly, and she saw that it wasn't doing what she needed it to do. The voice was wrong. The emotional center hadn't been found. She started over.

The decision to scrap years of work is the kind of thing writing advice tends to gloss over, because it's genuinely painful and there's nothing tidy to say about it. What Miller did was make a judgment call about her own work that most writers aren't willing to make, which is to prioritize what the book needed over what she'd already invested in it. Sunk costs are a powerful psychological trap. She ignored them.

What's worth noting is what she was doing during those years of first-draft failure. She was teaching Latin and Ancient Greek to high school students, which meant she was spending her working days inside the very language and literature that her novel was trying to inhabit. She was failing at it from inside the source material, which is probably why the second attempt worked. By the time she restarted, the myths weren't research anymore. They were something closer to mother tongue.

Writers who romanticize the scrapped draft tend to treat it as a loss. Miller's story suggests it's more often a prerequisite. The first draft was the education. The second draft was the book.

Teaching the Classics While Writing Them Into Fiction

For years while writing The Song of Achilles, Miller taught Latin and Ancient Greek, primarily at a secondary school level. The arrangement meant she was never far from the material. She was conjugating Greek verbs in the morning and then, in whatever hours remained, trying to write historical fiction that held up to the weight of Homer. The classroom kept her honest in a way that a solitary research practice probably wouldn't have.

She has described in interviews how deeply she went into the source texts, reading multiple translations of the Iliad and working through the original Greek. That's not the behavior of a writer doing background research before moving on. It's the behavior of someone who believed that getting the language right required understanding what the language came from. She wanted to know how Homer handled time and silence, how he paced grief, what it felt like rhythmically to read a passage where Patroclus appears and then disappears from the scene. That kind of attention takes years, not weeks.

The connection between her teaching life and her writing life is visible on every page of her novels. The sentence rhythms in The Song of Achilles owe something to classical prosody, to a writer who spent her days thinking about how ancient languages move. Her prose is controlled without being cold, which is a very hard effect to achieve and one that almost certainly required her to live inside the classical world as a professional before she could render it as a novelist. The genre quiz readers most associate her with -- mythological retelling -- exists because of how seriously she took the scholarship underneath it.

This also explains why her output has been slow by commercial-fiction standards. Two novels in over a decade is a quiet pace. It's the pace of someone who wants the work to last longer than the marketing cycle, which is a different priority than most publishing contracts are built around.

Slow Revision and Getting the Voice Right

Miller has talked about revision the way most writers talk about drafting. The first version of a scene is a sketch. The real writing happens in the going back. She's described a process of returning to passages repeatedly, testing whether the emotional register is true, whether the syntax sounds like her narrator, whether a sentence earns the weight it's asking the reader to carry. This kind of revision doesn't have a schedule. It takes as long as it takes.

The voice in The Song of Achilles is one of the novel's most discussed achievements. Patroclus narrates from a position of love without sentimentality, grief without self-pity, and the management of that balance across a full novel is not an accident. It's the product of revisions that happened over years, with Miller adjusting the narrator's distance and temperature sentence by sentence. She's spoken about how important it was to her that Patroclus feel like a real person, not a device, and that required a precision in the prose that took a very long time to find.

The decision to start the novel over after five years wasn't just a structural one. When she talks about the first draft not working, it sounds like what she really means is that the voice wasn't there yet. She hadn't found the specific register that would let her do what the book needed to do emotionally. Once she found it, the second draft could be built on top of it.

Writers who treat revision as cleanup are working at a shallower level than Miller operates. For her, the revision is where the prose actually becomes itself. The draft is a hypothesis about what the voice might be. The revisions are the proof.

Scholarship as Raw Material

Miller studied Classics at Brown University and then at Yale School of Drama, where she worked on a stage adaptation of the Trojan War cycle. Her academic background was the foundation of her fiction writing. When she describes her process of reading multiple translations of Homer and working through the original Greek, she's describing a writer who treats scholarship the way other novelists treat notebooks: as the place where the raw material accumulates before it becomes prose.

The particular quality of her fiction comes from the collision between scholarly precision and novelistic empathy. She knows exactly what the Iliad says about Patroclus. She also asks what it would feel like to be him, which is a question the epic form can't answer but the novel can. The tension between those two ways of knowing the material produces the specific charge in her sentences. They carry authority from the research and heat from the imagination, and both things have to be present or the prose falls flat in one direction or the other.

Her second novel, Circe, published in 2018, took the same approach to a different source. Circe is a minor character in Homer, transformed into a full protagonist by a writer who understood the original text well enough to see what it left out. That kind of reading requires years of engagement with the primary sources, not a research phase that ends before the writing begins. For Miller, the scholarship and the writing are the same continuous activity, and the book comes out of their overlap.

This is probably the hardest thing to replicate about her process, because it requires a genuine love of the source material that can't be faked. She spent a decade with ancient Greek literature because she wanted to, not because the book demanded it. The book benefited from that love, but the love came first. A novelist trying to adopt her method without that underlying enthusiasm for the scholarship would produce something that looked like her surface but not her depth.

Madeline Miller's Writing Routine

Madeline Miller's writing routine, across the decade she spent building The Song of Achilles, was shaped less by daily discipline in the Hemingway sense and more by a sustained and patient relationship with the material. She was teaching during those years, which meant the writing happened in whatever hours the school calendar allowed, likely in the evenings, on weekends, across summers. She has described her process in interviews with The Guardian, NPR, and elsewhere as slow and careful rather than prolific.

The routine, to the extent it can be reconstructed, was built around return rather than accumulation. She went back to Homer regularly, reread the translations, consulted the Greek. She went back to her own drafts and revised them, sometimes heavily. The book grew in layers over years, with each pass adding something the previous one couldn't see. The mid-process decision to discard the first draft and restart is the most dramatic single event in the routine's history, but it's consistent with everything else she's described: a writer for whom getting it right matters more than getting it done.

Circe followed a similar pattern, arriving seven years after The Song of Achilles. By the time it came out in 2018, Miller had spent enough time inside the Homeric universe that she could move through it sideways, picking up a character the epics had discarded and building a full novel around her interiority. The routine that produced both books is really a posture toward the work: patient, scholarship-driven, willing to wait for the prose to become itself. In a publishing culture that rewards speed, that posture is rarer than any specific habit or schedule.

What You Can Steal

Miller's process is uncomfortable to study if you've been sold the idea that writing fast is writing well. But there's more usable material here than it might first appear:

  • The willingness to scrap what isn't working is a skill, not a failure. Miller threw out years of work because the voice wasn't right. The draft wasn't wasted time, it was the education that made the second draft possible. If a version of your manuscript isn't working, your investment in it is not a reason to continue.
  • Live inside your source material before you try to inhabit it on the page. Miller taught Latin and Greek for years while writing. She didn't research the Iliad and move on. She stayed inside it until it stopped being external. Whatever world your novel lives in, you should know it better than your reader, by a long margin.
  • Revision is where the voice gets found. A first draft is a hypothesis. The voice in your novel may not exist yet when you start, and the way to find it is to go back, not forward. Miller's slow pace came from a writer who understood that rewriting is the real work.
  • Love the scholarship for its own sake. Miller's fiction works because the research wasn't a chore. She genuinely wanted to know what the ancient texts said, how they moved, what they left out. The emotional authority in her prose comes from that. Interest you're performing will read differently than interest you actually feel.
  • Slow output is not the same as weak discipline. Two novels in a decade looks like a slow career. The result is two books that people read with genuine attention, that win major literary prizes, and that hold up to rereading. There are writers who produce a book every eighteen months and never once achieve what Miller achieved with a decade's patience. The metric that matters is not volume.
