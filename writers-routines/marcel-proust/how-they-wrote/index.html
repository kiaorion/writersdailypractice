How Did Proust Write In Search of Lost Time?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Proust didn't so much finish In Search of Lost Time as keep feeding it. He wrote the thing by hand, lying in bed, over more than a decade, and he never really stopped adding to it, gluing and pinning fresh strips of paper onto pages that were already crammed full.

The Direct Answer
In bed, by hand, in notebooks, expanding the manuscript endlessly with pinned-on paper add-ons.
The famous strips he pasted into his drafts became known as paperoles, and the longest reportedly ran to nearly two meters.

Writing From Bed, in Notebooks

Proust wrote most of the novel from a semi-recumbent position, half propped on pillows with his knees for a desk. The scholar Diana Fuss described him working 'suspended midway between the realms of sleeping and waking.' He filled school notebooks, the cahiers, in a fast, sloping hand that's barely legible, working through the night in the cork-lined room while the rest of Paris slept.

Writing flat on his back was slow and awkward, and the pages show it. The cahiers are crossed out, looped over, and crammed into the margins. Céleste Albaret and a series of typists helped him wrestle the growing pile of paper, and later the typescripts, into something a printer could actually set.

He started the project around 1909 and was still buried in it when he died in 1922. The opening volume, Swann's Way, was turned down by several publishers. André Gide, reading for the Nouvelle Revue Française, waved it away and later wrote to apologize, calling the rejection one of the great regrets of his life. Proust paid to publish it himself, through Grasset, in 1913.

The Paperoles

Here is the part that makes archivists wince. Proust revised by addition. Instead of rewriting a page, he would draft new material on separate strips and scraps of paper and paste or pin them to the existing draft, at the top, the bottom, and the edges, until a single sheet unfolded like an accordion. These became his paperoles, and the longest reportedly ran close to two meters. He kept it up right onto the printed galley proofs, so the book swelled with every round of corrections.

The approach also suited a man who worked lying down. He could reach for a scrap, scribble a thought, and fix it in place without ever leaving the bed, so the manuscript could keep shifting whenever an idea arrived, at three in the morning as easily as at dusk.

I should construct my book, I dare not say ambitiously like a cathedral, but quite simply like a dress.

Marcel Proust, Time Regained

The method matched the mind. Proust wasn't chasing a tidy plot so much as trying to catch how memory really behaves, looping back on itself and swelling with detail. A form that could grow in every direction was about the only one that could hold what he was after.

The First World War handed him extra years before the second volume could appear, and he used them to expand the whole design enormously. What might have been a shorter book became seven volumes and roughly 1.25 million words. He was reportedly still reworking the passage on a character's death near the end, drawing on what he felt as he himself was fading. By Céleste Albaret's account, Proust came to her one night near the close of his life and told her he had finally written the word 'Fin', the end, adding that now he could die. Scholars argue over exactly when that page was actually drafted, and he kept correcting proofs regardless, but the image has stuck for a reason: a dying man who had poured his whole life into a single book and had just, at last, reached its final word.

What You Can Steal

  • Draft ugly, then layer. Proust's masterpiece grew by accretion. Get raw material down first, then build on top of it.
  • Sometimes you revise by adding. A scene can get stronger when you deepen it rather than trim it. Know which kind of book you're writing.
  • Rejection isn't the verdict. The novel that redefined the form got passed over by the sharpest editor in Paris. Proust published it anyway.
  • Let the project be as big as it needs. He stopped fighting the true size of the book and let it become what it was.
