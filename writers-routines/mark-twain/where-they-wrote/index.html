Where Did Mark Twain Write?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

For close to twenty summers Mark Twain did his best work nowhere near his famous Hartford house. He climbed a hill outside Elmira, New York, shut himself in a one-room octagonal study, and wrote.

The Direct Answer
In a small octagonal study on a hill at Quarry Farm, near Elmira, New York.
His sister-in-law Susan Crane built it for him in 1874, and he later called it the loveliest study you ever saw in a letter to his friend Joseph Twichell.

A study shaped like a pilot house

Twain's permanent home was the grand house in Hartford, Connecticut, and that's where people picture him. The serious drafting happened somewhere else. Between roughly 1871 and 1889 the Clemens family spent their summers at Quarry Farm, the hilltop home of Olivia Clemens's sister, Susan Crane. The farm sat on a hill east of Elmira, New York, with the valley and town spread out below. It was quiet, green, and a long way from the social demands of Hartford, and Twain got a lot done there.

In 1874 Crane surprised him with a private study set on a knoll about a hundred yards from the main house. It was octagonal, maybe twelve feet across, with a peaked roof and a window in each of its walls. People who saw it said it looked a little like the pilot house of a Mississippi steamboat, which suited a man who'd spent his young years piloting one. There's also a practical reason it sat off on its own. Twain was a heavy cigar smoker, and the detached study kept the smoke out of the house while giving him the silence he needed.

Twain loved it. Writing to his friend the Reverend Joseph Twichell that summer, he described what Crane had done for him.

Susie Crane has built the loveliest study for me, you ever saw. It is octagonal, with a peaked roof, each octagon filled with a spacious window.

Mark Twain, letter to Joseph Twichell, 1874

What he wrote up there

The isolation paid off on the page. Over those summers Twain drafted big stretches of The Adventures of Tom Sawyer and Adventures of Huckleberry Finn in that little room, along with parts of Life on the Mississippi and A Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur's Court. That's a startling amount of American literature for one twelve-foot octagon on a hill. The room sat far enough from the house that the family wouldn't drift over and disturb him, and the windows on every side gave him valley views in all directions while he worked.

The setup mattered as much as the view. He could carry his manuscript up the slope after breakfast, settle in, and stay until late afternoon without anyone coming near. No telephone, no callers, no household traffic. For a writer who could be pulled into conversation easily, that distance from the front door did real work. He wrote about how content he was up there, and the books are the evidence.

It's worth being precise about what the study was and wasn't. It wasn't his year-round home and it wasn't where he handled his career, his lectures, or his business schemes. Those lived in Hartford and on the road. Quarry Farm was the place he reserved for the deep, slow work of building a novel, and the octagonal room was the smallest, quietest point inside that retreat. Layered solitude, you could call it. A farm on a hill, a study off from the farmhouse, and one man with his manuscript inside it. The arrangement let him do in a few summers what years of Hartford distraction never could.

The study still exists. In 1952 it was moved to the campus of Elmira College so it could be preserved and seen, and you can visit it there today. Quarry Farm itself is now used by the Center for Mark Twain Studies. For the rest of how he worked once he was inside, see the full Mark Twain writing routine.

What You Can Steal

  • Get a door that closes. Twain's study worked because nobody could reach him in it.
  • Put distance between you and the house. A hundred yards of hill kept interruptions away.
  • Use a seasonal workplace. He saved his hardest writing for the months and the room that suited it.
  • Pick a spot you actually like. He called it the loveliest study he'd ever seen, and he kept going back.
