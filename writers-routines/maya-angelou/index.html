Maya Angelou's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | April 8, 2026 | 9 min read

Maya Angelou rented a hotel room in every city she lived in and arrived every morning at 6:30 AM. She wasn't staying there. She was writing there, from 6:30 until roughly 2 PM, in a room she kept by the month and asked the hotel to strip bare, with the pictures taken down and the walls left empty. She brought a yellow legal pad, a Bible, a thesaurus, a deck of cards, and a bottle of sherry. She wrote longhand until the afternoon, then went home, cooked, and read over what she'd done. She described this routine in full in her 1990 Paris Review interview with George Plimpton, and it's one of the most precisely engineered writing practices on record.

She described this routine in her 1990 Paris Review Art of Fiction interview with George Plimpton, and anyone who writes seriously should read that interview at least once. It's one of the clearest accounts of a working practice ever given by a major American writer, and the specifics of it are worth studying because they're unusually engineered. Angelou wasn't guessing. She'd figured out exactly what conditions her writing mind needed, and she'd reverse-engineered a daily ritual to produce those conditions on demand.

Profile

The Routine at a Glance

Wake Time
Around 5:30 in the morning. She had coffee with her husband, then drove or was driven to the hotel by 6:30. The early start was deliberate, partly because the mornings were her best hours and partly because she wanted to be inside the room before the day's obligations could find her.
Writing Location
A small rented hotel room, kept on a monthly rental arrangement, in whatever city she was living in at the time. She never wrote the books at home. The hotel room was the office, and home was the place she returned to in the afternoon.
Daily Output
Seven or eight hours of writing, roughly 6:30 AM until 2 PM. She didn't talk about a word count. The measure was time. If the writing came, she wrote. If it didn't, she sat in the room and waited until it did.
Tools
Yellow legal pads, a Bible, Roget's Thesaurus, a deck of cards for solitaire, a bottle of sherry, and an ashtray. Later in life the sherry became optional. The legal pads and the Bible never did.
Famous Ritual
The blank walls. She made the hotel strip the room of all decoration before she started the rental, and she kept it that way. Nothing hanging anywhere. The room was a box and she needed it to stay a box.
Books Written This Way
I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings, Gather Together in My Name, Singin' and Swingin' and Gettin' Merry Like Christmas, the rest of the autobiographical series, and her poetry collections.

The Hotel Room

The hotel room is the part of Angelou's routine that most writers find strange the first time they hear about it, and that quickly starts to make sense once they think about it for a minute. She was asked in the Paris Review interview why she didn't write at home, and her answer was practical. At home there were people she loved and who loved her back. The phone rang. There were meals to think about, laundry to fold, friends who might drop by. Every one of these was a real and reasonable demand on her attention, and any one of them could ruin a morning's writing without anyone meaning to. The hotel room solved the problem by putting her in a space where none of those demands could reach her.

The cost was one she could afford by the time she was writing Caged Bird in 1969. She rented the room by the month and kept it indefinitely, moving the arrangement to a new hotel whenever she changed cities. The staff understood she wasn't a guest. She'd arrive before breakfast, she'd leave in the afternoon, and she didn't want room service or fresh towels during the working hours. What she needed from the hotel was a door that locked, a bed she wouldn't use, a desk or table she would, and the absence of her entire regular life for the eight hours she was inside.

The genius of the setup is that it inverts the standard writing-advice about creating a dedicated home office. A home office is a room inside the house, and the house is full of the rest of your life. A rented hotel room is the opposite. It's a room inside a building full of strangers, none of whom know you or want anything from you, which means the only content in the room is the content you brought with you. Angelou had figured out that the material around her while she worked had to be as empty as possible so that the material inside her head could show up and take its place. The hotel room was the cleanest version of that she could find.

The Yellow Legal Pads

The tools she brought were not arbitrary. Each one had a specific job, and together they functioned as a kind of portable writing kit she could deploy in any rented room she happened to be working in that year. The yellow legal pads were the drafting surface. She wrote longhand, in ink, because she'd found that the slower pace of handwriting kept her prose honest in a way typing didn't. The Bible was there for rhythm. She'd grown up in a Black Baptist church in Stamps, Arkansas, and the King James cadences were embedded in her ear before she knew what a sentence was. When she needed to hear a beat, she opened the Bible and read a few verses aloud and the beat came back.

The Roget's Thesaurus was practical. When a word wasn't doing its job, she looked for a better one. The deck of cards is the strangest-looking piece of the kit until you understand what it's for. She played solitaire. Not for entertainment, not on breaks, but while the writing was happening. Her description, in the Plimpton interview, is that solitaire occupied her conscious mind just enough to get it out of the way so her unconscious could do the work. The dealing of the cards was a rhythm she could hold with her hands while her head wandered around looking for a sentence. When the sentence showed up she'd put the cards down, write it, then pick the cards up again.

This is one of the best pieces of practical writing advice I've ever come across, and it's something I've borrowed from her directly. A small, repetitive, thoughtless task running in the background of your working session can actually produce more writing than pure concentration will, because it gives your conscious mind somewhere to park itself. Knitting works. Doodling works. Walking around a room works. Angelou used cards because cards were portable and quiet and she could do them one-handed. Whatever your version of the cards is, it belongs in your writing kit.

Solitaire

The cards deserve their own section because the principle underneath them is bigger than the cards themselves. Angelou's theory, as far as I can reconstruct it from her interviews, was that the part of the mind that produces sentences is not the same part that manages the writing session. The producing part is older, more intuitive, and easily scared off. The managing part is the part that checks the clock, worries about the deadline, rereads what you just wrote, and wonders whether it's any good. If you let the managing part run the session, the producing part will go quiet, because it doesn't like being watched.

Solitaire was Angelou's method of keeping the managing part busy while the producing part did its work. The cards are just complicated enough to hold conscious attention and just simple enough that no real thought is required. You deal, you move cards around, you notice patterns, you reshuffle. Meanwhile, somewhere else in your head, sentences are assembling themselves. When one is ready, it surfaces, and you catch it on the yellow pad. Then you go back to the cards until the next sentence is ready. The hours pass quickly because you're always half-occupied, and at the end of the session you have a stack of pages you don't quite remember producing.

I know writers who have never heard of Angelou's routine and who have independently discovered the same trick with doodling, with knitting, with pacing a room. The trick is almost universal among people who've been writing seriously for decades. The surface activity doesn't matter. What matters is that the managing part of your mind has something small and harmless to chew on so that the producing part can work without being interrupted. Angelou was the first major writer I know of to describe the mechanism openly, and the description is worth the price of the Paris Review interview by itself.

Walls Stripped Bare

The request to strip the walls of the hotel room is the detail that turns Angelou's routine from practical into philosophical. It would have been easier to rent a room and ignore whatever watercolors or landscape prints the hotel had hung on the walls. Instead, she had them removed. Her explanation, in interviews, was that any decoration in the room became something for her eye to catch on, and catching was the enemy of the writing. A mind that's noticing a bad painting across the room is not a mind that's listening for the next sentence. She needed the visual field to be as empty as the mental field was supposed to become.

This is an unusually literal application of a principle every working writer figures out eventually. External clutter becomes internal clutter. The more visual information the room is feeding your eyes, the more of your attention is going to processing that information rather than the writing. Most people deal with this by trying to ignore the clutter, which doesn't really work because the brain keeps spending small amounts of energy filtering things out. Angelou's answer was more efficient. She removed the clutter in advance so there was nothing to filter. The room, once stripped, took exactly zero attention, which meant every bit of her attention was available for the page.

The lesson here is cheap to apply. You don't need to rent a hotel room. You need to look at whatever space you currently write in and notice what's on the walls, on the desk, in your peripheral vision while you work. Every object is a small charge your brain is paying to keep track of. Fewer objects, fewer charges, more attention available for the sentence. Angelou took this to the extreme, which is what writers at her level tend to do once they've figured out what actually works for them. The rest of us can get ninety percent of the benefit by clearing the desk and turning the monitor away from the window.

Maya Angelou's Hotel Room Writing Routine

The Maya Angelou hotel room writing routine started before sunrise. She'd be up at five thirty in the morning, drink coffee with her husband, and leave the house by six thirty to drive to the local hotel where she'd booked a room by the month. She'd arrive by six forty-five, settle in, and be writing by seven. The hotel staff knew not to enter. She'd told them in advance to leave the bed unmade and the wastebaskets full, because anyone coming in to clean would shift the air of the room and break whatever state she'd just spent an hour climbing into.

The morning block ran until somewhere around two in the afternoon, sometimes earlier if the work was going badly and she could see she was only producing pages she'd cut anyway. She wrote longhand on yellow legal pads, propped on her elbow on the bed, with a Bible, a thesaurus, a deck of cards for solitaire, and a bottle of sherry on the nightstand. The cards and the sherry were both decoy work. When the writing stuck, she'd play a few hands of solitaire or sip a small glass while her unconscious caught up to where the prose needed to go next. Then she'd put the cards down and write again.

She'd leave the hotel by mid-afternoon, drive home, and shower the room off. The whole Maya Angelou hotel room writing routine was structured to keep the writing self and the home self in different physical locations. She didn't want to bring the work home, and she didn't want home, with its phone calls and its dishes and its visiting friends, to come into the work. The hotel was the boundary. Evenings went to dinner, friends, music, and the social part of her life that fed the rest of the day. She'd be in bed by ten, ready for the five thirty alarm to start the cycle again.

Sources

The primary source for Angelou's routine is her Paris Review Art of Fiction interview, No. 119, conducted by George Plimpton and published in the fall 1990 issue, which is the fullest description she ever gave of her daily working practice in her own words. Her 2008 book Letter to My Daughter contains scattered references to the writing life. The Academy of Achievement interview conducted in 1997 and archived online covers similar ground. Her own autobiographies, particularly the later volumes in the series beginning with I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings, describe the working conditions of various years without quite giving the step-by-step detail the Plimpton interview gets to. See also: Maya Angelou on Wikipedia.

What You Can Steal

Angelou's routine has more transferable detail than almost any other in the archive. These are the pieces I'd actually copy:

  • Write somewhere that isn't home. It doesn't have to be a hotel room. A library, a quiet cafe you visit only for writing, a room in a friend's house you've borrowed for the morning: anything that removes you from the small demands of your own household. The distance is doing real work.
  • Clear the visual field. Strip the wall in front of your desk. Put the desk against a blank surface. Close the blinds if the view is distracting. The less your eyes have to process, the more of your attention is available for the page.
  • Assemble a portable writing kit. Angelou brought the same objects to every hotel. Yours might be a particular notebook, a specific pen, a small stack of reference books, a pair of headphones. The kit itself becomes a signal to your brain that it's time to write.
  • Give your conscious mind a small job. Solitaire was Angelou's version. Try doodling, knitting, a fidget toy, a slow walk around the room. The job has to be thoughtless enough that it doesn't compete with the writing but regular enough that it absorbs the worrying part of your attention. When you find your version, writing sessions get twice as long without feeling like it.
  • Measure the session in time, not words. Angelou didn't count pages. She counted hours. Show up for the hours, and the words arrive or they don't, but you never beat yourself up about a word count you didn't hit. The hours are the commitment. Everything else is a byproduct.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why did Maya Angelou rent a hotel room to write?

Angelou rented hotel rooms to create a space with no domestic associations. She had the rooms stripped of any decorative items and arrived each morning with yellow legal pads, a Bible, a dictionary, a thesaurus, and a deck of cards. The bare hotel room gave her mind nowhere to go but the page.

What time did Maya Angelou write?

She arrived at the hotel room at 6:30 AM and wrote until about 2 PM. After 2, she returned home, read what she'd written, and did light editing before the day continued.

Where did Maya Angelou describe her writing routine?

The most detailed account is in her 1990 Paris Review interview conducted by George Plimpton, part of the magazine's Art of Fiction series. She also discussed it in numerous television interviews throughout her career.

What did Maya Angelou bring to the hotel room?

Yellow legal pads, a Bible, a dictionary, a thesaurus, a bottle of sherry, and a deck of cards. She used the cards to play solitaire when she got stuck, a way of keeping her hands busy while her mind worked through a problem on the page.
