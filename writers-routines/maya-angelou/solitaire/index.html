Why Did Maya Angelou Play Solitaire While She Wrote?

Kia Orion | July 20, 2026 | 5 min read

A deck of cards doesn't belong on most writers' lists of essential tools. It was on Maya Angelou's, right next to the Bible and the thesaurus, and she had a specific theory for why it worked.

The Direct Answer
She used solitaire to occupy what she called her little mind, a phrase she borrowed from her grandmother, so the deeper, more intuitive part of her attention could work on the writing without interference.
She described this directly in interviews: cards and sometimes crossword puzzles, kept nearby specifically to give her restless, monitoring mind something to do.

Big Mind, Little Mind

Angelou credited her grandmother with the framework she used to explain this habit. Her grandmother spoke of having a little mind, a phrase Angelou adopted and built into her own working theory of attention. The big mind, in her version, was capable of the real depth a piece of writing demanded. The little mind was the part that fidgeted, checked the clock, and second-guessed a sentence before it had a chance to finish forming.

Left alone, the little mind tends to take over a writing session. It's the part of you that notices the work isn't going well and starts worrying about it mid-sentence, which is usually enough to stop the sentence from finishing at all. Angelou's solution was to give that part of her mind a job simple enough to keep it occupied without requiring any real thought, rather than trying to fight it into silence.

Why Cards Specifically

Solitaire fits a narrow, useful gap. It's engaging enough to hold conscious attention, since you're tracking cards and looking for moves, but repetitive and low-stakes enough that it doesn't demand anything close to full thought. That combination is what made it work as a decoy.

Usually a deck of cards and some crossword puzzles. Something to occupy my little mind.

Maya Angelou, The Daily Beast, "How I Write"

The mechanism matters more than the specific game. Any activity that's absorbing without being demanding could theoretically do the same job, occupying the surface-level, anxious part of attention while something quieter does the actual composing. Angelou happened to land on cards, in a rented hotel room, with a bottle of sherry on the same nightstand for the same reason: keep the fidgety part of the mind busy, and let the writing happen somewhere the fidgeting can't reach.

What You Can Steal

  • Give your inner critic a separate job. A repetitive, low-stakes task can absorb the anxious part of your attention while the real work happens elsewhere.
  • Match the decoy activity to the problem. It needs to be engaging enough to hold attention, but simple enough to require no real thought.
  • Name the two parts of your attention. Angelou's big mind and little mind framework makes the split easier to notice and manage.
  • Don't fight your own restlessness head-on. Redirecting it can work better than trying to suppress it through willpower alone.
