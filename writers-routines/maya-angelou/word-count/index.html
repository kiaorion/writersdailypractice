How Many Pages a Day Did Maya Angelou Write?

Kia Orion | August 2, 2026 | 5 min read

Angelou is one of the few writers who gave a real number for her daily output, and it isn't the number people expect. She kept a bare hotel room by the month, wrote lying across the bed on yellow legal pads, and went home in the early afternoon with a stack of pages she was about to cut to pieces.

The Direct Answer
Nine pages was a good day. She kept two and a half or three of them.
The figure is Angelou's own, from her 1990 Paris Review interview with George Plimpton. She left home at six, aimed to be working by six-thirty, and stopped somewhere between half past twelve and half past one. She measured the day in pages and hours and never once gave a word count.

Nine Pages In, Three Pages Out

The passage everyone half-remembers gets rounded up in the retelling. Various sites will tell you she wrote ten or twelve pages and cut to three or four. Here is what she actually said.

If I've done nine pages I may be able to save two and a half or three. That's the cruelest time, you know, to really admit that it doesn't work.

Maya Angelou, The Paris Review, The Art of Fiction No. 119, 1990

Read that again and notice the survival rate. Roughly a third of a morning's work made it through. The cruelty she's describing is the evening pass, when she went home, showered, ate dinner, and then looked at what she'd written with the day's optimism worn off.

Why Pages and Not Words

Angelou wrote longhand on yellow legal pads, lying across the bed with one elbow braced. She said the elbow was permanently calloused from the position. When you draft that way, a page is a physical thing you can see and stack. A word count is an abstraction you'd have to stop and calculate, and she wasn't going to interrupt a morning to do arithmetic.

The room supported the same logic. She rented it by the month, made the hotel strip the walls of every picture, and brought a dictionary, a Bible, a legal pad, and a deck of cards. What Nine Pages Looks Like in Words

If you want a rough conversion, a handwritten legal pad page runs somewhere near 200 to 250 words. Nine pages puts her drafting output around 2,000 words in a session of six or seven hours. What survived the edit was closer to 700. That's the number worth carrying around, because it's the one that describes the actual pace of finished prose from a writer with seven autobiographies to her name.

Compare it with Stephen King, who targets 2,000 words a day and keeps most of them because he revises later, in a separate pass. Angelou revised the same day, on the same pages, which is why her drafting number and her keeping number are so far apart. Two systems, both defensible, and the choice between them is really a choice about when you're willing to be honest with yourself.

She was also clear that the ease was manufactured. In the same interview she said it takes her forever to get the language to look easy. Nine pages down to three is what that looks like in practice.

Stretch the ratio across a career and it stops looking slow. Three surviving pages a day, five days a week, is a full manuscript in under a year. Angelou published seven autobiographies between I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings in 1969 and Mom & Me & Mom in 2013, plus poetry collections, essay collections, and screenplays, across a working life that also involved touring, teaching, and a professorship at Wake Forest.

The hotel room paid for itself inside that arithmetic. Renting a second space by the month is an expense most writers would flinch at, and Angelou treated it as the cost of getting six uninterrupted hours she could not talk herself out of. The room had no phone she wanted to answer and nothing on the walls to look at. What it bought was the nine pages.

What You Can Steal

  • Count in whatever unit you can see. Angelou counted pages because pages stack up on a bed. Pick the unit that gives you feedback without interrupting the work.
  • Expect to keep a third. Nine pages down to three is a healthy ratio, not a bad day. Plan your schedule around the survival rate rather than the draft rate.
  • Separate the writing room from the living room. She left home at six and came back after the work was done. The commute did the psychological switching.
  • Do the cutting when the day's optimism has worn off. She read the morning's pages in the evening, which is when she could tell what didn't work.
