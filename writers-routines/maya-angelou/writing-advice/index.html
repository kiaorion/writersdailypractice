Maya Angelou's Best Writing Advice

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

People loved to call Maya Angelou a natural. She hated it. Her advice, drawn straight from her own habits, was about labor, courage, and refusing to mistake an easy read for easy writing.

The Direct Answer
Work at the language until it sings. Talent is the small part.
She bristled at being called a "natural writer" in her 1990 Paris Review interview.

It's Work, Not Magic

Angelou drafted ten or twelve pages on a good morning and kept maybe three. The rest she cut. That ratio is the advice in its purest form. Nothing about her process suggested that words arrived in finished shape. They arrived rough, in piles, and she carved them down over hours and days until something held.

So when critics waved her off as a born talent, she pushed back hard. There was a particular kind of praise she couldn't stand, the sort that called her a natural and quietly erased the labor underneath. The people who said that, she told the Paris Review, "are the ones I want to grab by the throat and wrestle to the floor because it takes me forever to get it to sing. I work at the language." The phrase to sit with is "work at the language." She treated words like material to be shaped, not gifts to be received.

The goal was prose that felt effortless and cost her everything to make. "I try to pull the language in to such a sharpness that it jumps off the page," she said. "It must look easy, but it takes me forever to get it to look so easy." That's the trick at the heart of her advice. The smoothness a reader feels is the residue of enormous hidden effort. Years later she put the same idea more bluntly to the Daily Beast: "Easy reading is damn hard writing." If a sentence reads like a breeze, somebody sweated over it.

It takes me forever to get it to sing. I work at the language.

Maya Angelou, The Paris Review, 1990

Courage First

Asked which virtue mattered most, she didn't hesitate. "Courage, first," she said, calling it "the most important of all the virtues" because "without that virtue you can't practice any other virtue with consistency." That isn't a vague motivational line when you apply it to a desk. For a writer, courage means the nerve to write a bad page in the morning and the nerve to throw most of it out by evening. Both halves take guts.

She gave herself permission to fail on the page every single day, then went hunting for the few lines worth saving. The willingness to produce something clumsy was the price of admission. Without it, she'd have frozen, waiting for the perfect sentence that never shows up on command. Courage let her start ugly and trust the rewriting to do the real work later.

Her process backed the philosophy at every turn. She showed up at the same bare room at the same early hour and put words down whether or not they were any good, then read them back near five and cut without mercy. Discipline is what made the courage repeatable. The schedule removed the question of whether she felt like writing, so the only thing left to be brave about was the writing itself.

She even had a trick for the part of the brain that loves to bolt. Angelou described splitting her mind in two, a "big mind" that did the deep work and a "little mind" that wanted to fidget and run off. She kept a deck of cards and crossword puzzles in the room to occupy the little mind, giving its restlessness somewhere harmless to go so the big mind could stay on the page. The lesson underneath it is humble: a working writer manages distraction instead of pretending to be above it.

Put it all together and her advice is unglamorous on purpose. Stop calling it talent. Sharpen the language until it looks easy, knowing the ease is a lie you earned. Find the courage to write badly and then cut. Build a routine sturdy enough that you don't have to decide each day whether to write. None of it sounds romantic, which is exactly why it worked for decades.

What You Can Steal

  • Drop the word "natural." Treat good writing as work you do, not a gift you have.
  • Make it look easy by working hard. Sharpen the language until it jumps off the page.
  • Be willing to cut most of it. She kept three pages out of ten and called that a normal day.
  • Show up with courage. Write the bad page so you have something to fix.
