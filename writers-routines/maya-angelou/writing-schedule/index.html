What Time of Day Did Maya Angelou Write?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Maya Angelou was a morning writer in the strictest sense. She left the house before most people had finished coffee and was already at work by half past six. The afternoons and evenings belonged to a different version of her.

The Direct Answer
She left home at six and was at work by 6:30 a.m.
She kept those hours for months at a stretch, per her 1990 Paris Review interview.

The Morning Block

Angelou's day started early and ran on a tight rail. "I rent a hotel room for a few months," she told the Paris Review in 1990, "leave my home at six, and try to be at work by six-thirty." By the time most people were waking up, she'd already been at it for an hour. She'd write straight through the morning, lying across a made-up bed in a bare rented room, until the energy thinned out. There was no warm-up ritual, no slow start over coffee. She got in and she worked.

The early hour did two things. It put her at the page when her mind was freshest, before the day's demands had a chance to crowd in. And it gave her a long stretch of uninterrupted time, since nobody else she knew was awake to call or knock. She'd come to the same room at the same hour, day after day, for the few months it took to get a book moving. The rhythm itself became part of the work.

She didn't push into the afternoon. "I stay until twelve-thirty or one-thirty in the afternoon, and then I go home and try to breathe." Five or six hours, then she stopped. She didn't drain the tank to the last drop. Stopping while there was still something left meant she had somewhere to start the next morning, instead of staring at a blank page with a tired head.

I leave my home at six, and try to be at work by six-thirty.

Maya Angelou, The Paris Review, 1990

What Happened After She Stopped

The work wasn't done when she left the room. The morning made the mess; the rest of the day quietly cleaned it up. She'd go home, decompress, and come back to the pages with rested eyes. "I look at the work around five," she said. "I have an orderly dinner, proper, quiet, lovely dinner; and then I go back to work the next morning." The evening look told her what was worth keeping and what to throw out before she started fresh.

That split mattered. Drafting and judging are two different jobs, and Angelou refused to do them at the same time. Morning was for generating raw material with no critic looking over her shoulder. The five o'clock pass was where the editor finally got to speak. By the time she sat down again the next morning, the previous day's work was already trimmed and waiting.

On a strong day she might draft ten or twelve pages and keep three or four. Some mornings the math was worse. "If I've done nine pages I may be able to save two and a half or three," she admitted. That brutal ratio is exactly why the early start mattered so much. She needed the runway to write badly first and rescue the good parts later, and a 6:30 a.m. clock-in gave her the hours to do both.

There was room for a drink in the schedule too, though she kept it loose. The bottle of sherry rode along with her pads and her Bible. "I might have it at six-fifteen a.m. just as soon as I get in," she said, "but usually it's about eleven o'clock when I'll have a glass of sherry." Mostly it punctuated the morning rather than fueling it, a small marker partway through the long stretch at the bed.

What ties the whole schedule together is consistency over intensity. She didn't wait for inspiration to strike, and she didn't binge-write for eighteen hours when it did. She showed up at the same early hour, worked a contained block, stopped before she was wrung out, and let the evening sort the wheat from the chaff. The next morning she did it again. Books got finished because the clock, not the muse, ran the day.

What You Can Steal

  • Start before the world wakes up. A 6:30 a.m. start meant nobody could interrupt her best hours.
  • Cap the session. She quit by early afternoon instead of grinding until empty.
  • Split drafting and editing across the day. Write in the morning, read it back near five.
  • Protect the wind-down. The orderly dinner reset her for the next morning's start.
