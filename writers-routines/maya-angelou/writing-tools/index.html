What Tools Did Maya Angelou Use to Write?

Kia Orion | July 20, 2026 | 5 min read

Maya Angelou's hotel room was stripped of decoration, not bare, stocked with a specific, deliberate kit where every item had a job.

The Direct Answer
Yellow legal pads, a pencil or pen, a Bible, a Roget's Thesaurus, a deck of cards, and a bottle of sherry, each one serving a distinct purpose in how she worked.
She described this kit in detail in her 1990 Paris Review interview, and the combination stayed consistent across the decades she kept a rented room to write in.

The Legal Pad and the Bible

The yellow legal pad was where the actual drafting happened. Angelou wrote longhand, lying across the bed and propped on one elbow for so many hours over so many years that she developed calluses from the position. She's said the slower pace of handwriting kept her prose more honest than typing did, forcing a kind of deliberateness a keyboard doesn't.

The Bible served a different function altogether, closer to a tuning fork than a reference book. Angelou grew up in the Black Baptist church in Stamps, Arkansas, and the King James cadences were in her ear before she had any formal sense of what a sentence was. When her own writing lost its rhythm, she'd read a few verses aloud, not for content but for beat, and use that rhythm to find her way back into the sound of her own sentences.

The Thesaurus, and What the Bible Was Really For

Roget's Thesaurus handled the more practical problem, the moment a word wasn't doing its job and she needed a better one nearby. It's the least surprising item in the kit, standard equipment for plenty of working writers. The Bible is where her explanation gets more specific about what she was actually listening for.

The language of all the interpretations, the translations, of the Judaic Bible and the Christian Bible, is musical, just wonderful. I read the Bible to myself...to hear the language, hear the rhythm, and remind myself how beautiful English is.

Maya Angelou, The Paris Review, 1990

That's a writer using a text for its sound as much as its meaning, which explains why the Bible sat next to a thesaurus rather than replacing it. One tool solved word choice. The other solved music. Angelou needed both working at once, because her prose, like her poetry, depended on rhythm as much as it depended on precision, and a kit built around only one of those needs would have left the other unaddressed for hours at a stretch.

What You Can Steal

  • Build a kit around distinct jobs, not just habit. Every item in Angelou's bag solved a specific problem, from word choice to rhythm to focus.
  • Read something aloud when your prose loses its music. A few verses or a favorite passage can reset your ear faster than editing on the page can.
  • Keep a reference for precision nearby. A thesaurus earns its place when a word is close but not exact.
  • Write slower on purpose sometimes. Angelou credited longhand with keeping her prose more honest than a keyboard allowed.
