How Does N.K. Jemisin Build Her Worlds?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Jemisin's worlds feel engineered down to the geology, which makes people assume she maps everything before she writes a word. Her actual method starts smaller and messier, with a character and a test run. Here's how she builds a world she can trust for a whole novel.

The Direct Answer
She starts with a character, then writes a 'proof of concept' short story to test the world before committing a novel to it.
She test-drives a setting in a short story, refining the magic and the voice until they hold, per her Cornell talk and craft interviews.

Character First, Then the World

For all the geological detail in her books, Jemisin calls herself a character-focused writer. The spark tends to be a person, and the world grows to explain and to pressure that person. With her Dreamblood books, she's said the first image was someone slipping into a room to kill for religious reasons, and the religion, the culture, and the strange moon overhead all grew out of asking what would produce a person like that. People shape their beliefs around what surrounds them, so she builds the surroundings to fit the belief.

A lot of her raw material arrives sideways. She's said much of her work starts from strange dreams that she then tries to explain in logical terms, building a logic that fits the dream. Scattered pieces of ideas can sit around for years before they suddenly gel into something usable, which is one reason her worlds feel discovered as much as designed.

This is why her research isn't set dressing. If a world runs on geology, she reads geology. If it's populated by real cultures, she studies them and brings in sensitivity readers rather than flattening the specifics, whether that's 1980s hip-hop or the texture of one New York neighborhood. She's talked about refusing to contribute to the simplifying of her own culture's nuances.

The Proof of Concept

Before she trusts a world with an entire novel, she tests it in a short story. She calls these 'proof of concept' pieces, small stories that let her drive a setting around and watch how readers react before she scales it up. The Broken Earth trilogy grew out of exactly this. An earlier short story called 'Stone Hunger' used orogeny, an earth-moving magic, rendered through the senses.

Moving toward the novel, she kept the world but reworked the magic so it could read as a science that had gone wrong, and she wrote test chapters to find the right voice. That search is where the second-person narration of The Fifth Season came from. She's described it as something that just clicked once she tried it. The 'you' running through Essun's chapters surfaced during that testing rather than being planned up front as a gimmick.

She's run the same play more than once. The short story 'The City Born Great' became the seed for her New York novel The City We Became, a way to test the premise of a city waking up as a living thing before she built a book and a trilogy on top of it. The short-story trial run is close to a fixed step in how she works.

If you are trying to reimagine the world, it's a lot easier to do that when you can build the world from scratch.

N.K. Jemisin, Cornell University, 2023

What You Can Steal

  • Start with a person, not a map. Let the world grow to explain the character.
  • Test a world in a short story first. Cheaper than finding the flaws 90,000 words in.
  • Do the real research. If it runs on geology, read some geology.
  • Let the voice emerge from testing. Her second-person POV was found, not planned.
