N.K. Jemisin's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 14, 2026 | 9 min read

N.K. Jemisin won the Hugo Award for Best Novel three years in a row, from 2016 to 2018, one for each book in the Broken Earth trilogy. No author had ever done that before. The record is startling enough on its own, but what makes it more interesting is that she wrote the first book in that trilogy while still working a day job as a counseling psychologist, squeezing the pages out around a career that involved actively listening to other people's pain for forty hours a week. The achievement didn't come from having the ideal conditions. She made it from a fairly constrained position, one manuscript at a time.

Most coverage of Jemisin focuses on the history-making wins and the formal daring of the Broken Earth books, specifically the second-person narration of The Fifth Season, which addresses the reader as "you" throughout an entire apocalyptic fantasy novel. What gets less attention is the grinding, practical reality of how she built the writing life that produced that work. The routine changed significantly once she left the day job, and both versions, the constrained working-writer years and the post-Patreon full-time years, have something useful in them.

Walking as thinking. She has spoken about using walks to work through plot problems, moving her body when the draft is stuck rather than staring at the screen waiting for it to unstick.

The Day-Job Years

Before the Broken Earth trilogy made her the most decorated fantasy writer of the decade, Jemisin spent years writing around a career in mental health counseling. She worked as a psychologist for a significant portion of her publishing career, including through the writing of her debut, The Hundred Thousand Kingdoms, which came out in 2010 and was immediately nominated for the Hugo and Nebula awards. That book was written by someone who clocked in at a day job and wrote in the margins.

The counseling background is worth understanding as more than a biographical footnote. Clinical psychology trains you to pay attention to subtext, to what people won't say directly, to the gap between what someone claims to feel and what their body language contradicts. That kind of attention is exactly what epic fantasy usually lacks. Fantasy that spends a thousand pages on world-building and geopolitics and magic systems often forgets to notice how people actually hold themselves when they're scared, or how a survivor's internal monologue sounds different from a bystander's. Jemisin's psychology training gave her a second set of tools for the interior life of a character, and you can feel it in the Broken Earth books, where the trauma responses are documented with a clinical specificity that's rare in the genre.

Her 2014 blog post on nkjemisin.com, written when she was still in the psychology field, describes the difficulty of the split life frankly. She talks about writing exhausted after long days of client sessions, about the mental drain of that kind of work and how it competed with the imaginative demands of drafting fiction. Counseling, she wrote, requires you to be fully present for someone else's interior life for hours at a stretch. Coming home to do the same for fictional characters took a different kind of attention, but it was still attention, and there was only so much of it in a day. The constraint was real and she didn't romanticize it.

What she didn't do was wait for the constraint to lift before taking her writing seriously. The Fifth Season, the book that started the three-consecutive-Hugo run, was written while the day job was still in place. She found time somewhere in the structure of that life and used it. The specific hours aren't documented in detail, but the output is. Three published novels in the Inheritance trilogy, stories, essays, all of it produced around the job.

The Patreon Shift

In 2016, Jemisin launched a Patreon campaign and hit its funding goal quickly enough to let her leave the psychology work and write full-time. The timing was almost exactly when The Fifth Season won its first Hugo, and the two events together mark a clean before-and-after in her career. She's talked about the Patreon campaign in interviews and on her blog as something she approached with real uncertainty about whether it would work, and genuine relief when it did.

The shift mattered practically because it changed the structure of her writing days entirely. A writer carving time around a day job is always operating under resource scarcity. You guard the two available hours and you try to get into the work fast and stay there. A writer with the whole day has a different problem. The structure that used to be imposed from outside, by the job's schedule, has to come from inside instead. Some writers find that much harder. Jemisin seems to have adapted quickly.

In interviews after the transition, she describes writing during regular working hours, treating it like a job with something closer to office hours than the stolen-time model she'd been running before. She's spoken publicly about the freedom being real but also requiring a different kind of discipline. When your schedule is finally yours, the question of how to organize it is yours too, and nobody is going to do it for you. She talks about this with the directness of someone who spent years in a helping profession and knows what it costs not to take your own needs seriously as a worker.

The Patreon model also changed her relationship with her readers in a structural way. Monthly updates, a direct line between the work and the people funding it. That kind of visibility creates its own accountability rhythm. It doesn't replace the internal discipline of sitting down to write, but it supplements it. She knew, in a concrete way, that the work had a waiting audience. That's a different kind of pressure from a traditional publishing contract, and it comes with a different texture of motivation.

Coffee Shops and Walking

Two things come up consistently when Jemisin talks about her working environment. She writes in coffee shops. She walks when she's stuck.

The coffee shop preference is common enough among writers that it's almost a cliche, but Jemisin has explained the logic of it in a way that cuts through the cliche. At home, the things that need doing are visible. The laundry, the dishes, the email, the general clutter of a lived life, all of it competes for attention in a way that a coffee shop doesn't. In a cafe, your options are basically write or sit there doing nothing. The environment enforces a kind of productive constraint. You paid for a drink, you're in public, you have your laptop open. The social context makes inaction feel more awkward than it does at a home desk where you could easily justify an hour of reorganizing the bookshelf.

There's also something in the ambient noise. A coffee shop at mid-morning has a low hum of conversation, the sound of an espresso machine, the occasional scrape of a chair. For some writers that's too distracting. For others it's just enough signal that the brain has something to process at a low level, which keeps it from manufacturing its own distractions. Jemisin falls in the second category. She's described finding coffee shop ambient noise a useful backdrop rather than an intrusion.

She writes because she has stories she wants to tell, and she doesn't always know where they're going when she starts.
N.K. Jemisin, on her process

The walking habit is separately documented and worth taking seriously as craft advice. When a scene isn't working, she moves. She's described this in interviews as a deliberate strategy rather than a restless displacement activity. Walking changes your physical state, which changes your mental state. A problem that felt like a wall when you were sitting at a screen sometimes looks like a puzzle with a visible solution after twenty minutes on foot. The body and the brain are less separate than writing culture tends to treat them, and Jemisin seems to have figured that out intuitively.

The Second-Person Gamble and What the Routine Made Possible

The Fifth Season opens with an earthquake that cracks a continent, told in second person. "You are she. She is you." The choice to address the reader as "you" throughout a 450-page fantasy novel is either a very bold structural decision or a very foolish one, and the genre has plenty of examples of formal experiments that collapsed under their own weight. This one didn't. It held across three books and became one of the more discussed narrative techniques in fantasy fiction of the last decade.

The second-person gamble is worth connecting to the routine because it's the kind of decision that takes a particular kind of confidence to execute. You have to write a hundred pages into a technique before you know if it's going to work, and for a writer still balancing a day job, with limited drafting hours, committing that much of your scarce writing time to an experiment that might not pan out requires some particular conviction about the material. Jemisin has said in interviews that the second person felt necessary for the story she was trying to tell, specifically because the protagonist, Essun, has a dissociated relationship with her own identity, and the "you" creates a formal distance that mirrors her psychology. The background in counseling psychology matters here. That's a clinical insight being used as a narrative device.

She's talked in various interviews about writing the kind of fantasy she wanted to read. Specifically, she wanted genre fiction that took its Black characters seriously, that didn't center the white gaze, and that was formally ambitious rather than content to reproduce existing conventions. Those are the motivations that produced the second-person choice, the geological apocalypse setting, the orogeny magic system built on a metaphor for oppression and the exploitation of bodies. The routine that made the work possible, the stitched-together day-job years, the restructured full-time years, was in service of a very specific artistic vision. The practical and the formal were always connected.

What's useful to take from that is the clarity of the vision rather than the specific technique. Jemisin knew what she was trying to do in The Fifth Season before she knew exactly how to do it, and she was willing to write a structurally unusual book to get there. Writers who are less clear on what they're actually after tend to drift toward safe choices when the draft gets hard. She didn't have the luxury of drifting. The hours were too scarce for experiments she wasn't committed to.

Sources

Jemisin has documented her working life across her own blog at nkjemisin.com, which includes posts from the day-job years and the Patreon transition. The New York Times Magazine profile by Reggie Ugwu, published in 2018 around the release of The Stone Sky, covers her career arc and the Hugo wins in depth, including details about her psychology background and how she funded the full-time writing life. Her 2018 conversation with Dave Itzkoff in The New York Times, and a separate long interview with Wired from the same period, both discuss her working methods and the formal choices in the Broken Earth trilogy. Her Hugo acceptance speeches, available via the Hugo Awards archive, are worth reading for her own framing of the work and what she was trying to accomplish.

What You Can Steal

Jemisin's route to three consecutive Hugos was long and practical, and most of the useful lessons are in the logistics rather than the headline achievement:

  • Write around the day job instead of waiting for it to end. Jemisin's debut came out while she was still in the psychology field. The day job years are when you build the catalog and the audience, not a waiting room before your real career begins.
  • Treat your writing background as material. Her counseling psychology training shaped the interior lives of her characters in ways that distinguish her work from the genre average. Whatever your other expertise is, it belongs in the fiction.
  • Change your environment when the draft stalls. The coffee shop when you need to get words down. The walk when you need to think through what comes next. These aren't avoidance; they're tools with specific functions.
  • Commit to a formal experiment before you know if it'll work. The second-person narration in The Fifth Season was a structural risk. Jemisin took it because the story required it, not because it was safe. Know what your story requires, then build toward that.
  • The Patreon model is real infrastructure, not a tip jar. When the funding cleared, it changed the entire shape of her days. If you're serious about going full-time, building a direct relationship with your readers before you make the leap is worth taking seriously as a business decision.
