Nora Roberts's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 14, 2026 | 9 min read

Nora Roberts started writing in 1979 because a blizzard locked her in her house in rural Maryland with two small boys and no way out. She had stories in her head that she'd been meaning to get to someday, and suddenly someday had arrived, whether she wanted it or not. She wrote her first novel by hand at her kitchen table. That book didn't sell, and neither did the next few. She kept writing anyway. By the time she was selling regularly, she'd already developed the habit that would eventually produce more than 200 novels: she showed up at her desk every morning and put words on the page, regardless of how she felt about it.

The prolific writer question, the one people always ask about Roberts, tends to get framed wrong. They want to know the secret, the system, the productivity hack. Her answer has been consistent for decades. There is no secret. You sit down. You write. You come back tomorrow and do it again. Among the writers who've ever lived, very few have matched her output across such a sustained span of years, and the explanation she gives is so plain it almost sounds like deflection. It isn't. The plainness is the point.

Profile

The Routine at a Glance

Wake Time
She writes on a roughly nine-to-five schedule, treating fiction writing as a professional job rather than a creative vocation waiting on mood or inspiration.
Writing Location
A home office in Boonsboro, Maryland, where she has written for most of her career. She's known for working in the same dedicated space day after day.
Daily Output
She's described aiming for roughly eight hours of writing per day, five days a week, across a career spanning more than four decades. Specific daily word counts vary by project and draft stage.
Tools
Computer. She switched from a typewriter to a word processor early in her career and hasn't looked back.
Famous Ritual
The "ass in the chair" philosophy: the belief that showing up and writing, day after day without exception, is the only thing that actually produces books.
Books Written This Way
200+ novels as Nora Roberts, including the In Death series written as J.D. Robb (which alone runs to over 60 installments), trilogies, standalones, and short fiction.

Ass in the Chair

The phrase Roberts is most closely associated with is not a metaphor. She means it literally. You put your body in the chair. You open the document. You write the next sentence, and then the one after that, until the session is done. Then you come back tomorrow. If the words are bad, you fix them in revision. If the scene isn't working, you push through it and see what's on the other side. You do not wait for a better day or a cleaner head or the right kind of light. Writers who wait for the right conditions to write are, by her accounting, people who don't write very much.

In interviews over the years, Roberts has been almost impatient with the muse mythology, the idea that inspiration is something that descends on you from outside and that your job is to be ready when it arrives. She's said, in various phrasings, that you can't edit a blank page. The first draft exists to be fixed. Its only job is to exist. And it can only exist if you sat down and wrote it, on a day when you probably didn't feel particularly inspired to write anything at all.

This is a philosophy, but it's also a skill. The ability to write through resistance, to produce usable material on days when the work feels flat and the well seems dry, is something you build over time by doing it repeatedly. Roberts has been doing it for more than forty years. The early books trained her to show up. The later books are what she can do once showing up is automatic.

For writers in contemporary romance especially, this matters. The genre rewards volume. Series readers come back for more books, and more books only exist if someone writes them. Roberts built her readership, in part, by delivering consistently, year after year. The productivity and the quality aren't separate things. The habit that produced 200 books is the same habit that kept the books good enough for readers to keep buying them.

The Three-Draft Method

Roberts has described her drafting process in terms that make the messy reality of novel-writing easier to hold. The first draft is what she sometimes calls the discovery draft: you're throwing everything on the page, finding out what the book actually is, not worrying about whether any given sentence is good. The second draft is the layering draft, where she goes back through and adds depth, fixes what's missing, builds the emotional and thematic material that the first pass was too fast to catch. The third draft is the polish, where the sentence-level work happens and the prose gets clean.

What this framework does is give each draft a single purpose, which makes each pass much less exhausting than trying to do everything at once. Writers who attempt to write a perfect first draft tend to freeze, because the standards they're trying to meet at the sentence level are incompatible with the exploratory looseness a first draft needs. Roberts separates the two problems into different drafts and solves them separately. The first draft doesn't have to be good. It has to exist so the second draft has something to work with.

There's also a cognitive economy here worth paying attention to. A novel is a long project, and the longer a project runs, the more opportunities there are for doubt to creep in. If you're trying to draft and polish simultaneously, the project never feels done enough to be satisfying, and the gaps between where it is and where it needs to be stay visible the whole time. Separating the drafts means each stage can feel genuinely finished when it is, and you can close the document knowing you did what that pass was for. The next problem belongs to the next draft.

Sustaining Output Across Decades

"You can't edit a blank page."

Most writers who become prolific early in their careers don't stay prolific. The pace that produced their first ten books collapses under the weight of success, distraction, or simply the accumulation of options that fame brings. Roberts is one of a small number of commercial novelists who've maintained serious output for long enough that the catalog starts to look less like a career and more like a geological formation. She started publishing in the early 1980s and hasn't stopped. The In Death series alone, which she writes as J.D. Robb and began in 1995, has run to more than 60 books, with new installments still appearing.

The sustaining factor, by her own account, is that she treats writing as a job, with the same structure a salaried position carries. She goes to her office. She writes for the hours her workday runs. She doesn't finish early because she hit her quota and she doesn't go long because she's on a roll. The consistency is the point, and the consistency comes from not treating writing as something requiring a special state of mind. It requires a chair and time, both of which she provides daily.

There's something worth naming here about what this kind of sustained output actually builds. A writer who has written 200 novels has solved a particular plot problem at least 200 times. They've written hundreds of first chapters, hundreds of midpoints where the story wants to collapse, hundreds of endings that have to feel earned. Each repetition doesn't just produce a book. It deepens the technical knowledge that makes the next book a little easier. Roberts isn't just disciplined. She's one of the most technically practiced writers alive, and the technique was built one book at a time, across a career maintained by showing up when it would have been easier not to.

J.D. Robb and the Logic of a Second Brand

When Roberts began writing the In Death series in the mid-1990s, the books were a genuine departure from her established catalog. Set in a near-future New York, they follow a homicide detective named Eve Dallas and sit much closer to crime fiction than romance. Her publisher at the time suggested a pen name to avoid confusing readers, and J.D. Robb was the result. The name has since become its own brand, with its own dedicated readership and its own bestseller list presence, distinct from but running in parallel with the Nora Roberts catalog.

The practical implication is that Roberts has been maintaining two active writing identities simultaneously for three decades. The Roberts books are primarily romantic fiction: trilogies, standalones, saga-length family stories with strong emotional arcs. The Robb books are procedural crime novels with recurring characters who age and change across the series. The craft demands of the two are genuinely different, and the fact that both brands have stayed commercially strong across such a long run says something about how deep the technical resources are that sustain them.

For anyone thinking about writing across genre boundaries, the Roberts/Robb split is instructive. She didn't dilute either brand by trying to blend them. The romance readers and the crime readers know what they're getting, and both groups keep showing up. What makes both work is the same underlying discipline: a writer at her desk, drafting the next chapter, whether the calendar says "Nora Roberts month" or "J.D. Robb month." The brands are different. The routine behind them is the same.

Sources

The primary sources for Roberts's writing habits and philosophy are her own public statements across decades of interviews and appearances. Lauren Collins's profile of Roberts, "Real Romance," published in The New Yorker on June 22, 2009, is one of the most detailed accounts of her working life and covers her routine, her background, and the blizzard origin story. Roberts's own website, noraroberts.com, includes statements about her process and career history. Her remarks about the "ass in the chair" philosophy and not waiting for the muse have appeared across a wide range of Writer's Digest features, reader Q&As, and convention talks spanning more than twenty years. The origin of J.D. Robb and the In Death series is documented in multiple interviews given around the time the first book, Naked in Death, published in 1995.

What You Can Steal

Roberts's approach doesn't require any particular setup or equipment. The useful parts are all behavioral, which means they're available to any writer willing to try them:

  • Treat writing as a job, not a calling. Callings require inspiration. Jobs require showing up. Roberts goes to her office the way a surgeon goes to a hospital. The work is there regardless of how she feels about it that morning.
  • Give each draft a single job to do. The first draft discovers the story. The second draft builds the depth. The third draft cleans the prose. Trying to do all three simultaneously is the main reason first drafts die half-finished.
  • Don't edit as you draft. The blank page you're protecting yourself from by polishing yesterday's paragraphs is still blank. Finish the draft. Fix it after. Roberts's output depends on this sequencing.
  • Let resistance be irrelevant. Roberts hasn't said that writing is always easy. She's said she writes anyway. The distinction matters. You don't have to want to sit down. You have to sit down.
  • Build the second brand when the work demands it. J.D. Robb exists because the In Death books needed their own identity, not because Roberts was bored with romance. If a project genuinely belongs in a different lane, give it one. Clarity of brand serves readers.
