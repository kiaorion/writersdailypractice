Philip K. Dick's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 1, 2026 | 9 min read

Philip K. Dick wrote 60 pages a day at his peak. First drafts, unrevised, hammered out on a typewriter in whatever apartment he happened to be renting at the time. He produced 44 novels and 121 short stories across his career, spent most of that career in poverty, had five marriages, and died in 1982 at 53 before he ever saw Ridley Scott's adaptation of his work become a cultural monument. The story of his writing life is not the story of a man with a beautiful morning routine and a well-organized desk. It's the story of someone who wrote because stopping meant he didn't eat.

That context matters when you try to understand what his output actually required. The 60 pages per day figure shows up in accounts of his 1950s pulp years, when he was selling short stories to magazines for a cent or two per word and had to produce volume to generate income. Those years formed his habits in ways that never fully left him. Even when he was working on something more ambitious, like The Man in the High Castle in 1962 or Ubik in 1969, the underlying drive to fill pages quickly was part of how he worked. The commercial pressure had become a method.

Profile

The Routine at a Glance

Wake Time
No fixed schedule. Dick wrote in bursts rather than at a set daily hour, driven by deadlines and financial need. He was known to write through the night when a deadline pressed.
Writing Location
A series of rented apartments and houses in California, including Point Reyes Station and Santa Ana. He wrote at a desk or table wherever he was living, rarely in a dedicated study.
Daily Output
Up to 60 pages per day at his most productive, in the 1950s pulp period. The pace dropped in later years as the work became more philosophically complex, but the underlying habit of high-volume drafting stayed with him.
Tools
Typewriter throughout most of his career. He did not write by hand and moved directly from thought to typed page, which contributed to the raw, unpolished energy of his first drafts.
Famous Ritual
Writing in sustained bursts fueled by amphetamines, which he used extensively and documented in his own letters and the semi-autobiographical VALIS. The drug use was inseparable from the output during his most prolific years.
Books Written This Way
The Man in the High Castle (1962), Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep? (1968), Ubik (1969), A Scanner Darkly (1977), VALIS (1981).

The Pulp Years and Why They Mattered

Dick sold his first short story in 1951 and spent the following decade writing for pulp magazines including Fantasy and Science Fiction, Galaxy Science Fiction, and If. The economics were brutal. A cent per word was a reasonable pulp rate. To make rent, you needed volume. Dick produced it: 28 short stories in 1952 alone, another 26 in 1953. He was writing faster than most writers draft emails today.

What's easy to miss about those years is that they weren't a compromise he made before getting to the real work. They were the training. The habit of sitting down and producing pages, of not waiting for inspiration or the right mood, of treating the blank page as a problem that gets solved by typing through it, all of it formed during those pulp years and carried forward into every book he wrote afterward. The speed was a feature, not a workaround.

There's a certain kind of writing advice that tells you to slow down, to let the sentences develop fully before you commit them. Dick's entire career argues the opposite case. His best novels have a quality of thought in motion, of ideas generating faster than they can be organized, that you don't get from slow, careful composition. The breathlessness in his prose, the way reality keeps destabilizing itself mid-scene, came partly from a writer who was producing pages quickly enough that his skepticism about consensus reality had no time to cool down before it hit the page. The pulp pace didn't produce pulp ideas. It produced a specific kind of urgency that became his signature.

Amphetamines and the Cost of the Output

Dick's drug use is documented in enough primary sources that it can't be treated as gossip. He wrote about it in his letters, which were published after his death. Lawrence Sutin's biography Divine Invasions: A Life of Philip K. Dick covers it in detail. And A Scanner Darkly, published in 1977, includes an author's note listing friends he lost to drug abuse, with Dick acknowledging his own place in that community. The amphetamines that fueled his most productive years were also part of what damaged him.

The relevant question for anyone studying his routine isn't whether the drugs were a good idea. They clearly weren't, and Dick knew it. The question is what his use of them tells you about the conditions his output required. He needed to sustain a pace that the human body isn't naturally built for. Eight hours a day of typing, day after day, on subjects that required holding complex philosophical structures in working memory while also generating narrative, is hard work. The amphetamines were how he solved the energy problem when the work demanded more than he had.

The lesson here is about the gap between what a writing life demands and what a human being can supply without help. Dick's help was pharmaceutical and destructive. But the underlying insight, that high-output writing requires managing your energy as deliberately as you manage your time, transfers to any writer trying to maintain a serious practice. The question of what you're running on, whether that's sleep, exercise, structure, or something else, is worth answering honestly before you sit down and expect the pages to come.

The 1974 Vision and What It Did to His Work

In February 1974, Dick had an experience he spent the last eight years of his life trying to explain. A woman delivering medication to his house wore a fish-symbol necklace. When the light caught it, he said he received, in an instant, a burst of pink light and with it a download of information he couldn't account for rationally. He called it VALIS, short for Vast Active Living Intelligence System. He believed, at various points, that he had been contacted by an ancient satellite from a benevolent extraterrestrial civilization, that a cosmic force was trying to communicate through the physical world, or that his own unconscious had found a way to externalize itself through hallucination. He was never settled on the explanation.

What matters for understanding his writing life is what he did with the experience afterward. He started keeping the Exegesis, a private journal of notes and reflections on what had happened, which grew to roughly eight thousand pages by his death in 1982. He was writing every day, compulsively, not fiction but philosophy, trying to build a framework that could hold the experience without collapsing. Selections from the Exegesis were published posthumously in 2011, edited by Pamela Jackson and Jonathan Lethem. The fragments that survived suggest a mind that couldn't stop working the problem, even when the problem resisted every framework he tried.

The VALIS trilogy, including VALIS (1981), The Divine Invasion (1981), and The Transmigration of Timothy Archer (1982), came directly out of this period. These are Dick's strangest and most personal novels, written by someone who had stopped pretending to separate the philosophical questions in his fiction from the philosophical questions in his own life. The routine that produced them was unlike his pulp years: slower, more driven by obsession than by commercial deadline, organized around the daily practice of the Exegesis rather than around a story that needed to reach a word count.

Poverty, Commercial Work, and Writing Anyway

Dick lived in poverty for most of his career. This is not a metaphor. In the 1970s, he applied for food stamps. His house in Point Reyes Station, where he lived in the early 1970s, was broken into and his files were ransacked in an incident that haunted him for years. He had tax problems. He had five marriages and the financial obligations that came with them. Despite producing one of the most influential bodies of work in twentieth-century American fiction, he spent decades unable to pay his bills reliably.

The commercial calculation was always in the room with him when he wrote. Selling a story to a pulp magazine for a hundred dollars was enough to cover groceries for a week. A novel advance, even a modest one, could buy a few months of rent. The work existed inside this pressure the way Morrison's work existed inside her life as a mother and editor, as something that had to happen alongside everything else and couldn't wait for circumstances to improve. Dick never had the time to wait for better circumstances. The circumstances never got much better.

What his career demonstrates, at scale and across decades, is that serious work and commercial desperation are not mutually exclusive. The books he wrote to make rent include Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep? He wrote the novel that became Blade Runner because he needed money. The Man in the High Castle, which won the Hugo Award in 1963, was written partly for the same reason. Financial pressure didn't corrupt his imagination. In some ways it seems to have focused it, forcing him to generate and produce rather than to contemplate and revise.

Philip K. Dick's Writing Process

Philip K. Dick's writing process changed across the decades of his career, but certain elements held constant from the pulp years to the VALIS trilogy. He typed directly, without longhand drafts as an intermediate step. He worked in bursts rather than at fixed daily hours. He revised less than most serious novelists, particularly in his early career, where the economics of pulp writing meant revision was a luxury he couldn't afford. The first draft was often close to what got published.

His most productive period ran roughly from 1951 to 1964, when he was selling everything he wrote and writing constantly to keep up with what the market would absorb. During those years the routine was essentially industrial: sit down, produce pages, submit, cash the check, repeat. The quality varied enormously across the body of work from this period. Some of it is genuinely bad, written too fast to cohere. The best of it, the novels and stories that got taught in universities decades later, is distinguished by ideas so strange and generative that the rough execution stops mattering.

After 1974, the Exegesis became the daily writing practice. He worked on it in the mornings, sometimes for hours, before turning to fiction. The fiction in that period, including A Scanner Darkly and the VALIS novels, carries the weight of someone who had been processing the same set of questions every day for years before he started the chapter. The Exegesis was not a journal in the ordinary sense; it was a working space for ideas, and the novels were where those ideas finally found a narrative container.

Sources

The primary biography is Lawrence Sutin's Divine Invasions: A Life of Philip K. Dick, published in 1989 and reissued since, which covers the pulp years, the drug use, the 1974 experience, and the Exegesis in detail drawn from interviews and Dick's own letters. The Exegesis of Philip K. Dick, edited by Pamela Jackson and Jonathan Lethem (Houghton Mifflin Harcourt, 2011), is the primary document of his late-period writing practice. Dick's own 1978 essay "How to Build a Universe That Doesn't Fall Apart Two Days Later," collected in The Shifting Realities of Philip K. Dick (Vintage, 1995), edited by Lawrence Sutin, contains his most direct statements about his relationship to reality and fiction. Emmanuel Carrere's I Am Alive and You Are Dead: A Journey into the Mind of Philip K. Dick (Picador, 2004) offers a close biographical reading of the work. See also: Philip K. Dick on Wikipedia.

What You Can Steal

Dick's routine is useful in ways that don't require you to replicate the poverty or the amphetamines. Here's what actually transfers:

  • Use the deadline as a drafting tool. Dick's highest daily page counts happened when the pressure was real and immediate. If you're stuck, give yourself an artificial deadline with actual consequences. Tell someone you'll send them the chapter on Friday. The constraint often does what the motivation couldn't.
  • Write toward the idea, not around it. Dick's fiction works because the ideas are generative, strange, and specific enough to drive the narrative. If your draft feels inert, ask whether the central idea is interesting enough to carry its own weight. Dick never had a boring premise. The ideas were so strange they demanded resolution.
  • Keep a working journal separate from the manuscript. The Exegesis was where Dick processed questions the novels couldn't hold directly. A daily writing practice that includes something other than the project, a notebook, a journal, a place to think out loud, often feeds the manuscript in ways you won't be able to trace or plan.
  • Don't wait for financial stability before taking the work seriously. Dick's best novels were written under financial pressure that would have stopped most writers. The career demonstrates that you don't need good circumstances to produce good work. You need to produce pages regardless of circumstances, because the circumstances may never improve.
  • Let the obsession find its form. The VALIS trilogy came from a genuine philosophical crisis Dick couldn't resolve. The books work because the obsession was real. If there's something you can't stop thinking about, that's the thing worth writing, even if the form isn't obvious yet.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many pages a day did Philip K. Dick write?

At his most productive, Dick wrote 60 or more first-draft pages per day. These were unrevised pulp drafts written to sell immediately to science fiction magazines, and the pace was driven by financial need as much as creative energy. Across his career he produced 44 novels and 121 short stories.

Did Philip K. Dick use amphetamines while writing?

Yes. Dick's use of amphetamines is documented in his biographies, his own letters, and his semi-autobiographical novel VALIS. In the author's note to A Scanner Darkly (1977), Dick acknowledged belonging to the drug community he was writing about. He used amphetamines to sustain the pace required to write and sell enough pulp fiction to survive, and the drug use was part of a chaotic personal life that included five marriages and recurring financial desperation.

What was Philip K. Dick's writing schedule?

Dick didn't maintain a regular writing schedule the way some writers do. He worked in bursts, sometimes producing enormous quantities of pages in short periods, then going quiet. The rhythm was driven by commercial deadlines and financial pressure rather than a fixed daily practice. In the years after his 1974 experience, he added a daily journaling practice, the Exegesis, which he worked on separately from his fiction.

What is Philip K. Dick's most famous quote?

"Reality is that which, when you stop believing in it, doesn't go away." This line appears in his 1978 essay "How to Build a Universe That Doesn't Fall Apart Two Days Later," collected in The Shifting Realities of Philip K. Dick (Vintage, 1995). It has since become one of the most widely cited definitions of reality in popular culture.
