Push-Ups and Pull-Ups for Writers: Vonnegut's Method

Kia Orion | May 17, 2026 | 7 min read

Kurt Vonnegut stopped in the middle of his writing session to do pull-ups. Not after finishing, not as a warm-up before he started, but in the middle, as a deliberate interruption. He'd been at the desk for an hour or two, get up, do a set, then sit back down. He swam at the YMCA every morning on top of this. The physical movement was built into the structure of the writing day, not bolted on as an afterthought.

Haruki Murakami runs 10 kilometers every single day while working on a novel, after the morning writing session, and has done so for decades. He's written an entire book about the relationship between running and writing. Joyce Carol Oates runs. Henry David Thoreau walked four hours every afternoon and considered it the minimum his mind required to function. Charles Dickens covered 12 to 20 miles on foot through London most days, composing scenes in his head as he walked. Ernest Hemingway stood at his desk for the entire session rather than sit. The pattern is too consistent to be coincidence.

Push-Ups and Pull-Ups for Writers: What Vonnegut Was Actually Doing

Vonnegut's pull-ups are the most specific and most transferable example in this archive. The mechanics of what he was doing are easy to describe: he'd reach a natural stopping point in the session, get up from the typewriter, do a set of pull-ups and push-ups, then return. He mailed long letters to his wife on walks to the post office. Every physical interruption was built into the day's structure before he sat down, not improvised when things got hard.

The benefit he was capturing isn't primarily physical fitness. Sustained focus on a difficult writing problem can create cognitive tunnel vision. You're so deep inside one way of seeing the material that you can't access alternative approaches. A set of pull-ups breaks that tunnel. The effort shifts blood and attention away from the default-mode thinking that keeps running the same mental loops, and you return to the page with a slightly different mind than you left it with. Writers describe this as the problem "unlocking" during the break. The break is doing real cognitive work, not just resting.

Dan Brown uses a similar approach: an hourglass on his desk, and when it runs out he stops writing and does push-ups and stretches before flipping it and returning. The physical break functions as a session boundary, a way of stepping out of the immersive state and re-entering it fresh. Both Vonnegut and Brown arrived at this structure independently, through trial and error, which suggests they found something real.

"In long-distance running, the only opponent you have to beat is yourself, the way you used to be."

Running as a Writing Practice: The Murakami Method

Murakami's book What I Talk About When I Talk About Running (2008) makes the argument explicitly: the discipline required to run 10 kilometers every day for months on end and the discipline required to write a novel over the same period are the same mental muscle. Both demand showing up when you don't feel like it, maintaining form when you're tired, and resisting the pull to stop before you're done. Training one trains the other.

His schedule when drafting: wake at 4am, write for five to six hours, run 10K or swim 1,500 meters, read, listen to music, sleep by 9pm. He holds this for months without variation. The run comes after the writing, not before, which matters. The writing gets the sharpest hours. The run processes what the writing uncovered and sets up the next morning's session.

Joyce Carol Oates has described running as the activity where her best ideas arrive. She's run throughout her career alongside one of the most prolific writing schedules in American literary history. For her, as for Murakami, the run is thinking time in a different key, the body doing one thing while the unconscious works on another.

Walking: The Oldest Writer's Exercise

Before running became common, writers walked. Thoreau's four-hour daily walks through Concord weren't leisure. He wrote about them in his journals as essential to his mental functioning, the minimum the mind required before it would produce anything worth keeping. Dickens's London walks are documented in his letters and in John Forster's biography: 12 to 20 miles was a normal day, and he composed scenes and dialogue in his head as he went. He was essentially drafting on foot.

The neuroscience now explains what these writers found by feel. Low-intensity aerobic movement, the kind you sustain for 20 to 45 minutes, increases activity in the prefrontal cortex and releases brain-derived neurotrophic factor, a protein that supports new neural connections. The associative, non-linear thinking that produces unexpected creative connections is more accessible during and shortly after moderate aerobic effort than it is from a sitting position. The walk is not a break from the writing. For writers who've built it into the day deliberately, it's part of the writing process.

Standing at the Desk: Hemingway's Physical Commitment

Hemingway stood at a chest-high bookshelf in his bedroom at Finca Vigía for the entire writing session. A back injury originally made it necessary; he kept doing it after the injury healed because the writing came out differently on his feet. Short sentences. Physical verbs. A kinetic impatience in the paragraphs that you can feel on the page. Whether the standing caused this or whether it was already in his voice is impossible to say, but he believed the connection was real and he maintained the posture across his career.

Standing keeps the body engaged in a way that sitting doesn't. It's not exercise in the conventional sense, but it's physical engagement with the act of writing rather than physical passivity. Writers who stand while working often describe their sessions as feeling more active, more like they're doing something rather than waiting for something to happen. The posture can shift the relationship to the work.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does exercise actually help with writing?

Yes, and the evidence comes from both the documented habits of productive writers and from neuroscience. Low-intensity aerobic exercise increases blood flow to the prefrontal cortex and releases brain-derived neurotrophic factor. The writers in this archive who exercised regularly (Murakami, Oates, Vonnegut, Thoreau, Dickens) were among the most consistently productive of their generations.

Should I exercise before or after writing?

Murakami writes first and runs after, which protects the sharpest morning hours for the work. Vonnegut swam before writing and did calisthenics during. Oates runs as part of her thinking process and doesn't separate it rigidly from the writing day. The most honest answer is that either approach works, and the one you'll maintain consistently is the right one for you.

How many pull-ups did Vonnegut do?

He doesn't specify a number in his documented accounts of the routine. The principle is the interruption and the physical effort, not a specific rep count. A set that takes you out of the session and requires genuine effort is the target, not a particular number.

What You Can Steal

  • Build the physical movement into the session structure, not after it. Vonnegut's pull-ups worked because they were scheduled before he sat down. Deciding to take a break "when things get hard" means the break never comes or comes at the wrong time.
  • Use exercise to break tunnel vision, not just to stay healthy. The cognitive benefit of a mid-session interruption is different from the cardiovascular benefit of a morning run. Both are real. Plan for both separately.
  • Low-intensity aerobic movement is where ideas arrive. Walking, running at a conversational pace, swimming. High-intensity training is excellent for fitness but tends to clear the mind rather than activate it creatively. Thoreau and Murakami were walkers and distance runners, not sprinters.
  • Try standing for one week before dismissing it. Hemingway kept it because the writing changed, not because he loved the posture. The only way to know if it affects your prose is to try it long enough for the novelty to wear off.
  • Treat the physical routine as parallel training. Murakami's framing is the most useful: the discipline required to run every day and the discipline required to write every day are the same discipline. Building one builds the other.
