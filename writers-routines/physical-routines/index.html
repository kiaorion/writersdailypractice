Writers' Routines

Famous Writers Who Built Physical Movement Into Their Routines

Kia Orion | May 10, 2026 | 8 min read

Kurt Vonnegut stopped mid-page to do pull-ups. Haruki Murakami ran ten kilometers before noon every day he was working on a novel, for months at a stretch. Ernest Hemingway stood at his desk for the entire session, by preference and by choice. Joyce Carol Oates has described running as the activity where her best ideas arrive, and she has run and written every day for decades. This pattern shows up too consistently across too many writers to be coincidence.

The body and the writing mind are not separate systems. Physical movement changes how the brain works, how attention moves, how difficult material becomes accessible. The writers who figured this out did so through trial and error, not neuroscience, but the neuroscience now mostly confirms what they found by feel. A writing day that includes deliberate movement is structurally different from one that doesn't, and the difference tends to show up on the page.

The Writers and What They Did

Kurt Vonnegut
Pull-ups and push-ups between sessions + daily YMCA swim

Vonnegut's physical routine was built directly into the writing day rather than bracketed at the beginning or end of it. He swam at the YMCA every morning, then wrote in two-hour blocks with sets of pull-ups and push-ups built into each one. He interrupted the writing on purpose. The physical break came in the middle of the session, not after it finished.

He described mailing letters home from the post office as another deliberate interruption. The walking, the errand, the calisthenics were all reasons to leave the desk and return to it fresh. Vonnegut treated the human need to move not as a distraction from writing but as a mechanism inside it.

Haruki Murakami
10K run or 1,500-meter swim, every day while drafting

Murakami is the clearest case for physical training as a direct parallel to writing training. He's written about it explicitly in his memoir What I Talk About When I Talk About Running (2007): the mental discipline required to run long distances every day is the same discipline required to write a novel. Both demand that you show up when you don't feel like it, maintain form when you're tired, and resist the temptation to stop before you're done.

When he's working on a novel, Murakami wakes at 4am, writes for five to six hours, and then runs 10K or swims 1,500 meters. He holds this schedule for months, every day, without variation. The running is not recovery from writing. It's a second form of the same practice.

Ernest Hemingway
Standing at the desk for the entire writing session

Hemingway's physical relationship with writing was embedded in the setup itself. He wrote standing at a chest-high bookshelf, in a pair of loafers, for the full session. A back injury originally made standing necessary, but he kept doing it after the back healed because he noticed the writing came out differently on his feet than it did in a chair.

Standing keeps the body engaged in a way that sitting doesn't. The sentences Hemingway wrote on his feet tend to have a kinetic quality, a physical impatience, that you can feel in the rhythm. Short verbs, short sentences, action landing fast. Whether the standing caused this or whether it was already in his voice is hard to say, but the physical condition and the prose quality seem connected.

Why Physical Movement and Writing Go Together

The brain during physical movement generates a different kind of thinking than the brain at rest. Low-intensity aerobic activity, the kind Murakami and Oates do, increases activity in the prefrontal cortex and releases brain-derived neurotrophic factor, which supports new neural connections. The kind of associative, non-linear thinking that produces unexpected creative connections is easier to reach during or shortly after moderate physical effort than it is from a sitting position.

Vonnegut's approach captures something slightly different. His pull-ups weren't aerobic; they were interruptions. The benefit there is less neurochemical and more attentional. Sustained focus on a difficult writing problem can create a kind of cognitive tunnel vision, where you're so deep inside one way of looking at the material that you can't see alternatives. A set of pull-ups breaks the tunnel. You return to the page with a slightly different mind than you left it with, and that small reset can unlock what the previous twenty minutes of staring couldn't.

Henry David Thoreau walked four hours every day, which he considered the minimum required for his mind to function properly. "I think that I cannot preserve my health and spirits," he wrote in his essay "Walking" (1862), "unless I spend four hours a day at least sauntering through the woods and over the hills and fields." Charles Dickens covered twelve to twenty miles on foot in an average London day. He composed scenes while walking, often at night, and the city walking is documented in his letters and in John Forster's biography as essential to how he worked out narrative problems. Neither of them framed it as exercise. They framed it as thinking.

What This Looks Like in Practice

The specifics vary widely. Murakami's method requires elite athletic commitment that most people can't replicate while also writing. Vonnegut's method is more accessible: interrupt the session, do ten pull-ups, come back. Hemingway's method requires only that you stand up.

The common thread across all of them is that physical movement was scheduled, not spontaneous. These writers didn't take a walk when they felt like it or do push-ups when the writing got frustrating. They built the physical component into the structure of the writing day before they sat down. Vonnegut knew he was going to swim in the morning and do calisthenics between sessions before he wrote a single word. Murakami had his shoes by the door. The deliberate structure is what separates this from restless pacing or distraction-driven movement.

For writers trying to build physical movement into their own practice, the most transferable approach is probably Vonnegut's: pick a simple physical action, decide in advance when in the session you'll do it, and treat it as part of the session rather than a break from it. Not a reward for reaching a word count, not a punishment for poor progress. Just a built-in part of the method.

What You Can Steal

  • Schedule the physical movement in advance, not reactively. Vonnegut knew he was going to the YMCA before he opened his notebook. The structure was decided before the session started.
  • Try interrupting the session, not capping it. A set of pull-ups mid-page breaks tunnel vision in a way that pushing through to the end and then stretching doesn't. The return to the page with a reset attention is the mechanism.
  • Low-intensity aerobic activity before writing changes the neurochemistry of what follows. A 20-minute walk before the session is more useful than a 20-minute walk after it, if your goal is the quality of the morning's pages.
  • Treat the physical routine as parallel training, not recovery. Murakami's framing is the most useful here: the discipline required to run every day and the discipline required to write every day are the same discipline. They reinforce each other.
  • Standing while writing is worth trying once, seriously. Not as a productivity hack or an ergonomic upgrade. As a test of whether your prose quality changes when your body is engaged rather than slumped. For some writers it makes a difference. The only way to know is to try it for a week.
