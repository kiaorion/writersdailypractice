Raymond Chandler's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | July 19, 2026 | 10 min read

Raymond Chandler didn't publish his first novel until he was fifty-one. Before that, he was an oil company executive in Los Angeles, a vice president at the Dabney Oil Syndicate, until he was fired in 1932 at forty-four for drinking, absenteeism, and affairs with women on the payroll. With the Depression closing off most other options, he taught himself to write pulp fiction from scratch, starting by taking apart a published Erle Stanley Gardner novelette to see how the mechanism worked. His first story, "Blackmailers Don't Shoot," took him five months to write eighteen thousand words. Gardner, by comparison, could turn out a story that length in three or four days and produced roughly a thousand of them over his career.

The myth around hardboiled fiction is that it got written fast and rough, cranked out by writers who didn't have time for polish. Chandler is the counterexample the genre needs. He was slow by temperament and slower by method, revising sentence by sentence until the prose had the compression it's still famous for. He built the modern detective novel's voice out of patience rather than speed, and the discipline he wrote about in his letters, a rule so specific it still gets quoted eighty years later, explains how a man who started this late produced seven novels that are still in print.

Profile

The Routine at a Glance

Wake Time
Varied across his career. In his later years in La Jolla, Chandler described in a letter getting up around 4 a.m. to write before the day started.
Writing Location
Los Angeles for most of his working life. In 1946 he and his wife Cissy moved to La Jolla, California, where he wrote his final two Philip Marlowe novels.
Daily Output
Slow and deliberate by his own account. His first published story took five months to write. He measured a working day in hours at the desk, not words produced.
Tools
A typewriter throughout his career, an Underwood in his earlier years and, from around 1955, an Olivetti Studio 44 he was openly fond of. He drafted on yellow half sheets of paper, sized to limit how much he had to retype when revising a page.
Famous Ritual
The four-hour rule: sit at the desk for at least four hours a day. You don't have to write during that time, but you're not allowed to do anything else either.
Books Written This Way
The Big Sleep (1939), Farewell, My Lovely (1940), The High Window (1942), The Lady in the Lake (1943), The Little Sister (1949), The Long Goodbye (1953), Playback (1958).

Learning the Trade by Taking It Apart

Chandler had no fiction-writing background when he lost his oil job. What he had was an English education from Dulwich College, some early work as a journalist and poet in London, and a stubborn streak. Rather than guess at how pulp fiction worked, he studied it directly: he took a published Erle Stanley Gardner novelette and effectively reverse-engineered it, working out how the plot mechanics functioned before attempting his own. It's an almost clinical way to learn a craft, closer to an engineer studying a competitor's machine than a novelist waiting for inspiration, and it fits a man who'd spent the previous decade running an oil company.

The result of that method was painfully slow at first. "Blackmailers Don't Shoot" ran eighteen thousand words and took five months, sold to Black Mask for a hundred and eighty dollars in December 1933. Gardner, working the same market, wrote at a pace Chandler never came close to matching and never tried to. Chandler revised again and again before he considered a sentence finished, and that habit, expensive in time, is the reason his prose reads as tightly built decades later while most of his pulp contemporaries have been forgotten. Speed was never the point. The sentence was.

Four Hours, No Exceptions

In a 1949 letter to a correspondent named Alex Barris, later collected in Selected Letters of Raymond Chandler, Chandler laid out the rule he's still best known for. There should be a space of time, he wrote, four hours a day at the least, when a professional writer doesn't do anything else but write. He doesn't have to write during that time. If nothing is coming, he shouldn't force it. He can look out the window, stand on his head, do nothing at all. What he can't do is anything else that counts as productive: no reading, no letters, no glancing at magazines, no paying bills. Chandler distilled it to two rules: you don't have to write, but you can't do anything else.

The genius of the rule is what it removes. It doesn't demand output, which takes the pressure off a day when the work genuinely isn't there. But it closes every escape route except the one that matters. Boredom, in Chandler's design, becomes the mechanism that eventually pushes a writer back to the page. Willpower and inspiration never enter into it. You can't outlast four empty hours by doing something else productive, because the rule doesn't allow it. Sooner or later, staring at the wall stops being more interesting than the manuscript.

Yellow Paper and the Cost of a Sentence

Chandler composed on a typewriter his entire career, an Underwood in his earlier years and, from around 1955, an Olivetti Studio 44 he wrote about with real affection, calling it superior to anything made in America and built like an Italian racing car. He hunted and pecked with two fingers rather than touch-typing, which makes his prolific revision habits even more striking: every retyped page cost him real time and effort, not the quick correction a fast typist takes for granted.

That physical cost may be exactly why he drafted on yellow half sheets, paper cut down to roughly five and a half by eight and a half inches, small enough that retyping a single page after a revision didn't mean retyping an entire manuscript sheet. It's a workaround that reveals how seriously he took the sentence-level rewriting: he built his own paper size around the assumption that he'd be redoing pages constantly, and made the physical cost of that habit as small as he could. The result shows up directly in the prose. A line like "He looked about as inconspicuous as a tarantula on a slice of angel food" reads like it arrived whole, but it came out of a process engineered, quite literally, to make revision cheap enough to do constantly.

The 4 a.m. Olivetti sessions belong to a specific, harder stretch of his life. Chandler's wife Cissy, eighteen years his senior and the person he'd been married to since 1924, was already ill by the time he finished The Long Goodbye in the early 1950s, and she died in December 1954. The loss sent him into a period of heavier drinking, disrupted sleep, and at least one suicide attempt, and the letter describing his pre-dawn hammering at the Olivetti dates from May 1955, squarely inside that grief. The routine held anyway, in a diminished form. He went on to write Playback, published in 1958 during his own declining health, a novel that reads like a veiled reckoning with everything he'd lost.

What "The Simple Art of Murder" Actually Argues

In his 1944 essay "The Simple Art of Murder," published in The Atlantic Monthly, Chandler laid out his case for detective fiction as a serious literary form, not a lesser one. His famous description of the genre's ideal protagonist, a man who must go down mean streets and is himself neither tarnished nor afraid, gets quoted constantly, usually stripped of the argument surrounding it. The essay is really about honesty on the sentence level: "Everything written with vitality expresses that vitality: there are no dull subjects, only dull minds." That line is as much a description of his own method as a piece of criticism. Chandler thought most crime writers simply weren't bothering to write one sentence at a time with any real care, not that the genre itself was beneath serious craft.

That belief connects directly back to the four-hour rule and the yellow half sheets. All three are versions of the same argument: genre fiction fails when the writer treats it as disposable, and succeeds when it gets the same sentence-by-sentence attention a literary novel would demand. Chandler spent five months on an eighteen-thousand-word story most of his contemporaries would have finished in a week, and the difference between his surviving reputation and theirs is largely that decision, repeated for twenty-five years.

What You Can Steal

Chandler proved a late start doesn't disqualify a career. Here's what actually transfers:

  • Take a rule seriously enough to enforce it on yourself. Four hours at the desk, no exceptions, no other productive activity allowed. The specificity is what makes it work. A vague intention to "write more" has no teeth. A rule with a number does.
  • Learn craft by taking someone else's work apart. Chandler taught himself plot mechanics by studying a published story sentence by sentence. If you don't know how something works, don't wait for it to become intuitive. Dissect a working example.
  • Let boredom do the work willpower can't. Chandler's rule didn't demand output, it just removed every alternative to writing. Structure your own sessions so the path of least resistance leads back to the page.
  • Make revision physically cheap. Chandler cut his paper down so retyping cost less. Whatever friction stands between you and rewriting a bad paragraph, look for a way to shrink it.
  • Treat genre writing as real writing. Chandler's argument in "The Simple Art of Murder" still holds: there are no dull subjects, only writers who won't do the sentence-level work. Apply that standard to your own genre, whatever it is.
