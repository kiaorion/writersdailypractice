Rebecca Yarros's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 14, 2026 | 9 min read

When Fourth Wing landed on shelves in May 2023 and immediately broke records at Red Tower Books, the instinct was to file it under luck or timing. A dragon-rider romantasy arriving at exactly the right moment, with exactly the right cover, into exactly the right cultural window. The timing part is real. But what the "right moment" framing misses is the writer behind it: a mother of six, a military spouse who's moved her family across the country more than once, and a woman managing Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, a connective-tissue disorder that affects joints and causes chronic pain. Yarros didn't write a breakout novel because things aligned. She wrote it because she's built a working practice capable of producing books under conditions most writers would use as reasons not to write at all.

Her routine doesn't fit the romantic image. There's no mountain cabin, no silent study, no uninterrupted run of hours. What she has is a deadline, a family, a body that doesn't always cooperate, and a deeply practical approach to getting words on the page anyway. That's the thing worth studying.

The Routine at a Glance

Wake Time
Yarros hasn't documented a fixed wake time publicly. Her schedule adapts around six children, her husband's military service, and her health on any given day.
Writing Location
Home, wherever that happens to be. Military families move frequently, and Yarros has written across multiple states. The work has to travel with her.
Daily Output
She's spoken publicly about drafting quickly when under deadline. A precise daily word count isn't on record, but the pace of the Empyrean series suggests high-output sessions rather than slow, incremental accumulation.
Tools
Laptop. She writes full-length commercial fiction with the efficiency of a writer who knows time is a limited resource.
Famous Ritual
Writing around her family rather than in spite of it. Yarros has discussed the reality of her chronic illness affecting her capacity on any given day, which means she works when she can and pushes hard when she's able.
Books Written This Way
Fourth Wing, Iron Flame, Onyx Storm (the Empyrean series), plus more than a dozen prior novels in romance and new adult fiction.

Fast Drafting and What It Actually Requires

Yarros drafted Fourth Wing quickly by the standards of commercial fiction. The book runs to over 500 pages in print, which puts the word count somewhere north of 160,000 words. Getting that down in a matter of months, which accounts from her publisher and her own social media presence suggest she did, requires a specific kind of focus that doesn't come from waiting for inspiration. It comes from treating drafting as a technical problem with a solution: sit down, stay in the story, and write forward.

Fast drafting, at the level Yarros operates, is less about raw speed and more about decision-making. A writer who stops every few pages to second-guess the plot, backtrack to fix sentences, or research whether the military hierarchy she's invented makes internal sense is not going to finish a book in months. Yarros writes romantasy, a genre that requires both the emotional precision of romance (the push-pull, the earned tension, the specific beats readers come expecting) and the world-building load of fantasy. Carrying both simultaneously, at pace, is a skill that took her years of earlier novels to develop before she ever got to Xaden Riorson and Violet Sorrengail.

The thing about fast drafting that doesn't get said often enough is that it's only possible if you already know your characters well. Yarros had been writing romance for years before the Empyrean series, and that backlog of craft isn't incidental to her output speed. She knows how to set up emotional stakes quickly, how to calibrate tension without losing the reader, and how to write a scene that does three things at once because she's written hundreds of scenes that needed to do exactly that. The speed is a product of the depth, and the depth came from the years of work before the breakthrough.

Writing with a Chronic Illness

Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS) is a connective-tissue disorder that affects collagen throughout the body. In practical terms, it means joints that dislocate more easily than they should, chronic pain at varying levels of severity, and a body that doesn't always track with what the brain wants to do. Yarros has discussed her diagnosis publicly, and it's part of the context for her working life in a way that deserves more than a passing mention.

A writing routine built around chronic illness can't be rigid. You can't commit to three hours at the desk every morning when some mornings your hands won't cooperate or the pain makes it hard to focus on anything but managing the pain. What you can do is work in the windows when you're functioning well, build in the rest you actually need rather than the rest that makes you feel guilty, and stay close enough to your story that getting back in on a hard day doesn't cost you an hour of re-reading to remember where you were. Yarros's pace of output across the Empyrean series suggests she's found a version of this that works for her body, even if the specific shape of it changes from week to week.

There's a lesson in this for writers without chronic illness too. The assumption that productivity requires a fixed daily practice, the same hours, the same quota, the same desk, is a framework that works for some writers and fails badly for others. Yarros's example is useful precisely because she hasn't had the option of building a rigid routine and then defending it. She's had to stay flexible, which means she's had to figure out what the minimum viable version of her practice looks like on a bad day, and then actually do that version on bad days rather than nothing.

She's spoken in interviews about wanting to represent people with chronic illness in fiction, too. Violet Sorrengail, the protagonist of the Empyrean series, has a connective-tissue disorder of her own. That's not a coincidence. Yarros built a heroine whose body creates real, structural obstacles rather than cosmetic ones, and writing that character convincingly required drawing on lived experience with what that actually looks and feels like. The writing practice and the fiction it produces are, in this case, genuinely related.

She writes the books she wants to read, with characters drawn from the people she actually knows.

The Military Spouse Schedule

Military families move. That's a known feature of the life, and Yarros has kept her publishing career running through those moves while raising six children, so that by the time Fourth Wing came out, she already had more than a dozen novels behind her. Each move means a new school system, a new house to get functional, a new city to learn, all while managing the emotional weight that comes with a partner who deploys or is otherwise unavailable in ways civilian families don't deal with on the same schedule. Writing through that kind of disruption is a choice you have to make repeatedly, across different cities and different years.

The disruption also teaches something about what's actually essential to a writing practice versus what's just habit dressed up as necessity. When you move every few years, you can't rely on a specific chair, a specific coffee shop, a specific corner of a specific room. The ritual has to be portable, which means it has to live in your head and in your relationship to the story rather than in the furniture. Writers who've built their whole practice around a single beloved desk sometimes can't work without it. Yarros has never had that option, and her output is the argument that the option was never load-bearing to begin with.

Six children is, plainly, a lot of people to coordinate around. Yarros has written about her family as a core part of her life rather than an interruption to it, and the way she talks about the balance suggests she's operating from the same logic as Toni Morrison's kitchen table. The writing goes into the available time, not into a life cleared out to make room for it. That's a harder practice to build and a more sustainable one once you have it, because life doesn't suddenly get less crowded. If your practice can't survive the crowding, it can't survive.

Plotting a Series at Scale

The Empyrean series is plotted at a scale that requires more than a good first book. Fourth Wing introduced a world with a full military hierarchy, a dragon-rider college (the War College at Basgiath), a political structure involving a war that isn't what it appears to be, and a romance with a second-lead who's concealing something significant. Iron Flame and Onyx Storm expanded all of it. Threading those storylines across multiple books without losing track of what was promised in book one, or inadvertently contradicting what was established in book two, is a plotting problem that doesn't solve itself.

Yarros has talked about doing significant planning work before she drafts. Her approach to the Empyrean series appears to be what writers who study structure would recognize as a plotting-forward method: she works out the major beats before the draft begins rather than discovering the story as she goes. This is practical for a series that was greenlit for multiple books from early on, because you can't discover your way through an interconnected multi-book arc without creating continuity problems that are expensive to fix later.

The romantasy genre, which you can read more about in our guide to finding your genre fit, sits at an intersection where plotting matters enormously. Romance readers track the emotional arc with close attention and notice when a promised tension is dropped or resolved too quickly. Fantasy readers track the world-logic and will catch an inconsistency in how dragon-bonding works between book one and book three. Writing for readers who are paying attention to two complex systems simultaneously requires more structural planning up front than either genre alone would. Yarros's speed on the page is possible in part because she did the slow, painstaking work of mapping the series before she started writing it fast.

Rebecca Yarros's Writing Practice

Yarros came up through the new adult and romance market before the Empyrean series, publishing novels with smaller commercial footprints that gave her the chance to develop craft under conditions where a structural problem in book three wasn't going to affect two million preorders. That earlier body of work is worth knowing about because it explains the confidence of execution in Fourth Wing. By the time she wrote Fourth Wing, she had more than a decade of craft behind her, and she brought that accumulated skill to a bigger canvas than she'd used before.

In interviews ahead of the Empyrean series launch, Yarros described the series as one she'd wanted to write for a long time. The dragon-rider premise and the romantasy blend weren't genre calculations made after A Court of Thorns and Roses proved the market existed. She had the idea, she developed it, and then she wrote it. The market timing was fortunate, but the book itself existed before the market opportunity was obvious, which is usually how good books work.

Her pace through the Empyrean series, with each entry arriving roughly a year or less after the previous one, puts her in the tradition of commercial fiction writers who understand that readers stay engaged when the story keeps moving. Waiting four years between books in a series is a reader-retention problem as much as a craft one. Yarros has kept the series moving by treating the work like the professional obligation it is, which means writing on days when it's hard, writing when the children are loud, writing when her body is arguing with her, and writing when the book she's supposed to be finishing is less interesting than the other idea she keeps thinking about.

What You Can Steal

Yarros's practice is useful precisely because it was built around constraints. Here's what actually transfers:

  • Build a practice that survives your bad days, not just your good ones. Yarros can't rely on ideal conditions because she's never had them. If your routine collapses the moment something goes wrong, you don't have a practice. You have a hobby that requires good weather.
  • Draft forward, revise later. The pace of the Empyrean series only works if Yarros isn't editing as she goes. Get the draft down first. The structural problems and the sentence-level problems are both easier to see when the whole shape of the book exists.
  • Put years of craft in before the fast books. The speed people admire in Yarros's output is the product of over a decade of earlier novels. There's no shortcut to that depth. The only path is the earlier work.
  • Plot the series before you draft it. Multi-book arcs require promises made early that have to be kept late. Working those out in outline before you draft means you can write fast without painting yourself into a corner.
  • Use your constraints as material. Yarros wrote a heroine with a connective-tissue disorder because she knew that experience from the inside. Whatever your life looks like, the writing that comes out of it carries that specificity. That's a feature, not something to write around.
