Sally Rooney's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 23, 2026 | 9 min read

Sally Rooney wrote the first draft of Conversations with Friends in roughly three months. She was in her early twenties, still finishing a master's degree at Trinity College Dublin, and she had not yet published anything. That speed is the first thing worth understanding about how she works, because it shapes everything else: the quality of the prose, the stripped-back style, the way her characters talk in long contested exchanges that feel less like literary dialogue and more like two people trying to win an argument.

She's been marketed as a voice of a generation, a label she clearly dislikes, and the conversation around her books has often said more about the anxieties of literary culture than about the work itself. What tends to get lost is that she's a highly technical writer with a specific and repeatable method. The speed matters. The debate background matters. The decision to remove quotation marks from dialogue is a structural choice, not an affectation. Her relationship with fame is not just biographical color but is directly relevant to how and why she writes the way she does. This page tries to get at the actual routine underneath all the noise.

Profile

The Routine at a Glance

Wake Time
Not publicly documented. Rooney guards the domestic details of her life carefully and has not described a fixed wake time in interviews. What she has described is writing in sustained concentrated periods rather than short daily windows.
Writing Location
Works from home in Dublin. She has described her process in terms of long focused sessions rather than a specific physical setup, and has been deliberately vague about the details of her private life.
Daily Output
Fast by any standard. The first draft of Conversations with Friends ran to around 100,000 words and was completed in approximately three months. She has described starting a new project with no discipline required because the excitement carries her forward.
Tools
Laptop. She has mentioned in interviews that a significant part of her style developed through writing emails, which suggests the screen rather than the notebook is her native surface.
Famous Ritual
Each novel is written as a conscious response to a book she admires: Conversations with Friends to Jane Austen's Emma; Normal People to George Eliot's Daniel Deronda; Beautiful World, Where Are You to Henry James's The Golden Bowl; Intermezzo to Joyce's Ulysses.
Books Written This Way
Conversations with Friends (2017), Normal People (2018), Beautiful World, Where Are You (2021), Intermezzo (2024).

Speed as a Method

A hundred thousand words in three months is not slow. For context, that's a pace most published novelists never reach even in their most productive stretches. Rooney did it on her first book, while still a graduate student, with no contract and no deadline. What that kind of speed produces, and what I think accounts for a lot of what makes her prose feel different from carefully assembled literary fiction, is a certain unguardedness. Fast drafts don't give you time to get too careful. The sentences come out lean because there's no opportunity to fatten them up.

She's been candid about not needing discipline at the start of a new project. In interviews she's described the opening phase of writing as almost effortless, driven by excitement rather than willpower. The characters arrive, she's said, and stay in her mind until she figures out their precise relationships to one another. That's not discipline in the traditional sense. It's closer to urgency. She writes because she can't stop thinking about the people in the book, and the writing is how she works out what they're actually doing.

The later stages are harder. She's spoken about writing and rewriting scenes that wouldn't resolve, about reading philosophy to find a way through a structural impasse while working on Intermezzo. The fast-draft opening is real, and so is the long difficult middle. What she doesn't appear to do is dawdle at the beginning, building intricate outlines before touching the prose. She gets into the draft and uses the draft itself as the thinking.

For writers who spend months planning before they feel ready to begin, this is the most transferable part of her practice. The first draft is research, not a finished book. Rooney seems to know this earlier than most. She gets the draft down fast, which means she gets to see what the actual problems are faster, which means she spends more of her time on the real work of revision rather than the avoidance behavior of extended preparation.

The Debater's Ear

In 2013, while an undergraduate at Trinity College Dublin, Rooney became the top-ranked debater at the European Universities Debating Championships. She represented The Hist, Trinity's storied historical society, and won best speaker at the competition. This is not a small achievement. European university debating at that level is a test of how quickly you can construct a coherent argument from almost nothing and land it in front of an audience that is actively looking for the holes in it.

She quit competitive debate shortly after, and in 2015 published an essay in The Dublin Review explaining why. Her critique was sharp: the circuit had become disconnected from the real-world stakes it was supposed to be engaging, and winning had started to feel like an exercise in performing conviction rather than having any. That disillusionment is interesting in itself. But what matters here is what the debate years left behind in the prose.

Open any of her novels to a conversation between two characters and you'll find it structured like a debate. Someone advances a position. The other pushes back. The first retreats a little, concedes a point, then comes at it differently. There's always a direction to the exchange, always something being argued toward or away from. Her characters don't just talk at each other. They're trying to persuade each other, or resist being persuaded, which is not the same thing as having a conversation in the way most literary fiction handles it. The argument is the intimacy. Two people arguing at length, in Rooney's hands, are two people letting each other in.

This is a skill that came from years of competitive practice, not from reading novels. The Irish Times interview from 2017 is one of the few places she's discussed the connection directly, noting that a significant part of her style developed through the act of extended written argument rather than through fiction. Emails, she said. Debate preparation. The habit of making a case in plain sentences, without ornament, and seeing whether it held. You can hear that training in every exchange her characters have.

She doesn't see any need for quotation marks. What other stylistic choices look like rebellion are, in her case, just solutions to problems she found while writing.

The Stripped Style and What the Routine Has to Do with It

Rooney writes dialogue without quotation marks. This is the most-discussed technical feature of her prose, and it tends to get explained in theoretical terms: it blurs the line between speech and interiority, it creates a fluid consciousness, it reflects the performative nature of language. All of that is true. But there's a more practical way to think about where the choice came from, and it connects directly to how she writes.

She's said, simply, that she doesn't see any need for quotation marks. When you're drafting at speed, on a laptop, in a style shaped more by the essay and the email than by any particular novelistic tradition, the punctuation conventions of fiction can start to feel arbitrary. Rooney came to the novel from debate and from academic writing, both of which handle quotation differently. Her prose strips out anything that slows it down or signals formality. The absence of quote marks is the same impulse as the absence of ornate description, the short declarative sentences, the preference for dialogue over interiority. She's trimming the frame so the exchange between people can breathe.

The effect on the reader is real and worth taking seriously. In most fiction, dialogue and thought are visually distinct. You know when someone speaks and when someone thinks. Rooney collapses that gap, and the result is a prose texture where you can't be sure how much of what a character says they actually mean, because it reads the same as what they're thinking. That ambiguity is the subject of all four of her novels. Every relationship in her books turns on the question of whether people mean what they say and say what they mean. The style is the argument.

Writers who've tried to imitate this and found it difficult are usually making the same mistake: they think it's a stylistic decision you layer onto existing habits. It's not. It comes from building a prose style from scratch, out of nonfiction influences, at the speed of drafting rather than revision. If you've spent ten years learning to write dialogue with quotation marks, removing them is an awkward retrofit. Rooney arrived at the stripped page before the habits calcified. That's the real advantage of writing your first novel fast, in your twenties, before you've had time to pick up too many conventions to unlearn. Ambivalence About Fame and Why It Matters

Normal People was published in 2018 in Ireland and 2019 everywhere else, and within a year Rooney had become one of the most talked-about novelists alive. Then the television adaptation arrived and the scale of the attention expanded further. By the time Beautiful World, Where Are You came out in 2021, the fame had its own story, separate from the books, and Rooney was very public about finding it oppressive.

In interviews around that period she described fame as a kind of hell, a system that doesn't work for anyone except, as she put it, presumably some shareholders somewhere. She described what celebrity actually involves: invasions of privacy, obsessive attention from strangers, people who've decided they hate you without having met you. She reportedly has her own name muted on social media, doesn't read her reviews, and has said she questions whether any writer should have to disclose the facts of their personal life just because they've published a novel.

This isn't incidental to the writing life. Rooney's fourth novel, Intermezzo, arrived in 2024 after a longer gap than usual, and she's discussed in the Paris Review conversation with Merve Emre how she spent significant time writing and rewriting scenes that didn't feel right, reading philosophical texts to find a way through. Some of that difficulty might be the natural friction of a harder book. Some of it might be what happens when the conditions surrounding the writing change shape. Hemingway could write five hundred words every morning in Cuba because Cuba was far from the noise. Rooney has to write the same intimate books about interiority and longing while being one of the most dissected private lives in contemporary literature. That's an unusual pressure to work under.

What she's said about this, consistently, is that she writes to figure out what she thinks. The novel is the thinking process, not the presentation of conclusions already reached. That's why she guards the private self so carefully. The thinking-out-loud can only happen if there's a version of her that the public doesn't have access to. The books are as candid as they are because everything else is kept back.

Sally Rooney's Writing Routine: The Full Picture

Sally Rooney's writing routine is harder to reconstruct than most because she's actively resistant to the idea of the author as a public figure with a legible daily practice. What we have is partial. She writes in Dublin. She works on a laptop. She drafts fast when the project is new and rewrites slowly when the problems become visible. She has described her approach to a new book as not requiring discipline, because the excitement of the characters is enough to carry her forward, and she's described the later stages of a novel as a period of rereading and rewriting that can stretch over a long time.

What we also know is that each book is built around a literary forebear. Rooney told Merve Emre in their Paris Review conversation in October 2024 that she writes each novel in conscious dialogue with a book she loves: Conversations with Friends responding to Austen's Emma, Normal People to George Eliot's Daniel Deronda, Beautiful World, Where Are You to James's The Golden Bowl, and Intermezzo to Joyce's Ulysses. This is a structural practice, not just an intellectual game. It gives each novel a formal problem to solve rather than just a subject to describe. It's why her books feel pressurized rather than meandering, even when very little external action happens in them.

The routine, finally, is this: she gets into the draft before she feels ready, she writes through the characters rather than designing them in advance, she borrows the formal problem from a book she respects, she strips everything out of the prose that isn't load-bearing, and then she spends however long it takes to make the scenes do what the argument requires. The speed at the start and the rigour at the end are not contradictory. They're the same method at different stages.

Sources

The best primary source for Rooney's writing process is her conversation with Merve Emre, published by The Paris Review in October 2024, recorded at the Southbank Centre in London, where she discusses her approach to drafting, her debts to earlier novelists, and what she means by loving the limitations of the form. The Irish Times interview from 2017 is the clearest source for her comments on email as a stylistic influence and her account of how her style developed. For the debating background and her departure from the circuit, see her 2015 essay in The Dublin Review. Her views on fame are documented across several interviews, most directly in The New European's profile and interviews given around the publication of Beautiful World, Where Are You in 2021. Information on the three-month drafting of Conversations with Friends appears in early interview coverage of her debut, including at the Hazlitt profile and STET magazine interview from 2017.

What You Can Steal

Rooney's practice is more transferable than it looks, because most of what she does is a set of attitudes rather than a fixed schedule. Here's what's actually worth taking:

  • Get the draft down before you're ready. Rooney doesn't plan her way to the first page. She gets in fast, while the excitement is still running, and uses the draft as the thinking. If you've been planning for three months and haven't written a sentence, the plan is the problem.
  • Find a formal problem from a book you love. Every Rooney novel is built in response to a specific predecessor. Give your book a structural argument to make, borrowed from a work you respect. It sharpens the project in ways that a pitch document or a theme statement can't.
  • Let your nonfiction influences into the prose. Rooney's style came partly from debate prep and emails, not just from reading novels. Whatever you write outside of fiction, letters, essays, arguments, carries instincts worth bringing across. The stripped, fast, argumentative quality of her dialogue is a nonfiction skill applied to fiction.
  • Treat your private life as a working condition, not a subject. The intimacy in her books exists because there's a version of herself she keeps back. The writing isn't autobiography. She guards the interior life precisely so she can use it without losing it.
  • Revision is where the speed gets paid for. Fast drafting only works if you're willing to spend serious time in the rewrite. Rooney has described scenes she rewrote repeatedly over long stretches. The fast opening is not an excuse to skip the hard work. It's a way of reaching the hard work faster.
