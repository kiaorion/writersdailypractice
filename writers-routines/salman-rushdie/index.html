Salman Rushdie's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 1, 2026 | 9 min read

Salman Rushdie writes in the first minutes of the day, often still in his pajamas, going straight from bed to his desk before he's brushed his teeth or made coffee. The logic is simple and he's stated it plainly in interviews: sleep produces a kind of creative charge, a "little package of creative energy," as he put it in a conversation with journalist Cal Fussman, and squandering that charge on breakfast or email is a mistake he refuses to make. He works for an hour or two before doing anything else, then the rest of the day happens.

What makes this routine worth studying is that Rushdie maintained some version of this discipline across the hardest possible circumstances. He wrote Midnight's Children while holding a full-time advertising job at Ogilvy and Mather in London, five years of drafting on the side of a career in copywriting. Then came the fatwa years, when Ayatollah Khomeini issued a death sentence against him in 1989, and for nearly a decade Rushdie lived under police protection, moving through a succession of safe houses across the UK and US with an alias no one was supposed to know. He kept writing through all of it. The routine he'd built held, even when the conditions around it became genuinely extreme. That's the argument this page is making: a real writing practice isn't fragile.

Profile

The Routine at a Glance

Wake Time
Early morning, no fixed hour stated publicly, but he goes straight to the desk upon waking before any other activity begins.
Writing Location
His home office. During the fatwa years (1989–1998), that meant wherever the current safe house happened to be, with Special Branch officers stationed outside.
Daily Output
He has not discussed target word counts. The emphasis in his accounts is on protecting the first creative hours, not hitting a number. He works for one to two hours before stopping to attend to anything else.
Tools
Computer. Rushdie has talked about the physical act of typing as part of his thinking process, and his MasterClass covers revision at the sentence level, which implies working directly in a document rather than longhand.
Famous Ritual
Going directly to the desk in pajamas, without brushing teeth or making breakfast first, to protect what he called the creative energy nourished by sleep. The world hadn't started yet; the writing had to come before it did.
Books Written This Way
Midnight's Children (1981), Haroun and the Sea of Stories (1990), The Moor's Last Sigh (1995), The Ground Beneath Her Feet (1999), Shalimar the Clown (2005).

The Copywriter Who Learned to Write Novels

Before Rushdie was a Booker Prize winner or a man living under police guard, he was writing ad copy at Ogilvy and Mather in London. He came up with "irresistibubble" for Aero chocolate bars. He wrote "Naughty but Nice" for cream cakes. He wrote "That'll do nicely" for American Express, a line that ran for years. And while he was doing all of that, he was also five years deep into writing Midnight's Children, a novel that would eventually span 533 pages, cover the entire sweep of India's independence and partition, and win every major prize available to it.

The advertising job wasn't just a source of income. Rushdie has said directly that the professional habits he built in those years shaped his writing discipline in ways that outlasted the ad career itself. Copywriting demands compression, rhythm, and the willingness to throw out a line that isn't working and write a better one immediately, without sentiment. When you bill by the hour and a client is waiting, you can't sit around hoping inspiration arrives. You write, you revise, you move. Those habits transferred. When Rushdie finally left advertising to write full time in the early 1980s, after Midnight's Children had won the Booker Prize in 1981, he didn't abandon the discipline. He kept the professional relationship with the work that the ad years had forced on him.

The five-year timeline for Midnight's Children is worth sitting with. He funded research trips to India on a £700 advance from his first, largely forgotten novel Grimus (1974), taking what he later described as a "journey of fifteen-hour bus rides and humble hostelries" to recover the Bombay childhood the book would require. He was doing all of this while writing copy and paying rent in London. The novel came out of a full life, written in the gaps of a working one, and it's as maximalist and time-dense a book as the English language has produced. The discipline to build that kind of architecture in stolen hours is not a small thing.

Writing Under a Fatwa

On February 14, 1989, Valentine's Day, Ayatollah Khomeini broadcast a fatwa on Iranian radio declaring that Rushdie and everyone involved in publishing The Satanic Verses should be killed. A bounty followed. Rushdie went from being a celebrated novelist to being a man whose address could not be known. Special Branch assigned him an alias at the start. He chose "Joseph Anton," borrowing the first names from Conrad and Chekhov, two writers he loved, and for nearly a decade that was who he was in official records and to the officers who guarded him.

He wrote through all of it. The bibliography from the fatwa years is striking. In 1990, one year after the death sentence, he published Haroun and the Sea of Stories, a children's novel he'd partly written to explain his situation to his son Zafar. Then came Imaginary Homelands in 1991, a collection of essays and criticism he'd assembled from the previous decade's work. East, West, a short story collection, appeared in 1994. The Moor's Last Sigh, a full novel, followed in 1995. The Iranian government formally stepped back from enforcing the fatwa in 1998. In those nine years, Rushdie produced four books.

The fact of this output matters more than any lesson you can draw from it. You can say, correctly, that writing was his anchor during the instability, that the routine gave him control over something when everything else was outside his control. You can say the books were proof of life, evidence that the fatwa hadn't stopped what it was meant to stop. Those things are true. The simpler observation is that he'd built a practice durable enough to survive genuinely terrible circumstances, and most writing practices don't have to survive anything nearly that bad. What's your excuse for not writing today, against that?

All Writing Is Rewriting

Rushdie has said in his MasterClass on storytelling and writing that "all writing is rewriting," and he means it literally, not as an encouraging aphorism for people who hate their first drafts. His view is that the first pass at a sentence is almost never the correct one, that every paragraph carries false notes waiting to be found, and that the writer's job during revision is to hear the rhythm of the prose the way a musician hears pitch. He uses the word "music" deliberately. In his view, the way a sentence sounds is part of what it means, almost subliminally, and you can only catch the off notes by going back through the text with the same attention you gave it when you were generating it.

This matters for understanding his morning routine. Rushdie isn't a writer who generates a fast, dirty draft and cleans it up later in a separate pass. The evidence from his own accounts suggests he works more carefully and more slowly, treating language as something you wrestle with rather than receive. That's a different model than the "write drunk, edit sober" school of thought. The morning hours produce a certain quality of attention, and he's using that attention to build sentences that will hold up, not just to get words on a page so there's something to work with later.

He also advises writers to be their own best editors, which means developing the ability to read your own work as if someone else wrote it. That's harder than it sounds. Most writers either love their own sentences too much to cut them or hate their drafts so completely that they can't see what's working. Rushdie's version of revision is calibrated and surgical: read for what's superfluous, read for what's missing, read for rhythm and false notes, then fix only those things. His novels are dense and long, but they don't feel bloated. That's what sustained, serious revision produces.

Language as the Real Risk

Rushdie has said in interviews that the real risks in literature are "linguistic and intellectual," and that political courage in writing gets more attention than it deserves relative to the quieter courage of making something original on the sentence level. He's had more personal experience with political risk than almost any living novelist, so when he says the linguistic risks matter more, it's worth taking seriously. He survived the fatwa. The thing he's still working on, still treating as the central problem, is language itself.

This shapes how he talks about the writing life in a way that's different from most author interviews. He's less interested in the question of inspiration, less interested in the mystical aspects of creative production that writers love to discuss when asked about their routines. His MasterClass and his interviews return repeatedly to craft, to the sentence, to revision. The morning hours he protects aren't sacred time for communion with the muse. They're working hours. He's protecting them because the work requires sustained attention and that attention is finite, depleted by the day as it fills up with other things, so you take it before the day gets to it.

There's a quote of his that I think is the most honest thing he's said about what writing actually is: "Language is courage: the ability to conceive a thought, to speak it, and by doing so to make it true." The first-draft optimism of that statement, the faith that speaking a thing makes it real, sits right next to the revision philosophy, the insistence that what you first put down almost always needs work. Both things are true at once for Rushdie. You have to believe the language can do something, and you have to be willing to accept that your first attempt at making it do the thing was probably wrong.

Salman Rushdie's Morning Writing Routine

Salman Rushdie's morning writing routine is not complicated and he's never claimed it was. He gets up. He goes to his office. He writes. The pajamas, the unbrushed teeth, the skipped breakfast: all of that is just the mechanism for protecting the transition from sleep to desk, to arrive at the writing before the cognitive machinery has fully engaged with the rest of his life and started sorting through its demands. He told Cal Fussman that sleep produces a small, specific package of creative energy, and he treats it as a real resource to be spent deliberately rather than burned off on email or the morning news.

For the five years he spent writing Midnight's Children while working full time in advertising, the morning hours were among the few times available. The job occupied the day. The novel occupied the margins. There's something instructive about the fact that the book he produced under those conditions became the most decorated novel in the history of the Booker Prize, winning not just the 1981 prize but the Booker of Bookers in 1993 and the Best of the Booker in 2008, chosen twice by public vote as the greatest prize winner across four decades of competition. Midnight's Children was a stolen-hours book, written by a copywriter on the side, and it ran laps around the competition.

After the advertising years ended, Rushdie kept the structure. The routine survived his fame, his move from London to New York, the fatwa, the safe houses, the police protection, the stabbing at the Chautauqua Institution in August 2022, and the long recovery that followed. A routine that can survive a near-fatal knife attack has proven itself. What it requires, in the end, is only that you go to the desk before you do anything else. Everything after that is the work.

Sources

The primary source for Rushdie's morning routine and the "package of creative energy" account is journalist Cal Fussman's reporting on his conversation with Rushdie, cited across multiple writing routine archives. Rushdie's own account of his writing philosophy appears in his Paris Review Art of Fiction No. 186 interview, conducted by Jack Livings and published in 2005, where he discusses his working methods and approach to language. His MasterClass on storytelling and writing contains extended discussion of his revision philosophy, including the "all writing is rewriting" framework and his advice on rhythm and language. Joseph Anton: A Memoir (2012) covers the fatwa years in detail, including his continued writing under protection. The quote "A book is a version of the world..." originally appeared in his 1990 essay "In Good Faith," collected in Imaginary Homelands: Essays and Criticism 1981-1991 (1991). The advertising career details, including the Ogilvy lines and his statement that advertising taught him craft, are documented in multiple profiles including the archived Ogilvy blog and his Wikipedia biography.

What You Can Steal

Rushdie's routine translates well to writers who aren't novelists under police protection, which is to say almost everyone. Here's what's actually worth taking:

  • Go to the desk before you do anything else. Brushing your teeth, making coffee, checking your phone: all of those things begin a different kind of day. The morning writing hour works precisely because it comes before the rest of the day's circuitry has engaged. Protect the transition from sleep to work as a separate event, and don't let anything else happen first.
  • Treat revision as the real work, not the cleanup. Rushdie's approach to revision isn't fixing typos after the real writing is done. Revision is where the prose gets its music, where false notes get caught, where superfluous sentences get cut. Build your practice around returning to the work, not just generating it.
  • Build the discipline before you need it. Rushdie's routine held during the fatwa years because he'd built it during the advertising years, long before circumstances required it to be resilient. You want a practice that's already solid before life tries to disrupt it. Start now, with whatever time you have, so the habit is load-bearing before it has to carry anything heavy.
  • Take the professional attitude toward the work. In his advertising years, Rushdie couldn't wait for inspiration because a client was waiting. That habit of showing up and producing regardless of how he felt about the work on a given morning is something he credits directly to the ad career. A professional relationship with writing means sitting down whether you want to or not.
  • Write the book you need to read. Midnight's Children was partly a book Rushdie wrote because he needed to recover his Bombay childhood, to make sense of his own displacement. The motivation that comes from writing something you need to exist is more durable than any external deadline or market consideration. Figure out what that book is for you, and write it in the morning hours, before anything else gets to them.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Salman Rushdie's writing routine?

Rushdie writes first thing in the morning, going straight to his office in his pajamas before doing anything else. He has described sleep as producing a "little package of creative energy" that he doesn't want to waste on breakfast or brushing his teeth. He works for an hour or two, then begins the rest of his day.

How did Salman Rushdie write during the fatwa years?

Between 1989 and 1998, Rushdie lived under police protection and moved through a series of safe houses across the UK and US after Ayatollah Khomeini issued a fatwa calling for his death. Despite those conditions, he kept writing and published four books during that period: Haroun and the Sea of Stories (1990), Imaginary Homelands (1991), East, West (1994), and The Moor's Last Sigh (1995).

How long did it take Salman Rushdie to write Midnight's Children?

Five years. He wrote most of it while working as an advertising copywriter in London, funding research trips to India on a £700 advance from his earlier novel Grimus. Midnight's Children was published in 1981 and won the Booker Prize the same year. It later won the Booker of Bookers in 1993 and the Best of the Booker in 2008.

What does Salman Rushdie say about revision?

Rushdie has said that all writing is rewriting, and he means it as a description of craft rather than an encouragement. In his MasterClass, he argues that writers should be revising their language constantly, reading for rhythm, false notes, and what's been left unsaid. His view is that the first pass at a sentence is almost never the correct one, and that returning to the work is where the real writing happens.
