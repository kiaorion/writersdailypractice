What Is Salman Rushdie's Writing Advice?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Salman Rushdie's advice to other writers isn't mystical, and he's said as much himself. It comes down to showing up on a fixed schedule and treating revision as most of the actual work.

The Direct Answer
Show up on a fixed schedule, and expect nearly everything you draft to need rewriting.
That's the core of the advice he's given in interviews and in the MasterClass he taught on storytelling and writing.

Treat It Like a Job

Rushdie's advice starts with a refusal to wait around for inspiration. He's compared the work to punching a clock, something you do on schedule whether or not you feel like it that day. The comparison isn't incidental. He spent close to a decade as a professional copywriter before he wrote fiction full time, and the deadline discipline from that career followed him into novels.

I've always told myself simply to treat it like a nine-to-five job. If you have a job, you just go and do it. It doesn't matter whether you're feeling good that day.

Salman Rushdie, Harvard Business Review interview, 2015

He's made the same point more bluntly elsewhere in that conversation: once a writer stops giving themselves an excuse, the work tends to follow. 'Once your mind understands that it has no excuses, it's remarkable how it begins to play along, do its job,' he said.

All Writing Is Rewriting

The clearest piece of craft advice he's given is short enough to fit on a sticky note. 'It's often been said, and I think it's true, all writing is rewriting,' he says in his MasterClass on storytelling and writing. His point is less about first drafts being bad and more about them being unfinished by definition. Treating a draft as raw material, rather than a final answer, is what lets a writer keep moving instead of freezing at the first sentence.

He's also direct about how to get past the blank page: stop trying to make the first attempt perfect, and get something down. Once a draft exists, he argues, a different faculty takes over, what he calls the critical imagination, and that's the tool for finding what's wrong and fixing it. The creative imagination's job is to generate the material. The critical imagination's job is to interrogate it afterward.

By his own account, his process has shifted over the decades. 'Now I write much more slowly and I revise a lot as I go,' he told the Paris Review, describing a move away from pushing out a fast draft and cleaning it up later. The payoff, he's said, is that pages worked over early need less fixing down the line: 'I find that when I've got a bit done, it seems to require less revision than it used to.'

What You Can Steal

  • Show up on a schedule. Rushdie's advice is to treat writing like a job with fixed hours rather than something you wait to feel like doing.
  • Get a draft down before you judge it. Let the critical imagination stay off duty until there's something to critique.
  • Revise while the page is still fresh. Rushdie found that early attention to a passage means less cleanup work later.
  • Expect rewriting to be most of the job. If it's true for a Booker Prize winner, it's true for the rest of us.
