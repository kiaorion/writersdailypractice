Sarah J. Maas's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 14, 2026 | 10 min read

Sarah J. Maas is one of the few living novelists who has managed to sustain three interconnected fantasy series simultaneously while consistently landing on the New York Times bestseller list. The Throne of Glass series, A Court of Thorns and Roses, and Crescent City are not thin books. They run long, they carry enormous amounts of world-building, and they're written to be re-read. Readers come back for new installments the way people return to a place they love. Building something like that requires a working method that can handle serious structural complexity without breaking down under the weight of it.

Maas started writing the story that would become Throne of Glass when she was sixteen, posting chapters online through FanFiction.net. By the time it published through Bloomsbury in 2012, she'd been living inside those characters for years. That origin shapes how she talks about the work. She's not precious about the drafting stage, and she's never pretended that the first version of anything is the thing she's actually trying to make. The routine reflects that. Get the story down, then fix it.

Binge-Drafting: What It Actually Means

Writers tend to sort themselves into two camps. There are the daily-quota people, who sit down every morning and put a fixed number of words on the page whether or not they feel like it. And there are the binge-drafters, who write in long, concentrated bursts, going hard on a project for days or weeks before coming up for air. Maas belongs clearly to the second camp, and she's been open about it in interviews over the years. She doesn't frame it as a lack of discipline. She frames it as understanding how her own creative energy actually works.

Binge-drafting has a specific logic to it, especially for long-form fantasy. When you're inside a 700-page novel with three or four interconnected plotlines, the amount of information you need to hold in your head while writing any given chapter is enormous. The longer you're away from the manuscript, the more time you spend each session rebuilding that internal model before you can move forward. Daily-quota writers who write an hour and then step away for twenty-three hours are constantly paying the re-entry cost. A writer who stays inside the book for a week straight pays it once.

The tradeoff is that binge-drafting requires blocking time rather than stealing it. You can carve a daily hour out of almost any life. You can't as easily carve a two-week sprint. For Maas, who has spoken in interviews about working around family life and the demands of being a mother, this means the scheduling problem is real. The approach requires planning the drafting windows in advance, protecting them, and trusting that the silence between sprints is part of the process rather than a sign that something has gone wrong.

What comes out of a binge draft is often a mess. Maas has said clearly that her first drafts are rough and that heavy revision is where the actual writing happens. The binge gets the story out. It's not the book yet.

Music as a Drafting Tool

A lot of writers use music. Maas uses it differently. Before she starts drafting a book, she builds a playlist for it. She's talked about this in interviews and on social media over the years, and she shares the playlists with readers as a companion to each release. The playlist for A Court of Silver Flames went live when the book did, as a way for readers to hear the emotional landscape she'd been inside while writing it. It's become a recognizable part of how she releases work, but the origin of the practice is functional, not marketing. She builds the playlist because it helps her write.

What she's describing, at its core, is a way of keeping the emotional register of a book consistent across a long drafting period. A fantasy novel can take a year or more to draft. The tone of the opening chapters needs to connect to the tone of the climax even though they might have been written eight months apart. Music is a shortcut to mood. If you've spent six months conditioning yourself to associate a particular set of songs with a particular character's emotional state, you can put on those songs and be back inside that state in minutes, regardless of how much time has passed.

This is a more sophisticated version of the writer's ritual than most people give it credit for. Morrison watched the dawn light through her kitchen window to trigger her writing state. Hemingway reread his previous pages to get back into the prose rhythm. Maas puts on a playlist. The mechanism is different; the function is the same. You're building a Pavlovian link between a sensory input and a creative state, and then using that link to get to work faster every time you sit down.

Readers of romantasy tend to be emotionally invested in ways that go beyond most genre fiction. They reread. They make playlists themselves. The fact that Maas shares hers feeds that dynamic, but it also reflects how central the emotional consistency of a long series is to why readers come back. The books feel continuous in their emotional tone because the drafting process was emotionally continuous. The playlist is part of how she keeps that thread intact across hundreds of thousands of words and years of writing.

World-Building Across Three Series at Once

Maas is one of a small number of contemporary novelists who are actively writing across multiple series simultaneously, and all three of hers share the same fictional universe. A character from the Throne of Glass world can theoretically exist in the same cosmology as Feyre from ACOTAR or Bryce from Crescent City. That's an enormous amount of continuity to manage, and it requires a kind of structural memory that goes beyond what a single novel demands.

She hasn't published a detailed account of how she tracks this across projects, but the shape of her output gives some clues. She tends to complete one series or see it to a natural resting point before shifting primary attention to another. The ACOTAR books and the Crescent City books have overlapped in her schedule, but there's usually a dominant project in any given period. This is probably less a deliberate strategy than a reflection of how binge-drafting actually works. You can't go deep on two things at once. You go deep on one and let the other sit.

The Crescent City series introduced a contemporary urban fantasy setting alongside the established high-fantasy world of Throne of Glass and the fae-world of ACOTAR. Writing across those registers while keeping the cosmological consistency intact is a serious craft challenge. The world-building detail in her books doesn't feel like it was added in revision. It reads like something she had mapped before the drafting started, which is consistent with how writers who sustain long series tend to work. You don't invent the world as you go when the series is going to run for seven books. You know it first.

If you want to take the genre quiz to figure out where your own project fits, there's a case to be made that Maas's series run the full spectrum from YA fantasy to adult romantasy, which affects how the world-building needs to function. The emotional texture expected by ACOTAR readers is different from what the Throne of Glass audience signed on for in 2012. Managing that across a shared universe, across a career that's now more than a decade long, is a structural accomplishment as much as a creative one.

The First Draft Is Not the Book

Maas has said in interviews that she writes first drafts quickly and revises heavily. The revision process is where most of the actual writing happens for her. This is a less romantic way to describe the work than most readers imagine, but it's how a lot of commercially successful novelists actually operate, and there are good reasons for it.

A fast first draft lets you find out what the story actually is before you spend months perfecting sentences in scenes that don't belong in the final version. You're not writing the book yet. You're building the clay. The pressure of trying to write a good sentence while also discovering the plot creates a kind of paralysis that can stall a novel for years. By separating the drafting from the revising, you give each phase permission to do its own job. The draft exists to generate material. The revision exists to turn that material into a book.

For a writer producing the volume Maas produces, this approach is probably the only one that scales. Kingdom of the Wicked, A Court of Silver Flames, and House of Earth and Blood came out in overlapping years. The pace required to sustain that output while also being a mother and a public-facing author with a large fan community means the drafting process has to move. Perfectionism in the first-draft phase would make that pace impossible. The revision pass is where she can slow down.

What's interesting about this approach in the context of romantasy specifically is that the genre has strong reader expectations around emotional pacing. The tension between two leads needs to build across hundreds of pages before the payoff, and readers notice when it doesn't. Getting that arc right requires a revision eye that understands the genre's emotional grammar. Maas has demonstrated, across enough books and enough reader response, that she has that eye. The fast draft buys her the raw material; the heavy revision shapes it into something that earns the emotional payoff the genre promises.

Sarah J. Maas's Writing Routine

The Sarah J. Maas writing routine doesn't fit neatly into the archetype of the disciplined daily writer who shows up at the same desk at the same hour every morning. She's talked in interviews about writing around her family life, around her son, around the realities of being a working author with a large public presence and multiple active deadlines. The routine is flexible by necessity and intensive by design.

What characterizes her working method more than schedule is the way she enters a drafting phase. She builds the playlist first. She's described starting the music before starting the words, using the songs to locate the emotional territory of the book before she tries to put characters in it. Once the drafting starts, it tends to run hard, with sustained writing sessions that prioritize getting deep into the manuscript rather than stopping and restarting across dozens of short daily sessions. The playlist stays on. The world stays open.

After the draft is done, the revision work begins in earnest, and by her own account this is where she spends a significant portion of her time. The books that reach readers have been through multiple passes. The prose in an ACOTAR novel reads the way it does not because Maas writes that way in a first draft, but because she revises toward it. That distinction matters for anyone who's trying to learn from her method. The goal in drafting is completion. The goal in revision is quality. Keeping those two goals separate is probably the most transferable thing about how she works.

What You Can Steal

Maas's routine is most useful if you're working on something long, emotionally complex, or structured across multiple books. These are the pieces worth taking:

  • Build the playlist before you build the draft. Spend a few hours finding the songs that match the emotional tone of what you're about to write. Then put that playlist on every single time you sit down to draft. Over weeks, it becomes a faster on-ramp to the creative state than almost any other ritual.
  • Separate drafting mode from revision mode completely. In draft mode, your job is to generate material and finish the story. Sentences can be bad. Scenes can be wrong. You fix all of that in revision. Trying to do both at once slows the draft without improving the revision.
  • Understand your creative energy pattern before you build a routine around it. If you write better in long bursts than in short daily sessions, stop forcing the daily-quota model. Design your schedule around how your energy actually flows. The most productive writing habit is the one that matches your actual capacity, not someone else's.
  • Know the world before you write the book. For long series especially, the world needs to be more solid in your head than any of it will ever appear on the page. The reader feels the difference between a world that was discovered during drafting and one that was already known.
  • Plan the revision from the beginning. If you know the first draft is going to be rough, you're not setting yourself up to fail. You're making a conscious choice to prioritize speed and completion in round one, which frees you to prioritize quality in round two.
