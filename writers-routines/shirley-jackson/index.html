Shirley Jackson's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 14, 2026 | 9 min read

Shirley Jackson wrote some of the most unsettling fiction of the twentieth century from inside an ordinary house in North Bennington, Vermont, surrounded by four children, a distracted academic husband, too many cats, and a kitchen that needed constant attention. She wrote The Haunting of Hill House in a house that was itself haunted by noise. The grocery lists and dentist appointments and school pickups and dinner parties were the novel's weather, the backdrop against which she had to find, every day, a few hours of something approaching silence.

Her routine is interesting precisely because it refuses the clean mythologies we build around writers' lives. There's no Havana study, no Paris garret, no countryside retreat. Jackson's working conditions were a busy domestic household in a Vermont college town, and she produced more than most writers who had every advantage she lacked. The output, documented in Ruth Franklin's exhaustive 2016 biography Shirley Jackson: A Rather Haunted Life, was staggering for someone writing in the cracks: six novels, two memoirs, hundreds of short stories, and a body of magazine work that ran for decades.

Profile

The Routine at a Glance

Wake Time
Variable. Jackson was a night person by temperament, and much of her serious work happened after the children were in bed, when the house finally went quiet.
Writing Location
Wherever she could, including the kitchen table and her study when she could claim it. In North Bennington, Vermont, where she lived from 1945 until her death in 1965, the household was large and the interruptions constant.
Daily Output
She aimed to write every day and tracked her output carefully. In productive stretches she could produce thousands of words; she was known to draft quickly when the time was available.
Tools
Typewriter. Jackson was a fast and committed typist, and typed her drafts directly rather than working longhand first.
Famous Ritual
Writing around the household rather than waiting for it to clear. She composed between meals, between carpools, between the demands of four children and a husband who required considerable domestic support.
Books Written This Way
The Lottery and Other Stories (1949), Hangsaman (1951), Life Among the Savages (1953), The Bird's Nest (1954), Raising Demons (1957), The Haunting of Hill House (1959), We Have Always Lived in the Castle (1962).

Writing Inside the Chaos

The setup in North Bennington was not conducive to the kind of concentration most writers insist they need. Stanley Edgar Hyman, Jackson's husband, was a literary critic and professor at Bennington College, and their house was a gathering point for faculty, students, and the miscellaneous people who seemed to accumulate around academic households. Jackson ran it. She cooked, managed the children, kept the social calendar, and somewhere inside all of that she kept writing.

What she had that made this possible was flexibility about when the writing happened and discipline about the fact that it had to happen, not ideal conditions by any standard measure. Ruth Franklin's biography describes Jackson typing while supervising homework, drafting in the early morning before the house woke, working late at night after everyone had gone to bed. The time was assembled from whatever the day left over, fragment by fragment, and she treated each piece as usable rather than insufficient.

This is harder than it sounds, because the psychological requirement of "real" writing time is one of the most powerful forms of procrastination available. The writer who needs two uninterrupted hours before she can start will find that those two hours never arrive in quite the right form. Jackson didn't wait. According to both her own comic accounts in her memoirs and Franklin's research in her letters and papers, she wrote in a house where the interruptions were constant, and she kept writing anyway. The result is a body of work that makes most writers with much better circumstances look underproductive.

She described her domestic life in the two memoirs, Life Among the Savages (1953) and Raising Demons (1957), with a deadpan comedy that made those books surprise bestsellers. The chaos was real. But what the memoirs also reveal, if you read between the humor, is how deliberately she protected the writing underneath all of it. The mess was her material. The children, the house, the sheer comic weight of keeping a large household running in a small Vermont town, all of it fed directly into her work in ways that are easy to miss when you're reading about hill houses and lottery tickets.

The Two-Track Output

Jackson ran two careers at once for most of her adult writing life, and this might be the strangest and most instructive fact about how she worked. On one track: gothic horror, psychological fiction, the kind of story that makes you feel the floor of reality tilt slightly beneath your feet. On the other: comic domestic memoir, warm and funny and sharply observed, the kind of writing that appeared in women's magazines and made suburban readers laugh with recognition.

These weren't separate phases of her career. They happened simultaneously, often in the same year. The Haunting of Hill House, published in 1959 and now considered one of the great horror novels in the English language, came out two years after Raising Demons, her second domestic memoir. The woman who wrote about Hill House's malevolent geometry and Eleanor Vance's slow dissolution was also the woman writing cheerful pieces about losing her children in department stores and the social politics of small-town Vermont.

This matters for the routine because it means Jackson wasn't a writer who found one mode and stuck with it. She moved between registers as the day required, and she seemed to have found the two streams of work mutually sustaining rather than contradictory. The domestic comedy gave her a place to put the texture of her daily life, all the chaotic material that surrounded her, and the dark fiction gave her a place to put everything the comedy couldn't hold. Both required her to see the same household clearly. One asked her to find it funny. The other asked her to find it frightening. She was capable of both, sometimes on the same afternoon.

For writers who've been told they need to find their lane and stay in it, Jackson's output is a useful counterexample. The range was a temperamental fact about how her mind worked, and the career that resulted was richer for it. If you're interested in how horror writers have shaped literary fiction more broadly, Jackson is where that conversation often starts.

Productivity Without a Room of One's Own

"I delight in what I fear."

Virginia Woolf's famous argument was that a woman needs a room of her own and money to write. It's basically correct, as a structural claim about what writers need. Jackson had neither in the conventional sense. She had a study at various points in their time in North Bennington, but it wasn't sacrosanct, and the household regularly invaded it. The money was always tight and then tighter. Stanley's salary from Bennington helped, but Jackson's magazine work and book advances were the financial infrastructure of the family, which meant she had to produce commercially in addition to producing the serious fiction.

The productivity that resulted came from a few specific habits documented in Franklin's biography and in Jackson's own papers at the Library of Congress. She kept track of what she was working on across multiple projects. She moved between them fluidly rather than insisting on completing one before starting another. When the gothic novel stalled, she'd write something lighter and return to the novel later. When the deadline on a magazine piece was immediate, she'd meet it and then go back to the longer work. This kind of project-switching is often called fragmented and bad for deep work, but Jackson's output suggests the opposite can be true for certain kinds of writers: having somewhere else to go when one door closes keeps you from sitting motionless in front of the closed door.

She also wrote fast when she was writing. The typewriter helped. Jackson could draft quickly and revise later, and she didn't treat the first draft as something that needed to emerge perfectly formed. The speed of the initial output was a feature, not a sign of carelessness. The Haunting of Hill House was, by Franklin's account, drafted in a concentrated period in 1958, after years of thinking about the book. The accumulation happened in the background of daily life. The actual drafting happened fast.

The Craft of Dread and the Domestic Uncanny

Jackson gave lectures on writing, collected posthumously in Come Along with Me (1968). In them she talks about the relationship between the ordinary and the frightening in a way that maps directly onto how she worked. She was interested in the moment when a familiar setting becomes wrong, when the house you know turns subtly hostile, when the town you've lived in for years reveals that it has always been watching you. That interest wasn't abstract. It came from a woman who lived in a house that was simultaneously the site of domestic comedy and creeping dread.

North Bennington was not a welcoming town for the Hymans. The faculty wife who was also a witch, who kept black cats and practiced tarot and wrote stories about mob violence and psychological collapse, was a complicated neighbor for a small Vermont community. Jackson documented the social hostility in her letters and used it in her fiction: the town that turns on a family, the community that excludes the outsider, the polite surface that conceals something rotten. The domestic setting wasn't incidental to her horror. The domestic setting was the source of it. You can take a genre quiz to find writers in the same vein, but Jackson's particular blend of household realism and gothic menace is genuinely hard to replicate.

What the routine produced, at its best, was fiction that felt like it could happen in the next room. Hill House is terrifying partly because its architecture is described the way you'd describe a real house, room by room, in the language of someone who has spent a lot of time inside actual houses noticing how they work. Eleanor Vance's dissociation reads the way it does because Jackson understood, from the inside, the way a domestic life can slowly consume the self of the person maintaining it. The horror came out of the home life. The routine that made the home life sustainable also made the horror legible.

Jackson said in her lectures that the only way to write about terror is to be specific. General dread is boring. Particular dread, the wrong angle on a staircase, the cold spot in a specific room, the name written on a wall in a handwriting that is yours but that you didn't write, is the thing that catches. She got to specificity partly through temperament and partly through habit: a writer who has spent years noticing the exact texture of ordinary household life has the raw material for that kind of particular horror. The grocery list isn't separate from the gothic novel. The grocery list is training for it.

Sources

The essential biography is Ruth Franklin's Shirley Jackson: A Rather Haunted Life (Liveright, 2016), which draws on Jackson's papers at the Library of Congress and offers the most thorough account of her working life, domestic circumstances, and literary output. Jackson's own domestic memoirs, Life Among the Savages (Farrar, Straus and Young, 1953) and Raising Demons (Farrar, Straus and Cudahy, 1957), are primary sources for her account of writing inside a busy household. The posthumous collection Come Along with Me (Viking, 1968), edited by Stanley Edgar Hyman, contains Jackson's lectures on writing, unfinished fiction, and short stories, and is the closest thing we have to her direct statements about craft. Her collected letters, quoted extensively in the Franklin biography, provide additional documentation of her working habits and her relationship to the household she wrote inside.

What You Can Steal

Jackson's routine is useful specifically because it doesn't require any special conditions. Here's what actually transfers:

  • Write in the cracks, not just the clear stretches. The two uninterrupted hours you're waiting for may not arrive this week. An hour at the kitchen table after breakfast and another one after the kids go to bed adds up to a book eventually. Jackson proved this repeatedly.
  • Let the two kinds of writing feed each other. If you write serious literary fiction, try also writing something light and comic. If you write humor, try also letting yourself go somewhere darker. Jackson's two-track output fed her productivity rather than diluting it.
  • The ordinary domestic world is horror material. If your writing tends toward dark or psychological fiction, the specific textures of your daily life, the house, the neighborhood, the social dynamics of the community you live in, are better raw material than anything you'll invent from scratch. Jackson's settings feel real because they were.
  • Switch projects when you stall. Having a lighter, more commercial project available alongside the serious one gives you somewhere to go when the serious one closes its doors for a day. Jackson moved between the domestic comedy and the gothic fiction as needed, and the overall output benefited.
  • Specificity is the thing. Jackson's lectures on writing keep returning to this point. General darkness is dull. The particular wrong detail, the specific incongruity, is what makes a reader feel it in their chest. Train that eye in ordinary life, and it'll be there when you need it in the fiction.
