How Many Words Is Stephen King's It?

Kia Orion | August 2, 2026 | 5 min read

Writers reach for It when they want permission to write long. The book runs to roughly 445,000 words, which is four or five standard novels bound into one brick, and King built it on the same daily quota he uses for everything else.

The Direct Answer
Around 445,000 words.
The figure repeated most often is 445,134 words, from Broke by Books' survey of 175 novel lengths. Other counts run lower, down to 437,781. No publisher releases an official number, so treat anything in the 437,000 to 446,000 band as the same answer. Viking's 1986 first edition ran 1,138 pages.

Why the Count Is a Range and Not a Number

Publishers don't print word counts, so every figure you find online comes from someone running a digital edition through a counter. That's where the spread comes from. The most quoted number, 445,134, appears in Broke by Books' survey of 175 novels. Word Counters puts It at 437,781. The gap is about 7,000 words, which sounds like a lot until you remember it's under two percent of the book.

Different counters handle hyphenated compounds, dialect spellings and section breaks differently, and different editions carry different front matter. For a working writer the distinction is academic. Anything between 437,000 and 446,000 tells you the same thing, which is that It runs past four times the length of a normal commercial novel.

It also isn't King's longest. The Stand in its 1990 Complete and Uncut edition restores hundreds of pages trimmed from the 1978 release and comes in near 470,000 words. It sits second in the catalog.

Five Years From a Wooden Bridge to 1,138 Pages

King has traced the origin of It to 1978, when he walked across an old wooden bridge in Boulder, Colorado and thought about the troll underneath in Three Billy Goats Gruff. Marianne Moore's line about imaginary gardens with real toads in them turned over in his head into real trolls in imaginary gardens. He let the idea sit for roughly two years before writing anything.

Drafting started around 1980. He finished the manuscript in 1985, and Viking published the novel on 15 September 1986. Call it five years from first page to last.

Run that against his own arithmetic and something useful falls out. King's stated daily goal is 2,000 words. At that rate, 445,000 words takes about 222 working days, comfortably under a year. The years went into incubation and into the structural problem of running two timelines twenty-seven years apart with a full cast in each. He published several other novels inside the same window. Length is rarely what makes a long book slow.

How It Compares to What Publishers Actually Buy

Commercial horror and thriller manuscripts land between 80,000 and 100,000 words. Literary fiction sits in the same band. Epic fantasy is the one category where an agent will look at 150,000 from an unknown writer, and even there most will ask you to cut. It is four and a half times the top of that range.

The reason King could sell it is the same reason it exists. By 1986 he had been a bestselling novelist for twelve years and had already shipped The Stand at close to 800 pages in its trimmed form. Publishers price a King brick differently because they know it will move. If you're benchmarking your own manuscript against It, benchmark the architecture instead of the count. Two interleaved timelines carrying the same seven characters is what produces that page total, and you can build the same shape at 120,000 words.

What You Can Steal

  • Judge length by structure, not by ambition. It is enormous because it runs two complete timelines with the same cast. Decide what your book's architecture demands, then let the count follow.
  • A big book is not a slow book. At 2,000 words a day, King's 445,000 words is about 222 sessions. The five years went into thinking and revising.
  • Don't debut at 400,000 words. Agents buy horror and thrillers at 80,000 to 100,000. King earned the right to a brick over twelve years of sales figures.
  • Let an idea sit before you draft it. Two years passed between the bridge in Boulder and the first page. The incubation is part of the work, and it's the part nobody counts.
