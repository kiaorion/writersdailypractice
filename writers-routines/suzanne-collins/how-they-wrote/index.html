How Did Suzanne Collins Get the Idea for The Hunger Games?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Suzanne Collins doesn't give many interviews, but she's told this particular story more than once, and it hasn't changed much in the retelling. The idea for The Hunger Games came from an ordinary night of channel surfing, when two unrelated broadcasts collided in her head. Here's what she's actually said about it.

The Direct Answer
She was channel surfing one night between a reality TV competition and coverage of the Iraq War, and the two images blurred together into the premise of The Hunger Games.
She's told the story in nearly identical terms in an official Scholastic Q&A and in an interview with School Library Journal.

Two Channels, One Unsettling Night

Collins has described the exact moment more than once, and the details barely shift between tellings. She was flipping through channels late at night. On one, a group of young people were competing on a reality show for prize money. On the next, actual footage of young people fighting a real war. She was tired, and the two kept blurring together until they stopped feeling like separate things.

I was channel surfing between reality TV programming and actual war coverage when Katniss's story came to me. One night I'm sitting there flipping around, and on one channel there's a group of young people competing for, I don't know, money maybe? And on the next, there's a group of young people fighting an actual war. And I was tired, and the lines began to blur in this very unsettling way, and I thought of this story.

Suzanne Collins, official Scholastic Q&A on The Hunger Games trilogy

She's given a slightly different version of the same story to School Library Journal, describing herself as lying in bed rather than sitting up, but the substance is identical. Two kinds of televised competition, one entertainment and one lethal, running close enough together that her mind stopped keeping them apart. That collision became the premise of Panem's Capitol: a government that packages real violence as entertainment for a population that's stopped noticing the difference.

The Myth Underneath the Broadcast

The channel-surfing story explains where the premise clicked into place, but Collins has said the underlying shape came from somewhere older. She's cited the Greek myth of Theseus and the Minotaur, which she read as a child: Athens, punished for a past offense, forced to send fourteen young people to Crete every so often to be thrown into the Labyrinth and killed, with parents powerless to stop it. Collins has said she found that detail chilling even as a kid, and the shape of it is easy to spot in Panem's reaping, districts forced to hand over their children while parents stand by.

The Roman gladiator games sit underneath the premise too. Panem takes its name from the Latin phrase panem et circenses, bread and circuses, and Collins has said the Capitol is deliberately loaded with Roman references. The idea built from three things landing on the same night: an old story about forced sacrifice, an old form of state-sponsored spectacle, and a very current image of war and entertainment sharing a screen.

Why a War Story Landed on Her, Specifically

Collins's father was a career Air Force officer who deployed to Vietnam for a year when she was a child, and she's said he made a point of teaching his kids what war actually cost, not only where it happened. That upbringing likely explains why the two broadcasts registered as connected instead of just uncomfortable. What You Can Steal

  • Let two unrelated things sit next to each other in your head. The idea didn't come from research. It came from noticing that two broadcasts felt more alike than they should have.
  • Ideas land on prepared ground. Collins caught that collision because she'd already spent years thinking about war, thanks to her father. Whatever you keep circling back to is worth paying attention to.
  • Old structures still work. The Theseus myth is thousands of years old and it still gave her trilogy its bones. You don't need a new shape, just a true one.
  • Tell your origin story the same way every time. Collins has repeated this account for over a decade without adding embellishment. Consistency reads as credibility.
