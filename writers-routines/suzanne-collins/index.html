Suzanne Collins's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 14, 2026 | 9 min read

Suzanne Collins spent years in children's television before she wrote a single word of The Hunger Games. She worked on scripts for Nickelodeon's Clarissa Explains It All, The Mystery Files of Shelby Woo, and other shows in the 1990s, learning how to hold a young audience's attention across a rigid structure of acts and commercial breaks. When she eventually moved into fiction and produced the Underland Chronicles and then the Hunger Games trilogy, the television training went with her. You can feel it in the pacing. A Collins chapter rarely ends where you want it to, and it usually ends where you don't expect.

Collins is one of the more private figures in contemporary publishing. She gives few interviews and has said almost nothing in public about her daily schedule, her word counts, or her physical writing setup. What she has talked about, in a small number of documented interviews with outlets like The New York Times, School Library Journal, and Scholastic, are the ideas behind the work: her father's military career and what it taught her about war, her conviction that children can handle serious moral questions, her belief that a writer should only publish when they actually have something to say. Those ideas, more than any observable daily habit, are the real architecture of her practice.

What Television Taught Her About Structure

Collins spent the better part of a decade writing for children's television before she published her first novel. The shows she worked on, including Clarissa Explains It All and others in the Nickelodeon stable, required their writers to build stories in tight units of time, with act breaks timed to commercial slots and scene endings engineered to hold a young audience through the gap. It's an apprenticeship in compression. You learn quickly that if a scene doesn't end with a small, discrete reason to stay, the viewer is gone.

When Collins moved into fiction, she brought that training with her. The Hunger Games trilogy is, among other things, a masterclass in chapter-level pacing, and it's not an accident. Every chapter does what a television act must do: it opens in motion, it raises or shifts the stakes before the final page, and it closes with an image or a revelation that makes stopping feel wrong. Readers have described staying up until two in the morning because they couldn't find a natural stopping point. That's a craft choice, not an accident of plot. It's the television act break, transplanted into prose, and it works just as well on paper as it did on screen.

Collins discussed this background in her 2008 Scholastic author materials and in interviews around the release of The Hunger Games. She was straightforward about the connection, describing how writing for television had taught her to think about structure before she thought about anything else. The chapter-as-episode is her fundamental unit. What happens scene by scene inside it matters, but the chapter's job is to make the next chapter feel necessary, and she'd learned that obligation on television sets.

There's a structural lesson in this that fiction writers don't always take seriously enough. Television writers are required to think in units. The unit is the episode, the act, the scene, the beat. A novelist writing without that discipline can follow a scene wherever it wants to go, and many do, which is why so many novels lose momentum in the middle. Collins never lost track of the unit. Every chapter has a job. The job is structural, not emotional, and it has to be done regardless of what the characters feel like doing that day.

War, Her Father, and Writing with Something to Say

Collins's father was a career military officer who served in Vietnam. She has described him in several interviews as someone who believed children were capable of understanding war, and who made a deliberate effort to explain it to her and her siblings in a way that was honest without being gratuitous. He took them to historical sites and ruins and used them as starting points for conversations about conflict, about what justifies fighting and what doesn't, about the costs that ordinary people pay for decisions made by governments and generals.

That education is the engine under The Hunger Games. Collins has said directly, in her 2010 interview with The New York Times, that the trilogy was conceived as a war narrative for young readers. She was thinking about the concept of a just war, about what circumstances might make a violent uprising defensible and what moral line separates a revolution from an atrocity. Katniss's arc across three books is, at its core, a test of that question, and the answer Collins arrives at is appropriately complicated: victory doesn't clean the bloodshed, and the people who do the fighting carry the cost even when the cause was right.

Connecting her dystopian fiction to that background is important because it explains why the books have weight that most similar novels don't. Collins wasn't writing action plots with a war backdrop for flavor. The war was the subject, and the young adult format was a deliberate choice rooted in her belief, inherited from her father, that younger readers deserve serious moral engagement. The stakes are real in her books because she took the moral framework seriously before she wrote a single scene.

Collins also mentioned, in the same New York Times interview, that the original idea for The Hunger Games came from channel surfing one evening and finding herself watching both a reality television competition and footage of the Iraq War on different channels. The two images collided in her mind in a way that felt thematically immediate. The pairing, entertainment and warfare running on adjacent screens, became the satirical premise of the Capitol's games. That's the kind of idea that only lands if a writer is primed to catch it, and Collins was primed because she'd spent years thinking about war's imagery and meaning, not just its violence.

Long Gaps, Deliberate Output

The gap between the end of the original Hunger Games trilogy in 2010 and the release of the prequel The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes in 2020 was ten years. Collins published nothing in that decade, at least nothing under her own name in fiction. Ten years is a long time in commercial publishing, where the standard expectation is one book a year for a bestselling author and where gaps longer than two or three years can make a career feel stalled. Collins appears to have been indifferent to that pressure.

She hasn't offered a lengthy explanation for the gap in any interview I'm aware of. What she has said, across multiple conversations over the years, is that she only writes when she has something specific to say. That phrase, or a version of it, appears in several profiles and author Q&As. The implication is that the act of publishing requires a genuine reason for the book to exist, not just a market window or a contractual deadline. Whether that means she was writing and discarding work during those years, or simply not writing at all, isn't documented publicly.

What the gap does tell you, though, is something about how Collins relates to output as a measure of a writing life. For a lot of writers, especially those working inside commercial publishing's expectations, productivity is equated with legitimacy. A year without a book feels like a year wasted. Collins seems to operate from the opposite position: a book published without a genuine reason is the waste. The ten-year gap, followed by Ballad and then Sunrise on the Reaping just five years later in 2025, suggests she works in bursts determined by ideas rather than calendars.

This is a harder model to follow than it sounds. Most working writers can't afford ten-year silences, and most don't have the kind of publishing infrastructure that holds a readership through that kind of absence. But the underlying principle, that output should be governed by conviction rather than velocity, is worth sitting with even if you can't apply it literally. A book written from genuine necessity is almost always a better book than a book written to schedule.

"My father was in Vietnam. He was career military, so he felt it was important for us to understand what war was. He had us read, go to films, visit sites."
Suzanne Collins, School Library Journal, 2010

Privacy and Letting the Work Speak

Collins gives very few interviews. By the standards of a writer who's sold tens of millions of books and launched one of the most successful film franchises of the 2010s, her public profile is extraordinarily low. She doesn't maintain social media accounts, doesn't do book tours in the conventional sense, and tends to keep her rare interviews focused on the work rather than her personal life. Her author bio on most editions of her books is short enough to fit in a tweet.

This reticence shapes what we can and can't know about her writing practice, and I think it's worth naming that honestly rather than papering over the gaps with invented detail. Plenty of writer routine pages tell you that an author woke at six, wrote for three hours, and made tea at nine. In Collins's case, none of that is documented. What's documented is her thinking, her sources of ideas, and the principles behind her output decisions. Those are, arguably, the more interesting things.

There's also something instructive about the privacy itself. Collins's public persona consists almost entirely of her books. She's not building a personal brand or a platform in the contemporary sense. The fiction is the thing, and it has to stand on its own because there's no author presence amplifying it. For younger writers who spend a lot of time thinking about visibility and social reach, Collins is a useful counterexample. The Hunger Games became one of the best-selling series in publishing history without its author being particularly visible. The books did the work.

Writers who want to understand Collins's practice are left, mostly, with the books themselves. You can find her television instincts in any chapter. You can find her father's influence in the way she handles military ethics and the cost of violence. You can find her conviction that young readers deserve serious treatment in the way she refuses to soften Katniss's moral compromises. If you want to know how how to find your fiction genre, Collins's career is a good case study: she wrote toward her own deepest questions and trusted that readers would meet her there.

Sources

The primary source for Collins's statements about her father and war themes is her 2010 interview with School Library Journal, conducted around the release of Mockingjay. Her interview with The New York Times in 2010 (with Susan Dominus) is the most detailed profile of her work and thinking available, and covers the channel-surfing origin story, her father's influence, and her views on children's literature and war. Scholastic's official author materials, including the audio interview Collins recorded for the original Hunger Games publication, contain her direct discussion of her television writing background and how it shaped her approach to chapter structure. These three sources are the documented foundation for nearly everything verifiable about her practice.

What You Can Steal

Collins's practice is harder to reverse-engineer than most because she's so private. What transfers is a set of convictions about what writing is for, not a schedule or a word count:

  • Learn structure from wherever it's taught most ruthlessly. Collins learned chapter endings from television act breaks, where failing to hold an audience has an immediate, measurable consequence. Find the discipline that won't let you get away with a weak ending, and study it.
  • Write from a moral question, not just a premise. The Hunger Games is a war narrative first and a dystopia second. Collins had an actual question she was trying to work out, rooted in her father's teaching, and that question gave the books their gravity. A premise generates plot. A moral question generates meaning.
  • Only publish when you have something to say. Ten years of silence followed by two books in five years suggests Collins writes toward ideas rather than toward deadlines. If you find yourself writing to fill a schedule rather than to answer a question, that's worth noticing.
  • Let the work carry the platform. Collins is one of the best-selling authors of the past twenty years and one of the least publicly visible. The books built the readership. The author's personality didn't need to.
  • Take young readers seriously. Collins's books are categorized as young adult, but they don't soften or condescend. The moral complexity is intact. Whatever your audience, treat them as capable of handling what you actually think.
