Tana French's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 14, 2026 | 9 min read

Tana French spent years on stage before she ever finished a novel. She trained as an actress, worked in Irish theatre, and learned, through that work, how to disappear into someone else's head entirely. When she eventually wrote In the Woods in 2007, she applied the same skill to the page. The narrator, Rob Ryan, a Dublin murder detective carrying a buried childhood trauma, didn't feel like a character she'd constructed from outside. He felt inhabited. That quality, a first-person narrator so fully rendered that the reader cannot find the seam between author and character, became the signature of everything she'd write after.

French tends to get filed under crime fiction, and the classification is accurate enough, but it undersells what she's actually doing. Her books are long, atmospheric, and deeply interested in how people deceive themselves, sometimes for decades, about who they are. The murder is the occasion, not the point. Understanding that about her work is the only way to understand her routine, because the process that produced these novels was built around character, around discovering what a person would actually do when a particular pressure is applied to their particular wound.

The Actress at the Desk

French trained as an actress at Trinity College Dublin and worked in professional theatre in Ireland for years before her first novel was published. She's talked in several interviews about what that training gave her as a writer, and the answer is more specific than "good instincts for character." Acting technique, at least the tradition she trained in, requires you to build a complete interior world for a person who isn't you. You understand what they want, what they're afraid of, what they'd never admit, and what they'd lie about to themselves first. You don't play the surface behavior. You play the engine driving it.

That's what French does on the page. Her narrators are not viewpoint cameras positioned for narrative convenience. They're people with such fully rendered inner lives that the reader starts picking up on things the narrator doesn't notice about themselves, which is exactly what makes the books so uneasy to read. Rob Ryan in In the Woods tells you certain things about the night that shattered his childhood, and gradually you realize his account has gaps he can't see. Cassie Maddox in The Likeness explains her motivations with such confidence that you start doubting them. The narrators are unreliable not because French has rigged the game but because that's how people actually work.

She said in a 2010 interview with The Guardian that acting taught her to think in terms of what a character wants that they can't say directly. "In acting, you're always looking for the subtext," she noted, "because nobody says what they mean." That observation sits at the center of everything she writes. The crime fiction structure gives her the scaffolding, a murder that needs solving, pressure that can't be ignored, people who have to reveal themselves under questioning, and her character work fills every room inside that structure.

What's worth noticing is how the acting background shapes her relationship to pace. A trained actor doesn't rush a scene. You stay in the moment longer than feels comfortable, because that discomfort is doing something. French's novels are famously slow by genre standards, and some readers find that frustrating. Those readers are looking for a plot delivery system. What French is doing is closer to a sustained performance, and it moves at the speed of psychological revelation, not procedural efficiency.

Writing Without a Map

French has been open about her relationship with outlining, and the short version is that she doesn't rely on one. She's described herself in interviews as a discovery writer, someone who finds out what happens by writing toward it. This is a more complicated position in crime fiction than it is in literary fiction, because a mystery novel has structural obligations. There's a crime. Someone committed it. The reader expects, eventually, to know who. Writing into the dark when your plot depends on a hidden solution requires a particular kind of trust in the process.

What she's talked about is starting with character rather than puzzle. In a 2017 interview with The New Yorker, she discussed how a novel typically begins for her with a person she finds interesting, someone whose psychology contains an unresolved tension, and a situation that will put maximum pressure on exactly that tension. The crime is designed to crack the character open, to force them into choices that reveal who they really are. The plot, in that sense, grows out of character rather than being imposed on it.

This is why each book in the Dublin Murder Squad series centers on a different detective. French isn't building a franchise around a recurring hero. She's asking a different psychological question each time, which requires a different narrator whose particular damage or strength makes that question answerable. Cassie can't carry Faithful Place. Frank Mackey can't carry The Likeness. The character isn't swappable because the character is the investigation. French figured this out early and structured an entire series around it, which is an architectural decision that looks obvious in retrospect and is genuinely unusual in genre publishing.

The practical consequence of discovery drafting is that her books take a long time. French has spoken about writing drafts that don't survive, chapters that get cut because they led somewhere wrong, entire structural approaches that had to be abandoned. That's the cost of not knowing the ending when you start. The benefit is that the books feel genuinely lived in rather than assembled. You can't fake that quality by outlining in advance, at least French can't, and she seems to have made peace with the slower, messier alternative.

The Literary Pace Inside a Commercial Form

One of the more interesting things about French's career is where she's positioned herself in the market. She's published by Viking, she sells well, she wins awards including the Edgar and the Anthony, and she's considered a major figure in the crime genre. She's also writing books that are 150 pages longer than most genre novels, concerned less with plot mechanics than with the psychology of self-deception, and paced more like a Kazuo Ishiguro novel than a Lee Child one. That's an unusual position to hold sustainably, and it says something about how deliberately she's built it.

French has talked in interviews about reading broadly across literary fiction and crime fiction and finding the categories less useful than most publishing people claim. She's mentioned writers like John le Carré as models for what she's trying to do, books where the genre machinery serves a serious examination of how people operate under pressure, how institutions fail people, how the stories we tell ourselves about the past are almost never reliable. The Spy Who Came in from the Cold is a thriller, but it's doing something a typical thriller doesn't bother with.

What she's built, over eight books, is a body of work that earns its length by making every scene do psychological work. The slow chapters in a French novel are slow because the narrator is wrong about something important and the reader is beginning to see it. The pace is the argument. Speed it up and you lose the point.

There's also something worth saying about her relationship with difficulty. French's plots are demanding. In the Woods ends without resolving one of its central mysteries, a choice that generated genuine controversy among readers expecting genre satisfactions. The Witch Elm, a standalone about a man whose self-image is destroyed by a single event, is arguably her most formally ambitious book and also the one least likely to please readers who came for the Dublin Murder Squad. She makes those choices deliberately, and the willingness to disappoint some readers in service of what the book actually needs is the kind of confidence that comes from knowing your process, not your market.

Place as Character: Dublin

French was born in Vermont and grew up across several countries before eventually settling in Dublin, where she's lived for most of her adult life. This matters because the Dublin in her novels doesn't read like a backdrop or a postcard. It reads like something she knows from the inside, a city with its own social textures, class tensions, and particular atmosphere, the way weather and geography and history accumulate into a mood that shapes how people speak and what they avoid saying.

The woods at the edge of Dublin in In the Woods are not a generic setting. The specific dread they carry, a 1980s Irish childhood, kids disappearing, an investigation going nowhere, is rooted in how that era actually felt in that place. Faithful Place, the working-class Dublin street at the center of Frank Mackey's book, carries decades of social history in a few blocks. French doesn't explain the weight of these places. She trusts that the weight is there and writes as if you can feel it too.

She told NPR in a 2016 interview that Dublin gives her novels something she couldn't get anywhere else, a density of time, the sense of a city where history stays audible. "Every place has a story, but in Dublin the stories overlap and contradict each other," she said, in terms that capture why her settings feel so charged. You can take a genre quiz and get pointed toward crime fiction, but French's work reminds you that setting is a psychological instrument, not a genre requirement.

Her most recent novels have pushed at the edge of that Dublin specificity. The Searcher, published in 2020, moves to rural County Galway, a very different kind of Ireland, quieter and more isolated. It's worth watching whether that shift represents a broadening of her range or a one-time experiment, because the Dublin of the Murder Squad books feels less like a setting choice and more like a foundational condition of the series.

Tana French's Writing Routine in Practice

French has not described her daily routine with the same granular detail that some writers have. What she has described is a process, and the process is consistent enough across interviews to constitute a picture of how she actually works. She starts with a person rather than a problem. Before drafting begins, she builds a character who has a specific history, a specific set of defenses, and a specific blind spot, the thing they can't see about themselves that the novel will eventually make unavoidable. The crime scenario is then designed to target that blind spot directly.

She writes around family life in Dublin, fitting the work into the hours available rather than designing her day around a fixed writing schedule. That constraint shapes the pace of her output. Her novels come out every two to three years, which is slower than the typical genre pace but exactly in line with what it takes to write at her level of psychological depth without a rigid structure guiding you to the end. Every draft is a discovery, which means some drafts are discoveries that don't work, and you start again.

What she has talked about is the experience of inhabiting a narrator over the long course of a novel. She's described feeling, at various points, as if the character is making decisions she didn't plan. That experience, which sounds mystical and is actually just the result of having built a character fully enough that their logic becomes predictable to the author, is the goal she works toward. When Rob Ryan won't let her do certain things with the plot because he's not the kind of person who would do them, she's arrived at the level of character truth the book needs. The actor in her recognizes when she's found the performance.

Sources

The primary sources for French's craft approach are her published interviews. Her 2010 conversation with The Guardian covers the acting background and its influence on how she builds character. A 2017 profile in The New Yorker by John Colapinto, "The Murderous Dublin Detective Novel," goes into substantial detail on her process, her relationship with genre, and the structural logic behind the rotating Dublin Murder Squad narrators. NPR's Fresh Air has carried multiple interviews with French over the years, including a 2016 conversation around The Trespasser where she discusses Dublin as a setting and her resistance to outlining. Her publisher Viking's recorded interviews, available in various podcast formats, provide additional material on the standalone novels The Witch Elm and The Searcher.

What You Can Steal

French's method is one of the most transferable in contemporary literary crime fiction, particularly for writers who find plotting before drafting kills the work. A few things that translate directly:

  • Start with character before plot. Figure out what your narrator is wrong about, what they can't see in themselves, before you work out what happens. The plot should be designed to make that blind spot unavoidable, not the other way around.
  • Build subtext into every conversation your narrator reports. French's acting background taught her that what people say is almost never what they mean. Train yourself to hear both tracks, what's spoken and what's avoided, and your dialogue will carry twice the weight.
  • Let your setting have psychological weight. Place is character in French's work. A location should change what people do and how they feel about it, not just provide backdrop. When you write Dublin, write what Dublin does to people.
  • Trust discovery drafting if outlining kills your voice. The structural obligations of crime fiction are real, but French writes into them from the character out. The ending is earned by who the character is, not by a prebuilt puzzle solution. The process is slower, but the books can't be faked.
  • Let your narrator be wrong for the whole book. French's most powerful technique is the gap between what a narrator believes about themselves and what the reader gradually understands. You can't build that gap if you correct your narrator too soon. Stay inside their logic even when you can see what they're missing.
