Taylor Jenkins Reid's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 1, 2026 | 9 min read

Taylor Jenkins Reid writes from 8 AM to 6 PM, Monday through Friday, in Los Angeles. She sets a word count before she starts each day and she hits it. Her first drafts take four to eight weeks. Then she revises, and revises again, and sends the third draft to her agent. None of this sounds remarkable until you look at what the routine has produced: The Seven Husbands of Evelyn Hugo, Daisy Jones and the Six, Malibu Rising, Carrie Soto Is Back, five other novels before those. She's one of the most commercially successful literary novelists working today, and the system behind it is less mysterious than her readers tend to assume.

The routine matters because it punctures a particular myth about how emotionally charged fiction gets written. Reid's books feel like they came from somewhere fever-pitched and unplanned. The characters cry. The readers cry. The prose has the texture of something confessed rather than constructed. But none of that emerges from an unstructured creative fog. It comes from a woman who treats writing like a job with hours, who does months of research before she touches the draft, and who is willing, in her own words, to stare a bad first draft in the face and make it better.

The Job You Show Up To

Reid went full-time as a novelist in 2012, after a stretch that included casting work, desk jobs, and a stint teaching high school. When she made the shift, she didn't restructure her life around inspiration. She built a schedule and kept it. Eight to six, Monday through Friday, with a word count that drives each day's session. The structure looks like any other office job from the outside, and that's partly the point.

Most writers treat first drafts as the most protected part of the process, the hours when the real work happens. Reid's four-to-eight-week draft windows are intensely compressed for novels of the scope she writes. What that compression requires is showing up every day and producing, regardless of how the sentences feel. She's said the material is often bad when it first comes out. Her answer to that is to keep going anyway. The draft's job is to exist. Revision's job is to make it good.

There's something useful in how plainly she talks about this. A lot of writing advice circles around the idea that you need to believe in your work at the sentence level while you're producing it. Reid's version is essentially the opposite: acknowledge that the early output is a mess, commit to it anyway, and trust that the work of making it better is where the real craft lives. That's a more honest account of how novels actually get written, and it's the only version of the process that scales to ten books over fifteen years.

Months Before the First Page

The writing schedule is only half the story. Before Reid opens a draft, she spends several months in full research immersion, and the depth of that immersion is what separates her historical novels from books that merely gesture at their settings. For The Seven Husbands of Evelyn Hugo, she absorbed classic Hollywood history through books, films, and the podcast You Must Remember This, Karina Longworth's meticulous series on the scandals and hidden lives of Old Hollywood. Reid has said she listened to it repeatedly while writing, using it to stay inside the world.

For Daisy Jones and the Six, the research ran deeper. She spent close to a year listening to Bruce Springsteen, Tom Petty, Linda Ronstadt, Fleetwood Mac, and the Eagles. She read band biographies and rock documentaries, not to borrow plot points but to get the emotional texture right, the specific way a band in creative conflict sounds, the particular kind of pride and exhaustion that lives in road musicians. The novel's oral history format, which mimics Please Kill Me and the best music writing of the 1970s, only works because the research grounded every voice in something that felt earned.

For Atmosphere, her 2025 novel set partly in space, Reid traveled to Houston for a VIP tour of NASA's Johnson Space Center, walked through Mission Control, and toured a decommissioned space shuttle. She then worked with Paul Dye, a retired NASA flight director and test pilot, to work out the technical specifics of plausible space disasters. A science consultant verified character details with active astronauts. The book took eighteen months in total, with the majority of that time devoted to research rather than drafting. The writing itself, when she finally started, went quickly. That's always been her model: understand the world completely before you describe a single inch of it.

Beginning and End, Then Nothing

Reid has a specific structural starting point that she's described in multiple interviews: she knows how the story opens and she knows how it ends, but she has no map of what happens between those two points. She starts the draft and finds out. This sounds like pure pantsing, but it's operating inside a framework. The months of research mean the world is fully real before she starts. The characters, settings, and historical contexts are already locked. What she's discovering through the draft is the emotional architecture, the shape of the human story that moves through the world she's already built.

This approach shows up in the structure of her books in ways that aren't always obvious. The Seven Husbands of Evelyn Hugo has an ending that feels absolutely inevitable in retrospect, the kind of conclusion that seems like it could only have been planned from page one. But Reid has been clear that the middle was written into existence rather than plotted out. The inevitability comes from the characters' internal logic, which she understood before she started, not from a beat sheet. When she talks about starting with a moral dilemma and a character placed under pressure, she's describing a different kind of outlining, one built from psychology rather than plot.

The practical implication for writers is worth sitting with. Reid's version of "I know the beginning and the end" is more useful than a full outline because it keeps the middle alive. A complete outline can drain the urgency from a draft because you're executing rather than discovering. Two fixed points, beginning and end, give you enough structure to work toward without telling you everything that will happen before you get there. That tension, between knowing where you're going and not knowing how you'll get there, seems to be where her best scenes come from.

The Atmosphere Test

There's a question Reid has described using to evaluate whether an idea is worth a year or more of her life: can she imagine spending twelve months or longer researching this world and still finding it interesting? If yes, she's found her next book. If the research starts to feel like obligation rather than appetite, the idea isn't right yet. She's described this as her primary filter, more important than commercial considerations or even whether she has a clear story to tell.

The filter explains a lot about how her catalog holds together. Every major Reid novel exists inside a specific, richly textured world: classic Hollywood in the 1950s and 60s, the Los Angeles rock scene of the 1970s, the Malibu surf culture of the same era, professional tennis in the 1990s, deep-space mission operations. These worlds aren't backdrops. They're the books' load-bearing walls. The emotional stories her characters live through only work because the historical and cultural context is so completely inhabited. A reader finishes Daisy Jones feeling like they understand something about how rock bands form and break that they didn't understand before. That's not background texture. That's the result of a writer who spent a year inside a subject before she wrote a sentence about it.

The test also explains why her books don't blur into each other the way some commercially successful authors' books do. She's not returning to the same world with different character names. Each novel requires building a new research base from scratch, which means each one has its own specific weight and smell and sound. The atmosphere test, applied honestly and rigorously before the work begins, is what keeps the catalog from becoming a formula.

Taylor Jenkins Reid's Daily Writing Routine

The Taylor Jenkins Reid writing routine is built on a few mechanics that work together: a fixed daily schedule, a non-negotiable word count, and a research phase long enough that the fictional world is completely real before she starts drafting. She works 8 AM to 6 PM, Monday through Friday, which makes her writing life look more like a traditional job than the image most readers have of a novelist. There's no night-owl drafting or writing through weekends. The hours are regular and the process is repeatable.

The daily word count is the engine. Reid has said she knows she can usually predict when a draft will be finished before she starts it, which is only possible if the output per day is consistent. Four to eight weeks is a tight window for a full novel draft, and hitting it repeatedly across ten books means the word count discipline has never slipped. The first-draft material isn't expected to be good. She's said directly that a lot of it is garbage coming out. What matters is that it exists, because a draft you can revise is worth more than a perfect blank page.

The thematic playlists are a smaller piece of the system but not a trivial one. Reid builds a playlist for each book and plays it throughout the writing and revision phase, using the music as a way to stay inside the emotional register of the story. For Daisy Jones, the playlist was 1970s rock, which meant she was hearing the world of the novel every time she sat down to describe it. For Atmosphere, she used David Bowie, Indigo Girls, and Brandi Carlile, artists whose work carries a particular kind of longing and scale that matched what she was after. The music functions as a ritual threshold, the same way Morrison's coffee and window light did: a signal to the brain that the writing state is beginning.

What You Can Steal

Reid's process is particularly useful for writers who want to produce emotionally intense work without waiting for the right emotional state to arrive. Here's what actually translates:

  • Treat the schedule like a job. Fixed hours, Monday through Friday, with a daily word count. You don't need to feel ready. You need to show up at the same time each day and produce until the number is hit.
  • Separate research from drafting completely. Spend months understanding the world before you write a sentence of prose. When the world is fully real to you, the first draft moves faster and the details are specific enough to feel true.
  • Use two fixed points, not a full map. Know where the story starts and where it ends. Let the draft discover the middle. The discovery keeps the writing alive in a way that executing a beat sheet often doesn't.
  • Build a playlist for the book. Music that matches the emotional register of the story, played consistently throughout the writing phase, is a cheap and effective way to re-enter the world each session without spending the first twenty minutes getting oriented.
  • Accept that first drafts are bad. The willingness to write badly is what makes a writing routine durable. Waiting to write until you're sure it'll be good is how people go years without finishing anything. Write the garbage. Revise the garbage later.
