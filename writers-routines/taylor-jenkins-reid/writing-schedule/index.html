What Is Taylor Jenkins Reid's Writing Schedule?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Taylor Jenkins Reid doesn't wait around for a mood to write in. She's described her schedule as something close to a regular job, and her process as one where she genuinely doesn't know what's coming next in the story until she gets there.

The Direct Answer
She works a set weekday schedule and discovers the story as she writes it, without an outline.
In interviews she's described working Monday through Friday, roughly from morning into evening, and starting each day's session not knowing exactly what happens next.

A Workweek, Not a Muse

Reid has talked about treating writing less like a mysterious creative act and more like a job with regular hours. In interviews about her process, she's described working something close to a Monday-through-Friday week, sitting down in the morning and staying at it into the evening, the way you'd show up for any other job. The point isn't a rigid clock-in time so much as the discipline behind it: she doesn't wait for inspiration before she sits down, she sits down and the work happens because she's there for it.

That framing matters because her novels are long and structurally ambitious, whether that's the multi-voice oral history of Daisy Jones & The Six or the decades-spanning story of The Seven Husbands of Evelyn Hugo. Books built like that don't get finished on inspiration alone. They get finished by someone returning to the desk on a schedule, day after day, whether or not the writing feels good that morning.

She Doesn't Outline

One of the more specific things Reid has said about her own process is that she doesn't outline, and that she thinks outlining is a mistake a lot of writers make. Instead of mapping the plot before she starts, she typically begins with an interesting character and a moral dilemma, drops that character into a difficult situation, and then writes forward to find out how they respond. She's described waking up some mornings without knowing what's about to happen in the manuscript, and figuring it out at the keyboard the same way a reader would.

That's a real risk for a writer with her level of structural ambition. A book told entirely through interview transcripts, or one that jumps across decades of a character's life, has a lot of moving parts to track without a map. Her answer to that risk isn't more planning up front. It's showing up consistently enough that the discovery process has time to work itself out over a full draft, then revising hard once the shape of the book is on the page.

The research she does before drafting gives that discovery process something solid to push against, since she already knows the real world her invented characters are moving through. You can read more about how that research shapes her books in the full Taylor Jenkins Reid writing routine.

The Schedule Behind a Fast Backlist

Whatever the exact hours look like on a given day, the results speak to how consistent the underlying schedule has been. Reid has released a run of bestselling novels in fairly close succession, including The Seven Husbands of Evelyn Hugo, Daisy Jones & The Six, Malibu Rising, and Carrie Soto Is Back, each requiring its own research pass and its own structural puzzle to solve. That pace doesn't happen on inspiration alone. It happens because the desk time is scheduled in, book after book, rather than treated as something that shows up when it feels like it.

What You Can Steal

  • Treat writing time like a job, not a mood. Set weekday hours and show up whether or not you feel ready.
  • Try discovery writing on a smaller scene first. Start with a character and a hard situation, and write forward to see how they react before you decide the whole plot in advance.
  • Let structure emerge from research, not from an outline. If you already know the real world your story sits in, you need less of a map to explore it safely.
  • Save the heavy planning for revision. Discover the story in the first draft, then use what you learn to restructure once you can see the whole thing.
