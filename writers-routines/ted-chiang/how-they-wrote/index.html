How Does Ted Chiang Write His Stories?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Ted Chiang has published fewer than twenty stories in more than three decades of writing science fiction. That's not because he ran out of ideas or hit a wall. It's the result of a process that refuses to rush, backed by a day job that lets him ignore deadlines entirely. Here's how it actually works.

The Direct Answer
Ted Chiang writes slowly and rarely because he won't start a story until he knows how it ends. An idea has to sit with him, sometimes for years, before he trusts himself to draft it, and he sets aside anything that doesn't fully cohere rather than force it into a shape it doesn't want.
He also works full time as a technical writer, so nothing about his income depends on how many stories he finishes in a given year.

A Career Measured in Single Digits Per Decade

Chiang's entire bibliography fits into two collections: Stories of Your Life and Others in 2002, and Exhalation in 2019. Between them, he's published fewer than twenty stories since his first professional sales in the late 1980s. Compare that to a working novelist who might put out a book a year, and the gap looks less like slowness and more like a different relationship to output altogether.

He's said plainly in interviews that he's just a slow writer, and that writing is hard enough for him that an idea has to be genuinely compelling before he'll commit the time it requires. That's a low bar in theory and a high one in practice, since most premises that occur to him don't clear it.

He Won't Start Until He Knows the Ending

Most fiction writers discover the ending somewhere in the act of drafting. Chiang works the other way. He treats the ending, and the idea's full implications, as something he needs to understand before he writes a single scene. If he doesn't know where a story lands, he doesn't start it.

The clearest example is 'Story of Your Life,' the novella that became the film Arrival. Chiang began writing it, realized the premise depended on ideas about language he didn't understand well enough yet, and put the draft away. He spent around four years reading into linguistics before he came back and finished it. That's not writer's block. It's a writer refusing to fake competence he hadn't earned.

The Day Job That Buys Him Patience

Chiang has worked as a technical writer in the software industry for most of his adult life, since studying computer science at Brown. He's said he doesn't want fiction to be his full-time job, because writing full time would mean producing at a pace his ideas can't support. The paycheck comes from documentation, not stories, so nothing forces a Chiang story into print before it's ready.

What You Can Steal

  • Don't start drafting until you know your ending. Wandering into a first draft without one isn't always wrong, but if you're stuck, ask whether you actually know where the story is going.
  • Let an idea fail privately instead of finishing it publicly. Chiang sets aside stories that don't cohere. That's not quitting. It's refusing to publish something half-understood.
  • Research the parts you don't understand before you write them. Four years of reading isn't realistic on most deadlines, but a week spent learning the thing your story depends on almost always is.
  • A day job isn't automatically the enemy of your writing. If it buys you patience instead of eating all your time, it might be doing more for your work than a full-time writing schedule would.
