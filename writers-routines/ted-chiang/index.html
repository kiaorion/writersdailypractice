Ted Chiang's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 23, 2026 | 9 min read

Ted Chiang has published fewer than twenty short stories across three decades. He's won four Hugo Awards and four Nebula Awards, his novella "Story of Your Life" became the Denis Villeneuve film Arrival, and he's regarded by a large portion of science fiction writers as the best in the genre alive. He also works as a technical writer to pay his bills and has never published a novel. There's a version of this story where Chiang is a tragic underachiever who never reached his potential. That version gets it completely wrong.

The routine here is one of the most instructive in contemporary fiction precisely because it upends the standard productivity logic around writing. Chiang writes slowly, rarely, and with enormous patience for the gap between having an idea and understanding it well enough to write it. What he produces in those gaps is work that lasts. The routine is worth studying because it shows what happens when a writer refuses to let the pressure of output override the requirements of the work.

Profile

The Routine at a Glance

Wake Time
Undisclosed. Chiang keeps his personal daily schedule private, partly because so much of the work happens in thinking rather than at a desk.
Writing Location
At home, around his day job schedule as a technical writer in the Seattle area. The fiction writing fits into evenings and weekends rather than a dedicated morning block.
Daily Output
Extremely low by any conventional measure. Stories arrive at a rate of roughly one every one to two years. Chiang has described this as reflecting the time needed to develop an idea fully, not as writer's block.
Tools
Computer. His process is conceptual and research-heavy before a word of prose gets written, so the tools for drafting matter less than the tools for thinking.
Famous Ritual
Knowing the ending before writing the first sentence. Chiang has said in multiple interviews that he won't begin writing until he knows where the story is going and what it's trying to say.
Books Written This Way
Stories of Your Life and Others (2002), Exhalation: Stories (2019). Together, seventeen stories over thirty-plus years of publishing.

The Day Job Is Not a Compromise

Chiang has worked as a technical writer in the software industry for most of his adult life. He graduated from Brown University with a degree in computer science in 1990, and by the early nineties he was writing software documentation for a living while publishing short fiction on the side. He's continued doing both ever since. In interviews he's explained that he doesn't want to be a full-time fiction writer, not because full-time writing is unappealing, but because he writes so slowly and at such low volume that the economics of professional fiction would require him to produce work he isn't ready to produce.

This is a more honest accounting of the relationship between commercial pressure and creative quality than most writers are willing to make publicly. The standard narrative is that the day job is a temporary obstacle you endure until your writing income lets you quit. Chiang inverts it. The day job is the thing that lets him write exactly as slowly as the work demands, without any financial reason to rush a story into print before it's ready. Two collections over three decades, both of them extraordinary, both of them free of the filler that pads out books written under contract pressure.

What the technical writing gives him is also worth noting. Writing documentation for software is a discipline in clarity and precision, in finding exact language for complex systems, in not letting ambiguity slide because you got tired of looking for the right word. Those are the same skills that make his fiction feel the way it does. You read a Chiang sentence and you trust it completely. The precision isn't decorative. It comes from someone who's spent decades getting paid to be exact.

For writers who imagine their creative work and their paying work as fundamentally opposed, Chiang's career is worth sitting with for a while. The day job didn't shrink his fiction. In some ways it shaped it. That's not a universal truth, but it's true enough to complicate the standard story about what a serious writer's life has to look like.

Starting from the Idea and Knowing the End

Every Ted Chiang story begins with a philosophical or scientific premise that he takes seriously as a question. What would it mean if we could perceive time non-linearly? What are the actual implications of a universe that operates on the principle of least action? What happens to human identity if memory is perfectly reliable and perfectly searchable? These aren't premises that exist to serve a plot. For Chiang, the premise is the story's entire reason for existing, and the narrative is the vehicle that lets him think through it at full depth.

In a 2019 interview with The New Yorker's Joshua Rothman, Chiang described his process as starting from an idea and then asking what story would best illuminate that idea. He's said this in different forms in interviews with Locus and Lightspeed as well. The story structure, the characters, the emotional arc, these all emerge from what the idea requires to be fully explored. A different writer might find a character first and ask what ideas that character might embody. Chiang works the opposite direction, and you can feel it in how his fiction reads: the ideas aren't ornamental, they're load-bearing, and the emotional power lands only because the intellectual structure is sound.

The corollary to starting from the idea is that he won't write the story until he understands where it ends. This is a harder discipline than it sounds. Most writers begin with an image or a voice or a situation and discover the ending in the act of writing. That discovery process is generative and it works. Chiang doesn't trust it, at least not for the kind of fiction he makes. His stories are built around ideas that have specific logical conclusions, and if he doesn't know what that conclusion is before he starts, he risks writing himself into a cul-de-sac that required knowing the destination all along. He's talked about stories he put down for years, sometimes for a decade or more, because he had the premise but hadn't yet figured out what the story was actually saying. He waited until he knew.

For writers interested in science fiction writing, Chiang's method is a useful counterweight to the idea that genre fiction should prioritize plot mechanics above all else. His stories have plots, but they're never what you remember. You remember the idea. The plot is the means of delivering it at emotional intensity.

A Small Body of Work, Done Completely

A story published before it's ready is just a draft that escaped.

Chiang has published seventeen stories total as of his second collection, Exhalation: Stories, which came out in 2019. Both collections together run to a few hundred pages. By the standards of a productive novelist, that's maybe two years of output. Across thirty-plus years, it's a catalog so small that some writers would find it quietly humiliating. Chiang seems entirely at peace with it.

When you read both collections in sequence, the smallness starts to feel like one of its most significant qualities. There's no padding. There are no second-tier stories that exist because a contract required them, no work that feels like Chiang going through the motions. Every story is operating at full intensity, every premise is developed to its actual conclusion, and the emotional weight in pieces like "Story of Your Life," "Hell Is the Absence of God," and the title story of the second collection is the kind of thing that only happens when a writer didn't shortcut any part of the process.

The comparison to other writers in the field is instructive. Contemporary science fiction has many writers who publish a novel a year, sometimes more. Some of them are very good. But you'd be hard-pressed to find another living writer in the genre whose entire output, every story they've ever published, is operating at the level Chiang consistently hits. Quality and quantity aren't always in opposition, but they were for him, and he chose quality without apparent regret. That's rarer than it sounds.

The Patience to Not Publish Until It's Right

Chiang has talked in interviews, including a conversation with Lightspeed Magazine's John Joseph Adams, about the long gestation times some of his stories require. He described having a premise years before writing the story, sitting with the idea until the narrative form that would best serve it became clear. This isn't unusual among literary writers but it's less common in commercial genre fiction, where the pressure to publish continuously is more acute. Chiang ignores that pressure systematically.

What this patience produces, in practical terms, is stories with no loose ends. His premises are fully interrogated. The implications that a faster writer would miss, the corner cases that would expose a shallow treatment of the idea, the emotional registers that only open up after you've sat with a premise long enough, these are all present in his work because he didn't start writing until they were present in his thinking. The story "Exhalation," which won both the Hugo and Nebula for best short story in 2009, is a meditation on entropy and self-knowledge told from the perspective of a being whose universe is running down. Getting that right, getting the science and the philosophy and the emotional resonance to land simultaneously, required being in no hurry at all.

There's a broader argument here about the function of patience in creative work that I think applies well beyond Chiang's specific method. Writers talk a lot about showing up daily, and that advice is right for most situations, most of the time. But showing up daily doesn't mean publishing daily, or even drafting daily. Chiang's routine suggests a different use of the daily showing-up: sitting with the problem, letting the idea develop in the background, doing the thinking before the writing starts. If you take the genre quiz and land somewhere in speculative or idea-driven fiction, the question worth asking isn't just when you'll write but whether you've thought long enough to write something worth reading.

The patience also shows in how Chiang handles revision. He's described treating the first draft as one stage in a longer process of arriving at the story's final form. He revises until the work is saying what it needs to say, not until a deadline makes him stop. Technical writing gave him this habit too: documentation that ships with errors costs money, so you check it until it's right. Fiction that ships with wrong ideas costs something harder to name.

Sources

The primary source for Chiang's working method and his views on the relationship between premise and story is Joshua Rothman's profile in The New Yorker, "The Story of Ted Chiang" (2019), which covers his background, his day job, and the way he generates and develops ideas. John Joseph Adams's interviews with Chiang published in Lightspeed Magazine over multiple years are detailed conversations about craft, process, and the specific development of individual stories. Locus Magazine has published several interviews with Chiang across the years following his major awards; the 2002 and 2019 interviews in particular address his slow output, his day job, and his approach to ideas. The afterword notes in Stories of Your Life and Others and Exhalation: Stories are also worth reading as Chiang's own account of how each piece was conceived and developed.

What You Can Steal

Chiang's routine doesn't translate directly into most writers' lives because it runs at a pace most writers won't sustain. What does translate is the underlying logic:

  • Know what you're trying to say before you write it. Chiang won't begin a story without knowing the ending and the idea's conclusion. That discipline stops a lot of the wandering that wastes drafts.
  • Let a premise develop before you write it. Sitting with an idea for weeks or months isn't procrastination if you're genuinely thinking. Some stories take longer to understand than others. Rushing the understanding doesn't speed up the story; it just makes the story shallower.
  • The day job doesn't have to be the enemy of the writing. If the day job removes financial pressure to publish before you're ready, it might be doing your writing more good than harm. The question is whether it gives you time to write at all, which is a different problem.
  • Measure success by the quality of what survives, not the volume produced. Chiang's two collections will be read for as long as science fiction is read. The writers who published twenty novels in the same period have a longer list, but it's worth asking which list you'd rather have attached to your name.
  • Start from the idea, not from the plot. If you work in idea-driven fiction of any kind, ask what question the story is actually trying to answer. Then ask whether the narrative you're building is the best possible vehicle for that answer. If it isn't, the story probably needs more thinking before it needs more writing.
