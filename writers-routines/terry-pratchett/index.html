Terry Pratchett's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | June 1, 2026 | 10 min read

Terry Pratchett wrote 1,000 words every morning before he did anything else. No exceptions, no negotiation, no waiting for the right mood. He sat down, he wrote his thousand words, and then he went on with his day. That single habit, held without much ceremony for four decades, produced 41 Discworld novels, several standalone works, and a collaboration with Neil Gaiman that became Good Omens in 1990. The numbers are staggering by any standard. He was, in terms of sheer completed output, one of the most productive serious writers the English language has ever seen.

What made the routine worth studying was the philosophy behind it. Pratchett treated writing the way a tradesman treats work. You show up on schedule, you do the job, you go home. He had very little patience for the romantic version of the writer's life, the one where inspiration arrives on its own schedule and the artist waits in suffering for the muse to appear. He said writing daily was like brushing your teeth. You don't evaluate your emotional readiness first. You just do it. That framing sounds blunt until you clock what it actually enabled: over forty years of morning sessions that produced roughly 400,000 words a year, year after year, through a day job, through fame, through a knighthood in 2009, and eventually through a disease that took the ability to type and forced a complete reinvention of how the work got done.

Tools
Computer for most of his career. Dragon NaturallySpeaking speech-to-text software from 2007 onward, after his fingers could no longer reliably type.

Treating writing like a job rather than an art: showing up on schedule, hitting the daily target, and stopping. He compared the discipline to brushing your teeth.
Books Written This Way
All 41 Discworld novels, Good Omens (with Neil Gaiman), Nation, The Long Earth series (with Stephen Baxter), and his memoir A Slip of the Keyboard.

The Tradesman's Philosophy

Pratchett worked as a journalist and then as a press officer for the Central Electricity Generating Board for years before writing full time. That background shaped everything about how he thought about the job. Journalists file whether or not they feel creative. Press officers meet deadlines because missing them has consequences. The writing life he built after going full time in the mid-1980s kept that structure because he'd seen what happened when writers treated the work as something that required the right conditions: mostly, they didn't write much.

The 1,000-word daily target worked partly because it was achievable. A thousand words is about four pages. Most mornings, Pratchett could hit that in two to three hours and still have the afternoon for everything else. He wasn't grinding through twelve-hour sessions or trying to will an entire chapter into existence before lunch. The target was conservative enough that bad days were still good enough, and good days produced a surplus he could bank. At his rate, a Discworld novel of 100,000 words took roughly four months of mornings. He published anywhere from one to three books a year during his most prolific decades.

There's a quiet argument embedded in this approach that most writing advice skips past. Pratchett's output suggests the daily writing session is primarily about keeping the story warm, not producing great sentences. When you leave a manuscript alone for a week, you come back cold. You've lost the texture of what you were building, the specific rhythms your characters had developed, the half-formed ideas that were accumulating just below the surface. Daily contact keeps all of that alive. The novel stays in your head even when you're not at the desk, and that background processing is where a lot of the actual work gets done.

What 400 Words a Day After Alzheimer's Actually Means

In December 2007, Pratchett announced that he had been diagnosed with posterior cortical atrophy, a rare variant of early-onset Alzheimer's that initially affects the visual cortex and fine motor control rather than memory. He was 59. His typing became unreliable before much else did, and rather than slow down or stop, he switched to Dragon NaturallySpeaking and kept writing. The daily output dropped from 1,000 words to around 400. He kept going.

That detail deserves more weight than it usually gets. Pratchett dictated several more Discworld novels after the diagnosis, including Unseen Academicals (2009), I Shall Wear Midnight (2010), Snuff (2011), Raising Steam (2013), and The Shepherd's Crown (2015), which was published posthumously after his death in March of that year. Some critics argued the late books showed signs of the illness. That's probably true. What's also true is that they exist, and that they were made by a man writing 400 words at a time through a disease that was systematically dismantling the tools he'd spent his life developing.

The shift to dictation changed the prose in ways that are audible if you read the late books closely. Speech-to-text encourages longer, more discursive sentences. The compression that came naturally from typing has less room in dictation. Pratchett worked with assistants to help shape the transcripts, and The Shepherd's Crown was edited after his death by his longtime editor and collaborator. The process was different from anything he'd done before. The commitment to producing the work daily, however, was exactly the same.

He campaigned publicly for Alzheimer's research funding throughout this period, donating a million dollars of his own money to the Alzheimer's Research Trust in 2008, and he became an outspoken advocate for assisted dying rights. All of this happened alongside the writing. The books kept coming because the habit of the books was older and deeper than the diagnosis. It had been built into his days long enough that it continued by its own momentum even as the capacity for it diminished.

The Journalist Who Became a Fantasist

Pratchett's pre-Discworld career is worth understanding because it explains the work ethic. He started writing for local newspapers as a teenager in the early 1960s, became a journalist at 17, and then spent years working in PR for the Central Electricity Generating Board. That's not a glamorous CV for one of the most beloved English novelists of the twentieth century. There's no Oxbridge fellowship in it, no MFA, no years of carefully funded artistic development. He learned to write on deadline, about subjects he hadn't chosen, for audiences he hadn't picked.

The first Discworld novel, The Colour of Magic, came out in 1983. Pratchett was 35 and still working his day job. He went full time as a writer in 1987, after several Discworld novels had established that the series could support him. By then he'd been producing fiction in the margins of a working life for years, which meant the habits that would later generate 41 novels were already in place before he had the luxury of a whole day to write in. When the day job disappeared, the morning writing session didn't change. He just kept the same hours and the same targets and let the available time accumulate around them.

The journalism background shows up in the prose in ways that distinguish Pratchett from almost every other major fantasy writer of his generation. His sentences move. He doesn't linger. The satire lands because the observations are sharp and quickly made, like a columnist who knows he has 800 words and can't afford to waste any of them. Tolkien took years between books and filled them with extensive secondary-world construction. Pratchett wrote one to two books a year and kept the philosophy lean, giving you just enough Discworld to understand the joke and then moving you along. That velocity came from spending a decade writing on deadline before he wrote fiction full time.

Discworld as a System, Not a Series

Forty-one novels over forty years is a different kind of creative achievement from writing one great book or even a strong trilogy. Pratchett was building something closer to a system, a fictional universe with its own internal consistency, recurring characters, and evolving concerns, all of it produced one thousand words at a time every morning for decades. The daily habit made this possible in a way that binge-writing sessions couldn't have. You can't hold a world like Discworld in your head across six-week gaps. The routine was what kept the continuity alive.

The Discworld books get better as they go. The first few are fun parodies, clever enough, but the books from roughly Guards! Guards! (1989) onward develop a moral seriousness that the early books don't have. Sam Vimes, Granny Weatherwax, Death, the witches, the Watch: these characters accumulate weight across dozens of books because Pratchett spent every morning with them for years and understood them the way you only understand someone you've spent a lot of time with. The routine that looks like productivity discipline was also a form of deep character development, sustained over a much longer period than most novelists ever attempt.

He was also writing about real things. Pratchett used fantasy the way all the best satirists use their chosen form: to say true things about power, death, religion, and human behavior that would be less effective if said plainly. Small Gods is a serious examination of religious fanaticism. Monstrous Regiment is about gender and war. Going Postal is about capitalism and institutional failure. None of these books reads like a political essay, but all of them carry a sustained argument. Writing a thousand words a day in the same fictional universe for decades gave Pratchett the space to work out ideas at length that most novelists would have compressed into one or two books.

Sources

The primary sources for Pratchett's writing habits are scattered across decades of interviews. His essay collection A Slip of the Keyboard: Collected Nonfiction (2014) contains direct reflections on craft, the writing life, and his Alzheimer's diagnosis. The Guardian's interview archive includes several profiles across his career where he discussed the daily word count and his tradesman's attitude to the work. Marc Burrows' biography The Magic of Terry Pratchett (2020) provides the most comprehensive account of his working methods. His 2008 Richard Dimbleby Lecture, "Shaking Hands with Death," and his Daily Mail interview announcing his diagnosis in 2007 are key sources for the post-diagnosis period. His Alzheimer's Research Trust donation and advocacy work are documented in contemporaneous press coverage. See also the extensive fan archive at Terry Pratchett on Wikipedia.

What You Can Steal

Pratchett's routine translates well for writers at almost any level, and the lessons in it are more practical than most.

  • Pick a daily target that's actually achievable. A thousand words is four pages. Most writers can do that in two hours on a bad day. The number should be low enough that you hit it even when you're tired, because a habit that only works when you feel good isn't really a habit.
  • Treat writing like a job, not a mood. Pratchett's tradesman framing sounds unromantic, but it's what allowed him to keep going through an Alzheimer's diagnosis that would have given most writers a very understandable reason to stop. The work wasn't contingent on feeling like a writer.
  • Write in the same universe long enough to understand it deeply. The Discworld books got better as they went because daily contact with those characters over years produced a depth of understanding that can't be rushed. If you have a world worth staying in, stay in it.
  • Adapt the tool, keep the target. When Pratchett could no longer type reliably, he switched to dictation and wrote 400 words a day instead of 1,000. The form changed; the commitment to daily output didn't. When your circumstances shift, adjust the method before you abandon the practice.
  • Use your day job's discipline. Pratchett's journalism background gave him a relationship to deadlines and consistent output that most MFA-trained writers don't have. Any work experience where you had to produce on schedule is transferable. The muscle memory of meeting a deadline carries over.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many words did Terry Pratchett write per day?

Pratchett wrote 1,000 words a day as his standard floor. After his Alzheimer's diagnosis in December 2007, the daily output dropped to around 400 words, produced via Dragon NaturallySpeaking speech-to-text software. He kept writing daily until close to the end of his life in March 2015.

Did Terry Pratchett write every day?

Yes. He treated writing the way a tradesman treats work: you show up, you do the job, you don't wait for inspiration. He compared the discipline to brushing your teeth. The 41 Discworld novels are the output of that kind of consistency, held for four decades.

How did Terry Pratchett write after his Alzheimer's diagnosis?

After being diagnosed with posterior cortical atrophy, a rare form of early-onset Alzheimer's, in December 2007, Pratchett switched to Dragon NaturallySpeaking and dictated his novels rather than typing them. He produced five more Discworld novels after the diagnosis, the last of which, The Shepherd's Crown, was published posthumously in 2015.

What is Terry Pratchett's most famous writing quote?

"Writing is the most fun you can have by yourself." He also frequently said that the first draft is just you telling yourself the story, which captures his no-mysticism view of the craft: get it down first, then shape it. Both quotes appear across various interviews and his essay collection A Slip of the Keyboard.
