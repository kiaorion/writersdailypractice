Where Did Virginia Woolf Write?

Kia Orion | June 28, 2026 | 5 min read

Woolf wrote her later novels in a small wooden lodge at the bottom of the garden at Monk's House, her country home in Rodmell, Sussex. It was a converted outbuilding with a view across the orchard, and it gave her exactly the separation from the household she needed.

The Direct Answer
In a writing lodge at the bottom of the garden at Monk's House, Rodmell, Sussex.
The lodge stood in the orchard with views toward Mount Caburn, and she wrote parts of her major novels there, as documented by the National Trust, which now runs the house.

A Room at the End of the Garden

Monk's House was the Woolfs' country home from 1919 onward, a modest weatherboarded cottage in the village of Rodmell that Virginia ran with Leonard. Her workroom wasn't inside it. She wrote in a separate lodge down in the garden, away from the noise of the house, where she could close the door and disappear into the morning's work. The short walk from the cottage to the lodge functioned as a kind of threshold, a few steps that marked the move from domestic life into the writing day.

The lodge began as a humble outbuilding and changed over the years; an earlier garden room was converted from an old tool store before the arrangement settled. By the mid-1930s a new lodge stood in the orchard, repositioned so she could look out across the Sussex countryside toward Mount Caburn and the village church. The setting wasn't decoration. The downs she could see from the door were the same ones she walked in the afternoons, so the view from her desk and the ground under her feet later in the day were continuous.

It was a plain space, not a grand study, and that was the point. A small wooden room with a door she could shut gave her exactly the privacy and quiet she needed to work, without the formality that might have made the writing feel ceremonial. The house is now cared for by the National Trust, and the lodge has been preserved at the foot of the garden, so visitors can still stand roughly where she stood and see the orchard she looked out on.

What She Wrote There

Monk's House is where much of her best-known fiction took shape. She wrote parts of her major novels there, among them Mrs Dalloway, To the Lighthouse, and The Waves, on through to her last novel Between the Acts. London was where the Woolfs spent much of the year and ran the Hogarth Press, but the Sussex lodge became the place most associated with her deepest concentration, the room she returned to season after season.

The idea of a private, separate room for a woman to work in wasn't abstract for her. She'd argued for it directly, in the book whose title has outlasted almost everything else she wrote, and the lodge at the end of the garden was that argument made literal: her own door, her own quiet, her own view.

A woman must have money and a room of her own if she is to write fiction.

Virginia Woolf, A Room of One's Own

What You Can Steal

  • Put physical distance between you and the household. A separate room, even a shed, signals that work has begun.
  • Pick a spot with a view you like. Woolf's looked out over the orchard and the downs she walked.
  • You don't need a grand study. Hers was a converted outbuilding, plain and small.
  • Keep the writing space single-purpose. The lodge was for work, which made it easier to drop into.
