Will Wight's Writing Routine

Kia Orion | July 19, 2026 | 8 min read

In 2013, Will Wight self-published a novel called House of Blades while finishing his MFA at the University of Central Florida, hoping it might bring in enough to cover rent. It did more than that. Within about a year he was supporting himself as a full-time independent writer, and by 2016 he'd started the series that would make him one of the defining names in progression fantasy: Cradle, thirteen books that carried a damaged, disrespected young cultivator named Lindon from the bottom of his world's power system to the very top. The series has hit the New York Times bestseller list and topped Amazon's Kindle charts more than once, all without a traditional publisher's marketing department behind it.

What makes Wight worth studying is how openly he treats writing as a system he can inspect and adjust, more than the size of the audience he built. He talks about his process in blog posts, interviews, and livestreams the way a small business owner talks about production schedules. That candor is useful, because it means his routine is documented, in his own words, across more than a decade of public writing about writing, rather than left to guesswork.

Profile

The Routine at a Glance

Wake Time
Not something Wight has pinned down publicly to the hour. What he's said consistently, going back to his earliest interviews, is that the writing target comes before anything else in the day, not after.
Writing Location
A home office. Wight became a full-time independent writer in Florida shortly after House of Blades took off, following his B.A. and M.F.A. in creative writing at the University of Central Florida in Orlando.
Daily Output
He hits a word-count target before he allows himself downtime. He doesn't publish the number, but the pace it produces is remarkable: roughly two full novels a year, sustained for over a decade.
Tools
No dedicated outlining software or story bible. By his own account: "piles and piles of notes," kept in plain text files in the same folder as the manuscript itself.
Famous Ritual
Minimum-viable planning before drafting, then heavy revision after. He also livestreams on Twitch around each release, running spoiler sessions and live Q&As with readers the day a book goes live.
Books Written This Way
Traveler's Gate trilogy (2013-2014), Elder Empire (2015-2020), Cradle (2016-2025, 13 books), The Last Horizon (2023-present).

The Rent Check That Became a Career

Wight wrote House of Blades while working on his master's degree, and by his own account he hoped it would earn enough to help with rent, with no plan to build a publishing empire. Instead, it sold well enough that he could go full-time as an independent author within roughly a year, a timeline he's said exceeded anything he'd projected. He founded Hidden Gnome Publishing to handle the business side, and kept writing.

The MFA matters more to his process than it might seem. Wight has said his graduate coursework was integral to the structure he still uses to build his books, which puts him in an unusual position inside progression fantasy: a genre built largely by writers who came up through Royal Road and web serials now has one of its biggest names holding a formal degree in the craft. He carried that training straight into his indie career, using it to build systems in both the fiction and the business behind it.

That founding story is worth sitting with, because it explains something about how Wight approaches every book since. He's never treated writing as a hobby waiting to become a career. From the first sale, it was already a job with an income attached, and jobs need a repeatable process. What came next was him building one.

The Six-Month Book

Wight has described his working philosophy in five words: "I want to write the best six-month book I can." It's an acknowledgment of a constraint, not a boast about speed. Rather than chase an idealized, unhurried novel that might take years to perfect, he commits to a defined production window and does the best work he can inside it. The Cradle series, thirteen books across nine years, holds to something close to that pace: roughly two books annually, an output most traditionally published fantasy writers never approach.

The system isn't rigid, and Wight has been explicit about that too. In 2022, working simultaneously on a Kickstarter campaign, the launch of a new series, and Waybound, the final Cradle novel, he extended his usual timeline on purpose. "I really want both of these books to work," he said, choosing to take more time rather than force a release date he wasn't confident in. The six-month framework is a default, not a cage. It exists to keep him from either of the two failure modes indie writers know well: publishing too slowly to build an audience, or publishing so fast the books stop being good.

That balance matters more in progression fantasy than in most genres. Readers who follow a cultivation series expect a rhythm, chapter after chapter, book after book, and platforms like Royal Road and Kindle Unlimited reward writers who can sustain one. Wight built his production system to match the genre's actual demands, not some abstract idea of literary pacing borrowed from a different kind of book.

Plan Just Enough to Start

Wight's advice to newer writers keeps circling back to one question: "What is the minimum I need to know before I can start writing?" For him, that minimum is small: a one-or-two sentence plot summary, a general shape for the story, and whatever names and details he needs for the very next scene. Everything past that, he's argued, is planning disguised as procrastination. "Anything that happens in the first draft can be fixed later," he's written, comparing the process to pottery: the first draft is the lump of clay, not the finished piece.

That philosophy shows up in how he actually organizes a book. Despite building one of the more intricate power systems in the genre, cultivators advancing through named stages like Copper, Iron, Jade, and Gold, Wight has said his notes are informal: text files saved next to the manuscript, not a wiki or a dedicated worldbuilding database. He's admitted this makes lookups inefficient, joking about having to "shovel through a small mountain of notebooks" to check a character detail. He's considered building something more organized. He hasn't gotten around to it, and the books keep coming anyway.

The gap between the world's apparent complexity and the plainness of his actual notes is instructive. Readers assume an elaborate magic system requires an elaborate bible behind it. Wight's practice suggests the opposite: a loose, searchable pile of notes, revisited constantly, beats a beautiful system nobody has time to maintain. The architecture lives in the books themselves, refined draft over draft, not in a separate document that has to be kept perfectly in sync with them.

Writing in Public

Because Wight publishes independently, he doesn't have a marketing department standing between him and his readers. He's built that relationship directly, through a blog he's called "A Blog of Dubious Intent," through Twitch livestreams timed to book releases, and through spoiler sessions where he answers reader questions about a book minutes after it goes live. Longtime readers can quote what he's said in these sessions from memory, which tells you something about how closely his audience tracks his process.

That transparency isn't incidental to the business. Progression fantasy's readership lives on Royal Road, Kindle Unlimited, and Audible, platforms built around serial trust between a writer and the people following along chapter by chapter. Wight has said plainly that when he's quiet online, it means the work is going well: "the less you hear from me, the more productive I'm being. When I have free time, THAT'S when I check Facebook and screw around online." He's made watching the mechanics of how the books get made part of what readers get for following him, instead of hiding that process the way most authors do.

For a genre this dependent on digital, serial-native platforms, that openness functions as its own kind of craft skill. Wight learned to run the whole pipeline, from the blank page to the reader's inbox, not just how to write cultivation fiction, and he's been willing to show his work at every stage of it.

What You Can Steal

Wight's routine is a production system built by someone who treated writing as a job from the very first sale. Here's what actually transfers:

  • Set a production window, not a perfect draft. "The best six-month book I can write" beats an unhurried novel that never quite gets finished. A defined window forces decisions a wide-open one never will.
  • Hit your target, then reward yourself, not the reverse. Wight's earliest interviews describe a writer who earns his downtime with a word count first. That order matters more than the specific number.
  • Plan the minimum, then fix it in revision. Ask what you actually need to know before you can start the next scene. Everything else can wait until the first draft exists to revise.
  • Keep your notes wherever you'll actually use them. A messy text file you check constantly beats an elegant wiki you never open. Wight's "piles of notes" have outproduced tidier systems for over a decade.
  • If you're building an audience directly, let them see the process. Wight's livestreams and blog turned his production pipeline into part of the relationship with readers, not a secret kept from them.
