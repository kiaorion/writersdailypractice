What Is Will Wight's Writing Advice?

Kia Orion | July 19, 2026 | 5 min read

Will Wight built a career on treating writing as a job with a repeatable process, not a mystery to wait on. His advice to other writers tends to reflect that directly, aimed at the specific ways people talk themselves out of starting.

The Direct Answer
Start with the minimum you actually need to know, don't mistake endless planning for productive work, and judge your progress by how much you're writing, not how much you're talking about writing online.
Wight has been candid that quiet periods on his blog and social media usually mean the opposite of what readers might assume.

Don't Confuse Planning With Progress

Wight's most repeated advice to newer writers is to ask what the minimum requirement actually is before starting a scene, a chapter, or a book. "Don't get bogged down in details," he's warned, arguing that extensive pre-writing planning is often just a more comfortable way to avoid the harder work of drafting. "Anything that happens in the first draft can be fixed later" is the corollary: nothing you write today has to be right, it just has to exist.

Quiet Online Means Productive Offline

Wight has been unusually direct about the relationship between his visibility online and his actual output. "The less you hear from me, the more productive I'm being," he's said. "When I have free time, THAT'S when I check Facebook and screw around online." It's a useful inversion for any writer measuring their progress against someone else's social media presence: activity online and activity on the page tend to run in opposite directions.

That advice comes from someone who has published more than twenty novels since 2013 while livestreaming, blogging, and running a self-publishing business at the same time. He's arguing against mistaking online presence for the work itself, not against being present online at all. For the fuller picture of how this philosophy shows up in his actual schedule, see the full Will Wight writing routine.

What You Can Steal

  • Ask what the minimum requirement actually is. Most planning past that point is procrastination wearing a productive-looking outfit.
  • Don't judge your output by your online presence. A quiet week online might be your most productive week on the page.
  • Give yourself permission to write it wrong first. Revision exists to fix what drafting gets wrong.
