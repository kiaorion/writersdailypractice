What Is Will Wight's Writing Schedule?

Kia Orion | July 19, 2026 | 5 min read

Will Wight self-published his way into a full-time writing career in 2013, and the schedule he built to sustain it hasn't changed much since. He treats the writing target as the first obligation of the day, not the last, and the rest of the schedule builds around that one fixed point.

The Direct Answer
He hits a daily word count target before he allows himself any downtime, and has sustained that discipline long enough to average roughly two full novels a year for over a decade.
As far back as his 2013 debut, Wight described a schedule built around a simple order of operations: hit the target, then relax.

Write First, Then Reward Yourself

In one of his earliest interviews, given the same year House of Blades came out, Wight described a routine built around a fixed daily word count. He'd hit that number, then allow himself to unwind with a video game or a book. The order mattered. Downtime was the reward for finishing, not a break he took in the middle of the work.

That structure has held up remarkably well against the pressure of a growing catalogue. Between the Traveler's Gate trilogy, the six-book Elder Empire series, all thirteen Cradle novels, and the ongoing Last Horizon series, Wight has published more than twenty novels since 2013 while working outside a traditional publishing schedule altogether. There's no editor's deadline forcing the pace. The daily target does that work instead.

The Six Month Book

The six-month-book philosophy behind that schedule still bends when it has to. In 2022, running a Kickstarter, a new series launch, and the final Cradle book at the same time, he extended his usual window because, in his words, he "really wanted both of these books to work."

For the fuller picture of how that daily habit connects to his planning process and his public-facing writing life, see the full Will Wight writing routine.

What You Can Steal

  • Hit your target before you relax. Wight's earliest interviews describe downtime as something earned after the word count, not squeezed in before it.
  • Set a window, not a perfect draft. "The best six-month book" beats an unhurried one that never gets finished.
  • Let the schedule bend when the book needs it. Extending a deadline on purpose, once, is different from missing one by accident every time.
